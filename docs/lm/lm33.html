Learning Module Overview
  • Out of sample and net of costs, the eight rules' Sharpe ratios run from 0.63 (R4) to 1.14 (R2) a year over 1,851 daily returns. Each standard error by (20.9) is about 0.37, so R2's 95% interval, 0.42 to 1.86, holds all seven other estimates.
  • Negative skewness and raw kurtosis of 10 to 23 lift (20.9)'s standard error only from 0.363 to at most 0.371, if the data have the fourth moment it needs. The HAC standard error (33.1) at the book's lag of 7 moves it by −8% to +4%, while the volatility's standard error rises by half.
  • Lo's correction on 90 calendar months lowers R1's annual ratio from 1.06 to 0.92, but the correction's own robust standard error, 0.38 on a ratio of 1.31, leaves the need for it undecided.
  • PSR(0) is one minus a one-sided p-value: 0.999 for R2. Against zero R2 needs 523 sessions, 2.15 years of sessions. With V^SR​=1 and SR0​=0, and without the "1 +", (33.3) is (21.4) one-sided at power ½.
  • Ledoit and Wolf's test finds R2's lead of 0.16 over R1 consistent with chance: a p-value of 0.52 by HAC and by the circular block bootstrap. None of the seven pairs is rejected after Holm.
  • Deflated for eight trials, R2's benchmark is 0.52 a year and its DSR 0.955. The rules correlate 0.82 on average, and their effective number of trials is 2.07.
  • Harvey and Liu's haircut for eight independent tests cuts R2's ratio by 21%, to 0.90, and (4.3)'s Kelly fraction by the same 21% at a fixed volatility.

1Introduction

LM20 gave one Sharpe ratio a standard error, and LM21 asked how long a record must be to detect a given ratio. Volume V then fixed eight rules for five funds: R1 and R2 (LM27), R3 and R4 (LM28, LM29), R5 (LM30) and R6 to R8 (LM31).

Motivating CaseBetter, or lucky?

Eight rules for five Taiwan-listed funds were fixed on 24 October 2026, before any result was computed. Net of costs, they earned 1,851 daily returns from the close of 4 March 2019 to 8 October 2026, 7.60 calendar years. The constructed reference mix R2 has the highest Sharpe ratio, 1.14 a year. Equal weights, R1, earn 0.98, and the maximum-Sharpe rule R4 the lowest, 0.63.

Is R2 better, or lucky? Its standard error by (20.9) is 0.367, so its 95% interval runs from 0.42 to 1.86 and holds all seven other estimates. R2 beats R1 by 0.16 a year, and their daily returns correlate 0.70. Ledoit and Wolf's test of the gap gives a p-value of 0.52, by two methods alike (Exhibit 1).

R2 is also the best of eight. If eight independent rules all had a true Sharpe ratio of zero, the best of their estimates would still average about 0.52 a year over 1,851 daily returns.

This module gives each rule an error bar in Sections 2 to 4 and the record it needs in Section 5. Section 6 tests the rules against R1, Sections 7 and 8 correct the best for being picked, and Section 9 gives the code.

2The Checkpoint: Eight Rules with Error Bars

compute a Sharpe ratio's standard error and interval by (20.9), with raw kurtosis

This section is a checkpoint on Volume IV: it applies that volume's standard errors and tests to the eight rules. LM20's (20.9) gives the variance of a Sharpe ratio estimated from T iid returns:

TVar(SR)≈V^SR​,V^SR​:=1−SRSkew+41​(Kurt−1)SR2,SE(SR)=V^SR​/T​.​

Here SR=xˉ/s is per observation. The plug-in uses (19.3)'s sample skewness and raw kurtosis (divisor T), whose normal values are 0 and 3. LM20 derived it by the delta method for iid returns with a finite fourth moment, and it holds for large T, here 1,851 daily returns or TR23's 5,849. The normal case gives Lo's IID formula (9), (1+SR2/2)/T​ (Lo 2002).

The data may lack (20.9)'s fourth moment. LM20's Student-t fit to TR23 has 3.065 degrees of freedom, below 4, so its fourth moment is infinite. The rules' raw kurtosis of 10 to 23 has no standard error. (20.9)'s standard errors, and the tests built on them, may then be too small.

Example 1R2 by hand

R2's daily excess returns have mean xˉ=0.0564% and s=0.774%, skewness −0.112 and raw kurtosis 13.58. Find the standard error of its Sharpe ratio, a day and a year.

Solution

The daily ratio is 0.0564/0.774=0.0729. By (20.9),

V^SR​​=1+0.0729×0.112+41​×12.58×0.07292=1+0.0082+0.0167=1.0249.​
So the standard error is 1.0249/1,851​=0.0235 a day, and 0.0235×243.57​=0.367 a year. The kurtosis term is about twice the skewness term. The ratio times T​ is 0.0729×1,851​=3.14, the t-stat of the mean excess return with divisor T, by LM21's identity.

Knowledge Check 1

R4's two-sided 95% interval, −0.09 to 1.35, contains 0, yet its PSR(0) is 0.956. Why can both hold?

Answer

The interval is two-sided at 5%, with 2.5% in each tail. PSR is one-sided: its p-value, 0.044, is below 5% but above 2.5%. The two-sided 90% interval, 0.02 to 1.23, is the twin of a one-sided test at 5%, and it excludes 0.

3Dependent Days: the HAC Standard Error

derive a Sharpe ratio's HAC standard error by the delta method, and check its lag sensitivity

The rules' daily excess returns are nearly uncorrelated: ρ^​1​ by (19.5) runs from −0.054 (R1) to 0.002 (R4). Their squares are not. R1's squared deviations (xt​−xˉ)2 have ρ^​1​=0.246, and (20.9)'s kurtosis term rests on those squares.

Write the Sharpe ratio as h(xˉ,s2)=xˉ/s, a function of two means: xˉ, and s2, the mean of (xt​−xˉ)2. The delta method (20.8) needs the covariance of the two means. With dependent days and large T, that covariance is about the long-run covariance that LM23's (23.7) estimates, divided by T.

DefinitionThe HAC standard error of a Sharpe ratio

Let the per-date terms and the gradient of h be

vt​=(xt​−xˉ, (xt​−xˉ)2−s2)⊤,∇h=(1/s, −xˉ/(2s3))⊤.​
With (23.7)'s Bartlett estimate
Ψ^=Γ^0​+k=1∑ℓ​(1−ℓ+1k​)(Γ^k​+Γ^k⊤​),Γ^k​=T1​t=k+1∑T​vt​vt−k⊤​,​
the standard error is
SEHAC​(SR)=∇h⊤Ψ^∇h/T​.(33.1)
The lag is the book's ℓ=⌊4(T/100)2/9⌋ (LM23): 7 for 1,851 returns, 9 for TR23's 5,849 and 3 for 90 months. No small-sample factor is applied.

(33.1) is stated without proof as a large-T result. It needs stationary returns whose dependence fades (a mixing condition). It also needs a finite moment of order a little above 4 (Ledoit and Wolf 2008, p. 852, for the same delta method), which TR23 may lack (Section 2). It is Lo's (2002) generalized-method-of-moments standard error, his eqs. (14)–(15), with Newey and West's (1987) weights and the book's lag.

At ℓ=0 the estimate is Ψ^=Γ^0​, the mean of vt​vt⊤​. By (19.3) its diagonal entries are s2 and s4Kurt−s4, and its off-diagonal entry is s3Skew. So

∇h⊤Γ^0​∇h​=s2s2​−2⋅s1​⋅2s3xˉ​s3Skew+4s6xˉ2​s4(Kurt−1),​
which is V^SR​ of (20.9) term by term.

4Annualizing Autocorrelated Returns

annualize a Sharpe ratio of autocorrelated returns by Lo's correction, and judge whether the data need it

LM19's (19.9) annualizes the Sharpe ratio of a sum of m periods. With (19.7)'s ratio written out, it reads

SR(m)=1+2∑k=1m−1​(1−k/m)ρk​​m​SR​.

Lo (2002, eq. (20)) calls the factor that multiplies SR the scale factor. It equals m​ when every ρk​ is 0, and more generally when the weighted sum in the denominator is 0. He derives it for sums of returns with compounding ignored, which fits monthly log returns. LM19 estimated the denominator from (19.8)'s overlapping sums, while here it comes from ρ^​1​,…,ρ^​11​, whose noise (25.6) measures.

Example 5Ninety months

Annualize the rules' monthly Sharpe ratios by 12​ and by (19.9). Does the correction matter?

Solution

The 90 calendar months run from April 2019 to September 2026, 12 to 23 sessions each. The 19 returns of March 2019 and the 6 of October 2026 are excluded. A month's log excess return is the log change of net wealth between month-ends minus its sessions times ln(1+rf​/Aw​). Every log is defined, since 1+Rt​>0 on every date.

R1's monthly ratio is 0.305, so 12​ gives 1.06. Its ratio 1+2∑k​(1−k/12)ρ^​k​ is 1.309, so (19.9) gives 0.305×12/1.309​=0.92. Over the eight rules the scale factors run from 2.74 to 3.33 against 3.46.

Annual Sharpe ratio R1 R2 R4
By 12​ 1.06 1.05 0.62
By (19.9) 0.92 0.94 0.49

The correction is itself noisy. R1's ratio has a robust standard error of 0.38 by (25.6), and its excess over 1, 0.309, is 0.78 iid standard errors of 0.395. (19.9)'s ratio and (25.6) are large-T results, and 90 months are a small sample for them. So 90 months cannot tell whether the correction is needed, and the corrected ratio carries the noise of 11 autocorrelations.

The monthly ratios use log returns and Example 2 uses simple returns, and the two differ before any autocorrelation enters. On daily log excess returns the annual ratios run from 0.54 (R4) to 1.08 (R2), against 0.63 to 1.14.

Knowledge Check 3

Monthly returns follow an AR(1) with ρ1​=0.2, so that ρk​=0.2k (constructed). By how much does the 12​ rule overstate the annual Sharpe ratio?

Answer

By (19.9) with m=12 the scale factor is 2.879, against 12​=3.464. So 12​ overstates the annual ratio by a factor of 1.203, or 20%. Lo's Table 2 (his p. 41) prints 2.88.

5The Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio and the Minimum Track Record

derive the probabilistic Sharpe ratio and the minimum track record length, and relate them to power

A one-sided test of H0​:SR≤SR0​, for a fixed benchmark SR0​, divides the gap by (20.9)'s standard error. Bailey and López de Prado (2012) call one minus its p-value (21.2) the probabilistic Sharpe ratio:

PSR(SR0​)=Φ(V^SR​​(SR−SR0​)T−1​​).(33.2)

The kurtosis in V^SR​ is raw, and the approximation is (20.9)'s, with its iid returns, finite fourth moment and large T.

PSR is not the probability that the true ratio exceeds SR0​, which would need a prior. In LM24 a flat prior gives a posterior probability equal to the one-sided p-value to the printed digits, but as a statement about μ, not about the data. Shrinking a Sharpe ratio toward a prior uses LM24's tools and is not done here.

Setting PSR equal to 1−α and solving for T gives the minimum track record length, in observations:

MinTRL=1+V^SR​(SR−SR0​Φ−1(1−α)​)2,SR>SR0​.​(33.3)

The T−1 is the authors' (their §2.5 and eq. (11), authors' version), while (20.9) uses T. For R2 the z-value is 3.097 with T−1​ and 3.098 with T​, larger by 1,851/1,850​, that is by 0.027%.

LM21's (21.4) asked another question: how long a record a true ratio needs for a two-sided test at level α to reach a given power. With V^SR​=1 and SR0​=0, and without the "1 +", (33.3) is (21.4) one-sided at power ½ (Problem 7). At its MinTRL, a test of a true ratio equal to the estimate rejects only half the time.

導讀PSR 是單邊檢定

PSR 是單邊檢定（one-sided test）的 1 減 p值（p-value），虛無假設（null hypothesis）是真實夏普比率（Sharpe ratio）不超過基準（benchmark）SR0​。它不是「真實夏普比率大於基準的機率」，那需要事前分布（prior distribution），見 LM24。MinTRL 是讓 PSR 達到 95% 所需的觀察筆數。如果真實值剛好等於估計值（estimate），在這個長度下檢定只有一半的機會拒絕，也就是檢定力（power）只有 ½。LM21 的 (21.4) 用的是雙邊檢定（two-sided test），又要求 80% 的檢定力，需要的紀錄長得多：TR23 要 11.9 年的日資料，MinTRL 只要 4.20 年份的交易日。

6Comparing Two Sharpe Ratios

compare two Sharpe ratios by Ledoit and Wolf's test, and correct a family of such tests

R2 leads R1 by Δ^=+0.162 a year. LM21's (21.3) would test the lead as if the two samples were independent, but the two rules hold the same funds on the same days.

Ledoit and Wolf (2008) write the difference as Δ^=h(θ^), a function of four means, θ^=(xˉj​,xˉ1​,xj2​​,x12​​), the last two uncentred second moments. With Ψ^ by (23.7) on the per-date terms vt​, the book's ℓ=7 and the gradient at θ^,

h(θ)=θ3​−θ12​​θ1​​−θ4​−θ22​​θ2​​,∇h=((θ3​−θ12​)3/2θ3​​, −(θ4​−θ22​)3/2θ4​​,−2(θ3​−θ12​)3/2θ1​​, 2(θ4​−θ22​)3/2θ2​​)⊤,SE(Δ^)=∇h⊤Ψ^∇h/T​,vt​=(xj,t​−xˉj​, x1,t​−xˉ1​,xj,t2​−xj2​​, x1,t2​−x12​​)⊤,count=#{i:SE(Δ^boot,i​)∣Δ^boot,i​−Δ^∣​≥SE(Δ^)∣Δ^∣​},p-value=B+1count+1​.​(33.6)

The standard error SE(Δ^) gives a normal p-value and interval as (21.2) does, under (33.1)'s conditions. The count and the p-value are Ledoit and Wolf's bootstrap test (§3.2.2). It resamples blocks of b consecutive return pairs on LM22's circle, which keeps the two rules' joint dependence.

Each resample's standard error, stated without proof, is Götze and Künsch's (1996), via Ledoit and Wolf's eq. (8). Its terms vt​ and its ∇h use the resample's own means, and vˉblk​ is the terms' mean over one complete block:

Ψ^boot​=⌊T/b⌋b​complete blocks∑​vˉblk​vˉblk⊤​,SE(Δ^boot,i​)=∇h⊤Ψ^boot​∇h/T​.​

Unlike (22.4), the resamples come from the data, not from a null world, so each is centred at Δ^. The "+1" keeps the p-value above 0 (their Remark 3.2, eq. (9)). The interval Δ^±F^boot−1​(0.95)SE(Δ^), with the 95% quantile of the centred statistics, is a symmetric studentized interval, each statistic being divided by its own standard error.

Three settings are the book's. First, the data's standard error uses Bartlett weights at ℓ=7. Ledoit and Wolf use a kernel estimate with a factor T/(T−4) and, in the bootstrap, Andrews and Monahan's (1992) prewhitened quadratic-spectral kernel.

Second, b=22 is LM22's rule, the largest stationary estimate over the eight rules' squared demeaned returns (16.66 to 22.41), where Ledoit and Wolf calibrate b (their Algorithm 3.1). As in LM22 it sets the circular blocks too, though the circular estimates reach 25.65, so experiment 3 includes b=26.

Third, Ledoit and Wolf do not say how to cut a resample when b does not divide T. Here T=1,851=84×22+3, so a resample draws 85 blocks, keeps the first 1,851 rows and takes Ψ^boot​ from the 84 complete blocks.

7Deflating the Best of Eight

deflate the best of n Sharpe ratios, and estimate the effective number of correlated trials

R2 is the best of eight, picked after the results were known. Suppose n independent rules all have true ratio zero. By (20.9) each estimate is then about normal with standard deviation 1/T​, which PSR's convention writes 1/T−1​. So their maximum is about that standard deviation times Z(n)​, the largest of n iid standard normals. LM21's (21.9), with its m tests read as n trials, gives Pr(Z(n)​≤z)=Φ(z)n. Hence

EZ(n)​=∫0∞​(1−Φ(z)n)dz−∫−∞0​Φ(z)ndz,DSR=PSR(EZ(n)​/T−1​).​(33.4)

The deflated Sharpe ratio (Bailey and López de Prado 2014) is PSR at that benchmark. It assumes n independent trials, each with true ratio zero under H0​. The null scale 1/T−1​ is the book's, while their eq. (1) scales by the standard deviation of the trials' own estimates.

8Haircuts and Sizing

explain how a haircut corrects a Sharpe ratio for multiple testing, and its effect on sizing

Harvey and Liu (2015) correct a Sharpe ratio for multiple testing by adjusting the two-sided p-value (21.2) of its t-stat SRT​ for n tests. They convert the adjusted p-value into a Sharpe ratio:

adjusted p-value=1−(1−p-value)n,or  min(1,np-value),t-statadj​=Φ−1(1−adjusted p-value/2),HSR=t-statadj​/T​.​(33.7)

The first form is for n independent tests (their eq. (4), Šidák's form in LM21), the second Bonferroni's (21.6). The haircut is 1−HSR/SR.

Holm's (21.7) gives adjusted p-values too. In increasing order, the p-values are multiplied by n, then by n−1, and so on, kept at their running maximum and capped at 1. Harvey and Liu apply Holm with p-values from a factor model (section "Multiple Testing Framework"), so Holm on the eight rules' own p-values is the book's adaptation (Exhibit 7). Their false-discovery version (BHY) is not used: LM21 owns the false discovery rate.

Example 9R2's haircut

Solution

R2's t-stat is SRT​=3.137, with the two-sided p-value 0.00171. For eight independent tests the adjusted p-value is 1−(1−0.00171)8=0.0136. Knowledge Check 7

Holm's haircut of R4 is 0%, while Bonferroni's is 75%. Why?

Answer

R4 has the largest p-value, 0.083, which Holm multiplies by 1. The product exceeds every smaller p-value's adjusted value, so R4's adjusted p-value is its own and its HSR its whole ratio. Holm's adjusted p-values cannot fall below their running maximum, so R6, R7 and R8 share the adjusted p-value 0.051 and the HSR 0.71 a year (Exhibit 7). Bonferroni multiplies every p-value by 8.

9Sharpe Statistics in Python

compute these statistics in NumPy, and check them against SciPy and arch with every option passed
Python
import numpy as np
from arch.bootstrap import optimal_block_length
from scipy import stats

def moments(x):                       # divisor T throughout, (19.3)
    m, s = x.mean(), x.std(ddof=0)
    c = x - m
    return m, s, (c**3).mean() / s**3, (c**4).mean() / s**4

def lrv(V, lag):                      # (23.7), Bartlett, V centred
    S = V.T @ V / len(V)
    for k in range(1, lag + 1):
        G = V[k:].T @ V[:-k] / len(V)
        S = S + (1 - k / (lag + 1)) * (G + G.T)
    return S

def se_hac(x, lag):                   # (33.1); lag 0 gives (20.9)
    m, s, _, _ = moments(x)
    c = x - m
    g = np.array([1 / s, -m / (2 * s**3)])
    V = np.column_stack([c, c**2 - s**2])
    return np.sqrt(g @ lrv(V, lag) @ g / len(x))

# x: one rule's 1,851 daily excess returns
m, s, sk, ku = moments(x)
assert np.isclose(sk, stats.skew(x, bias=True))
assert np.isclose(ku, stats.kurtosis(x, fisher=False, bias=True))  # raw
lag = int(4 * (len(x) / 100) ** (2 / 9))                 # 7
obl = optimal_block_length((x - m) ** 2)  # columns stationary, circular
b = round(obl["stationary"].iloc[0])      # the book: largest over rules

rng = np.random.default_rng([1, 20261011])  # once per experiment
nb = -(-len(x) // b)                        # ceil(T / b) blocks
sr_boot = []
for i in range(9999):
    starts = rng.integers(0, len(x), size=nb)
    idx = ((starts[:, None] + np.arange(b)) % len(x)).ravel()[: len(x)]
    sr_boot.append(x[idx].mean() / x[idx].std())

scipy.stats.kurtosis returns the excess kurtosis unless fisher=False, and its default bias=True divides by T, as (19.3) does, so pass both. arch's optimal_block_length returns a stationary and a circular column, and LM22's rule reads the stationary one.
