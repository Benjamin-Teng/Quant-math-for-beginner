Learning Module Overview
  • TAIEX's up days in 292 blocks of 20 sessions average 10.96 with variance 4.72. A binomial with p^​=0.548 gives 10.96 and 4.95 by (15.2): a variance near np(1−p) is consistent with independent days, not proof of it.
  • Rare binomial trials tend to the Poisson, (15.4), whose mean and variance are both λ. Days with a move of 3% or more average 0.612 a month, but their variance is 1.846: the counts are overdispersed, and nine months had five or more against 0.123 expected.
  • A normal quantile is μ+σΦ−1(u), (15.6), and 1.96 is Φ−1(0.975). TAIEX's 1% quantile, −3.567%, lies 0.82 points below the normal's −2.749%, and its QQ plot bends away from the line in both tails.
  • A lognormal fitted to the 23 calendar-year growth factors 2003–2025 has median 1.1235 (the compound rate, +12.35% a year), mean 1.1516 and a 30.0% chance of a losing year; a model of independent days gives about 26.5%.
  • The Student-t has variance ν/(ν−2) for ν>2 and kurtosis 3+6/(ν−4) for ν>4, (15.10). Matching TAIEX's excess kurtosis 5.27 gives ν=5.14; maximum likelihood gives ν=3.065, where the kurtosis is infinite.
  • At or below −5% the normal expects 0.0817 days, the maximum-likelihood Student-t 19.6, and TAIEX had 15. At −10% the Student-t expects 2.57 days, which the 10% price limit almost excludes.
  • One day, 7 April 2025, lifted TAIEX's excess kurtosis from 4.25 to 4.94 and moved its 1% quantile by 0.005 points. Counts and quantiles need fewer moments and moved less; the chord from −5% to −6% rests on a few days and fell from 6.03 to 5.24.
  • Each question gets its own model and limiting assumption: the binomial for up days (independence), the lognormal for a year (independent years), the maximum-likelihood Student-t from −3% to −5% (symmetry), and plain counts beyond −7% (the price limit).

1Introduction

LM5 fitted a normal model to 0050's daily returns and found its tail far too thin. The model expected 0.058 days at or below −5% in twelve years, and 9 occurred. LM13 and LM14 built the language of random variables, distribution functions and moments. This module applies it to the distributions that finance uses most. Each has one to three parameters and answers some questions well and others badly; the data decide which.

Motivating CaseWhich model for which question?

Take TAIEX's 5,849 daily total returns from 3 January 2003 to 8 October 2026. A normal model with their mean, 0.0625%, and standard deviation, 1.2084%, expects 0.08 days at or below −5%. TAIEX had 15 (LM13's count). A Student-t fitted by maximum likelihood, with ν=3.065 degrees of freedom, expects 19.6 (Exhibit 1).

Now count the days with a move of 3% or more, either way, month by month. They average 0.612 a month, but their variance is 1.846, three times the mean. Three months had eight: September and October 2008, and March 2020.

Which model should answer which question? And what does the crowding of large moves into a few months say about independence?

Sections 2 and 3 count events with the binomial and the Poisson. Sections 4 to 6 fit the normal, the lognormal and the Student-t. Section 7 compares tail measures and chooses a model per question, and Section 8 collects the Python calls.

2Counting Successes: Bernoulli and Binomial

calculate binomial probabilities, means and variances, and compare the binomial with up days in blocks of sessions

TR23's first 20 returns, 3 January to 7 February 2003, contain 12 up days. Is 12 many? The answer needs a model for the count.

A Bernoulli trial has two outcomes: a success with probability p and a failure with probability 1−p. Its indicator X=1{success} takes the values 1 and 0, so (14.1) gives E[X]=p. Because X2=X, also E[X2]=p, and (14.4) gives Var(X)=p−p2=p(1−p).

DefinitionBinomial distribution

Let n independent trials ((13.13) of LM13) each succeed with the same probability p, 0<p<1. The number of successes X has the binomial distribution Bin(n,p):

Pr(X=j)=(jn​)pj(1−p)n−j,j=0,1,…,n.​(15.1)

By (13.7), one given sequence with j successes has probability pj(1−p)n−j. There are (jn​) such sequences, one for each choice of the positions of the successes, and they are disjoint, so their probabilities add. By the binomial theorem of LM1's Deep Dive on e, the probabilities (15.1) sum to (p+1−p)n=1.

For the moments, use j(jn​)=n(j−1n−1​) and j(j−1)(jn​)=n(n−1)(j−2n−2​), and then the binomial theorem again:

E[X]=npj=1∑n​(j−1n−1​)pj−1(1−p)n−j=np,E[X(X−1)]=n(n−1)p2.​

Since Var(X)=E[X(X−1)]+E[X]−(E[X])2 by (14.4),

E[X]Var(X)​=np,=n(n−1)p2+np−n2p2=np(1−p).​(15.2)

LM16 reaches this variance in one line, adding the variances of independent indicators. LM17 shows why a sum of many independent trials is close to normal.

Example 1Up days in a block

TR23's first 5,840 returns form 292 blocks of 20 sessions; the last 9 returns, 24 September – 8 October 2026, are excluded. The blocks contain 3,200 up days (no return is exactly 0), so their up-day frequency is p^​=3,200/5,840=0.548. Model each block's count as Bin(20,0.548). Compute its mean and variance and the expected numbers of blocks with at most 5 and at least 16 up days.

Solution

By (15.2), the mean is 20×0.548=10.96 and the variance 20×0.548×0.452=4.95. The first block's 12 up days lie within one standard deviation, 2.23, of the mean 10.96. The 292 blocks have mean 10.96, by construction, and variance 4.72 (divisor 292).

By (15.1), Pr(X≤5)=0.00677, or 292×0.00677=1.98 blocks, against 2 observed. Similarly Pr(X≥16)=0.01807, or 5.28 blocks, against 3. Exhibit 2 compares every count.

PitfallIndependent trials with one p

A variance near np(1−p) is consistent with independent days; it does not prove independence. In LM13's 5,848 pairs of adjacent days, the second day was down with frequency 0.4521, and with 0.4639 after a down day. Let X1​, X2​ be a pair's down-day indicators, both taken at frequency 0.4521 (the first day's is 0.4519). Their covariance E[X1​X2​]−E[X1​]E[X2​] (LM16) is 0.4521×(0.4639−0.4521) and each variance 0.4521×(1−0.4521), so their correlation is ρ1​=(0.4639−0.4521)/(1−0.4521)=0.0215 (0.0214 from the unrounded frequencies), as for up-day indicators.

If only adjacent days are correlated, LM16's variance of a sum carries ρ1​ into a block of 20:

20×0.548×0.452×(1+2⋅2019​⋅0.0215)=5.16.​
The blocks give 4.72, below both 5.16 and 4.95: 292 blocks cannot see a dependence this small either way (LM21 tests such gaps).

Knowledge Check 1

For n=20 trials, which p maximizes the binomial variance? Compare the blocks' 4.95 with that maximum.

Answer

The variance np(1−p) is a downward parabola in p with derivative n(1−2p), zero at p=21​. Its maximum is n/4=5. The blocks' p^​=0.548 lies near 21​, so 4.95 lies near 5: there the variance hardly depends on p.

3Rare Events: the Poisson Distribution

derive the Poisson distribution as the limit of rare binomial trials, and recognize overdispersion in counts of large moves

TR23 has 175 days with ∣R∣≥3% among 5,849 (LM14); set p to the frequency 175/5,849=0.02992 a session. A month has about 20 sessions, so its count of large moves looks like a binomial with many trials and a small p. What matters is the mean λ=np. Fix λ and let n grow. At j=0, (1.6) with −λ in place of its variable gives

Pr(X=0)=(1−nλ​)n→e−λ.
DefinitionPoisson distribution

A count X has the Poisson distribution Poisson(λ), with λ>0, when

Pr(X=j)=j!e−λλj​,j=0,1,2,…(15.3)

TheoremPoisson limit of the binomial

Fix λ>0 and a count j≥0. Then, as n→∞,

(jn​)(nλ​)j(1−nλ​)n−j→j!e−λλj​.(15.4)

Deep Dive 1 proves (15.4) for every j. By (3.2), the probabilities (15.3) sum to e−λeλ=1. The mean comes from the same series, after the term j=0 is dropped and the index shifted:

E[X]=j=1∑∞​jj!e−λλj​=λj=1∑∞​(j−1)!e−λλj−1​=λ.

The same shift gives E[X(X−1)]=λ2 (Problem 4), so

E[X]=Var(X)=λ.(15.5)

A Poisson count has a variance equal to its mean, the property that monthly counts of large moves fail in Example 3.

Deep DiveDeep Dive 1: why the binomial tends to the Poissonoptional · click to expand

Fix j and take n>λ. Split the binomial probability into four factors:

​(jn​)(nλ​)j(1−nλ​)n−j=j!λj​⋅njn(n−1)⋯(n−j+1)​⋅(1−nλ​)n⋅(1−nλ​)−j.​
The second factor is a product of j terms (n−l)/n, l<j, each tending to 1, as in LM1's Deep Dive on e. The third tends to e−λ by (1.6). The fourth is a fixed power of a term that tends to 1. So the product tends to e−λλj/j!. ■

Example 2From a rare binomial to the Poisson

A 20-session month in which each session, independently, has a large move with probability 0.02992 has a count X∼Bin(20,0.02992), with mean λ=0.5984. Compare its pmf with that of Poisson(0.5984) at j=0, 1 and 2. Then take the case's monthly mean, 0.612 (λ^ of Example 3), and compute (1−0.612/n)n for n=20, 250 and 10,000, against e−0.612.

Solution

By (15.1) and (15.3):

j Bin(20,0.02992) Poisson(0.5984)
0 0.5447 0.5497
1 0.3360 0.3289
2 0.09845 0.09842

The two pmfs differ by at most 0.0071 here. The limit at j=0 is slow: (1−0.612/n)n is 0.53711, 0.54186 and 0.54226 for n=20, 250 and 10,000, against e−0.612=0.54227. The gap, 0.0052 at n=20 and 0.0004 at n=250, is close to e−λλ2/(2n) with λ=0.612 (0.0051 and 0.0004). The reason is that, by (3.3),

nln(1−nλ​)=−λ−2nλ2​−…,
and the error of the approximation is of order 1/n2.

Example 3Do large moves arrive like a Poisson count?

TR23's 286 calendar months, January 2003 (18 sessions) to October 2026 (6 sessions), contain the 175 days with ∣R∣≥3%. Fit a Poisson by the months' mean count, and compare expected and observed months.

Solution

The fitted mean is λ^=175/286=0.612. By (15.3), 155.1 of the 286 months should have no large move, and 210 had none. One large move: 94.9 expected, 38 observed. Five or more: 0.123 against 9. Eight or more: 8.1×10−5 against 3 (Exhibit 3).

The counts have variance 1.846 (divisor 286), 3.02 times their mean, where (15.5) requires 1: they are overdispersed. Months of unequal length (6 to 24 sessions) do not explain it. With one p for every session, the months' binomial variances average 0.594, and their means have variance 0.0055. By LM16's law of total variance, the count's variance is their sum, only 0.60. Each limit regime alone has a ratio near 3: 2.95 before June 2015, 3.01 after.

Calm and turbulent months come in runs because volatility changes over time; LM26 models it. LM39 uses Poisson counts for price jumps. Grinstead and Snell (1997, §5.1, Table 5.1) tabulate the binomial against its Poisson limit.

PitfallRare is not independent

A Poisson count needs rare events that arrive independently, at a constant rate. Large moves are rare, but they cluster. The fitted Poisson matches the average month and misses the bad ones: 9 months with five or more large moves against 0.123 expected. Check a count's variance before modelling it as a Poisson.

導讀二項分布與卜瓦松分布：計數的模型

一個交易日漲或不漲，可以用柏努利分布（Bernoulli distribution）描述：成功機率 p，平均數（mean）p，變異數（variance）p(1−p)。n 次獨立（independent）而成功機率相同的試驗，成功次數服從二項分布（binomial distribution），平均數 np、變異數 np(1−p)。加權股價報酬指數每 20 個交易日一組，上漲天數的變異數是 4.72，與二項分布的 4.95 相近，但這只表示資料與獨立相容，並不是證明。當 p 很小、n 很大而 np=λ 固定時，二項分布趨近卜瓦松分布（Poisson distribution），它的平均數與變異數都等於 λ。這個指數每月單日漲跌 3% 以上的天數平均 0.612 天，變異數卻是 1.846，約為平均數的三倍，這叫過度離散（overdispersion）：大波動會群聚，不像獨立同分布（iid）的稀有事件（rare event）。

Knowledge Check 2

Why not model the up days of a 20-session block with a Poisson?

Answer

The Poisson is the limit of a binomial with a small p, and up days have p^​=0.548. A Poisson's variance equals its mean, while a binomial's is 1−p times its mean: 4.95 against 10.96 here.

4The Normal Distribution: Quantiles and QQ Plots

calculate normal quantiles with Φ−1, and read a QQ plot of returns against a fitted normal

LM5 computed normal probabilities, Pr(R≤−x)=Φ((−x−μ)/σ). Risk questions often run the other way: which loss is reached on 1% of days? That loss is a quantile. LM14's quantile function FX−1​(u) is the smallest value at which FX​ reaches u. Because Φ is continuous and strictly increasing from 0 to 1, exactly one z solves Φ(z)=u for each u in (0,1); that z is Φ−1(u).

PropositionNormal quantiles

Let R∼N(μ,σ2) with σ>0, and let 0<u<1. Then

FR−1​(u)=μ+σΦ−1(u),Φ−1(1−u)=−Φ−1(u).​(15.6)

Proof. By (5.5), Pr(R≤μ+σΦ−1(u))=Φ(Φ−1(u))=u, and FR​ is strictly increasing. The symmetry Φ(−z)=1−Φ(z) of LM5 gives Φ(−Φ−1(u))=1−u. ■

LM4 used 1.96, without proof, as the value that a standard normal variable exceeds with probability 2.5%. By (15.6) it is Φ−1(0.975)=−Φ−1(0.025)=1.9600. With its sign reversed, the 1% quantile of a daily return is the one-day value at risk at 99% (LM32).

Example 4Normal quantiles against TAIEX's

SciPy's norm.ppf gives Φ−1 at 0.95, 0.975, 0.99 and 0.999 as 1.6449, 1.9600, 2.3263 and 3.0902. With Rˉ=0.0625% and s=1.2084%, compute the normal's 5%, 2.5%, 1% and 0.1% quantiles. Compare them with TAIEX's empirical quantiles F^−1(u)=R(⌈uT⌉)​ (LM14).

Solution

By (15.6) and symmetry, the normal's 5% quantile is 0.0625%−1.2084%×1.6449=−1.925%; the other levels follow in the same way (three decimals):

Level u Rank ⌈uT⌉ Normal (%) TAIEX (%)
5% 293 −1.925 −1.862
2.5% 147 −2.306 −2.648
1% 59 −2.749 −3.567
0.1% 6 −3.672 −6.313

At 5% the normal is slightly too deep. From 2.5% on it is too shallow, and the gap widens to 0.82 points at 1% and 2.64 points at 0.1%. TAIEX's 0.1% quantile is its sixth-worst day, 11 October 2018.

DefinitionQQ plot

Sort the T returns, R(1)​≤⋯≤R(T)​. The QQ plot (quantile–quantile plot) of the returns against a fitted normal places rank j at the point

(Φ−1(Tj−21​​), sR(j)​−Rˉ​),j=1,…,T.​
The coordinates are the normal quantile and the standardized return; normal returns lie near the 45° line.

Example 5Reading the QQ plot

Locate the worst day, the best day and rank 59 in Exhibit 4, and explain the plot's S shape.

Solution

The worst day, 7 April 2025, sits at (−3.758,−8.079). The lowest of 5,849 normal draws lies about 3.7 standard deviations below the mean; TAIEX's lies 8.08 below. The best day, 10 April 2025, sits at (3.758,7.604).

Rank 59 sits at (−2.326,−3.004), 0.68 standard deviations below the line: Example 4's 1% gap of 0.82 points, divided by s=1.2084%. The plot has an S shape, below the line on the left and above it on the right. That shape means more mass in both tails than the normal has: heavy tails.

PitfallWhich level does a QQ point stand for?

Rank j sits at the level (j−21​)/T, the middle of the step of F^ from (j−1)/T to j/T; at j/T the best day would sit at Φ−1(1)=+∞. A quantile quoted at a stated level keeps LM14's rule R(⌈uT⌉)​: the QQ convention is for drawing, not for quoting.

導讀常態分位數與分位數-分位數點圖

機率是給定門檻求面積，分位數（quantile）反過來，是給定機率求門檻。常態分布（normal distribution）的分位數函數（quantile function）是 μ+σΦ−1(u)，LM4 用到的 1.96 就是 Φ−1(0.975)。在 1% 的水準，常態模型給 −2.749%，加權股價報酬指數經驗分布（empirical distribution）的 1% 分位數卻是 −3.567%，相差 0.82 個百分點，而且越往尾端差得越多。分位數-分位數點圖（QQ plot）把排序後的標準化報酬對常態分位數作圖：資料若接近常態，點會落在 45 度線附近；這個指數的點左端低於直線、右端高於直線，呈 S 形，表示兩側尾巴都比常態厚，也就是厚尾（heavy tail）。

Knowledge Check 3

Which standard deviation σ would make a normal with mean 0.0625% expect 15 days at or below −5% in 5,849? How many days at or below −2% would it then expect?

Answer

The model needs 5,849Φ((−5%−0.0625%)/σ)=15, so (−5.0625%)/σ=Φ−1(15/5,849)=−2.7988 and σ=1.809%. That normal expects 5,849Φ(−2.0625/1.809)=743 days at or below −2%, against 252. A wider bell moves the whole curve; the data need a different shape.

5The Lognormal Distribution

derive the density, median, mean and variance of a lognormal growth factor, and fit one to annual growth factors

A year's growth factor G is positive, a product of many daily factors, so its logarithm X=lnG is a sum. LM17 explains why a product of many independent factors is close to lognormal; LM38 builds lognormal prices.

DefinitionLognormal distribution

Let X∼N(μX​,σX2​) with σX​>0. Then G=eX has the lognormal distribution with parameters μX​ and σX​.

PropositionDensity, quantiles and moments of a lognormal

Let G=eX with X∼N(μX​,σX2​) and σX​>0. Then, for y>0 and 0<u<1,

fG​(y)=σX​y1​φ(σX​lny−μX​​),FG−1​(u)=eμX​+σX​Φ−1(u),E[G]=eμX​+σX2​/2,Var(G)=(eσX2​−1)e2μX​+σX2​.​(15.7)
In particular the median is eμX​, below the mean.

Proof. The exponential is increasing, so FG​(y)=Pr(X≤lny)=Φ((lny−μX​)/σX​) for y>0. The chain rule (2.5) and Φ′=φ (LM5) give the density. The same monotonicity and (15.6) give the quantiles; the median, at u=21​, is eμX​. The mean is (5.12). For the variance, 2X∼N(2μX​,4σX2​), so (5.12) gives E[G2]=e2μX​+2σX2​; subtract (E[G])2=e2μX​+σX2​, by (14.4). ■

Example 6A lognormal for annual growth

TR23 has 23 calendar-year growth factors G, 2003–2025, with 2003 measured from its first close (2 January 2003). Their logarithms have mean μX​=0.11646 and standard deviation σX​=0.22207 (divisor 23). LM14 found an arithmetic mean return of 14.97% and a compound rate of 12.35%. Fit a lognormal with these parameters. Compute its median, mean and standard deviation, the probability of a losing year, and the 5% and 95% quantiles.

Solution

By (15.7), the median is e0.11646=1.1235, or +12.35%: the compound rate, because eμX​ is the geometric mean of the factors. The mean is e0.11646+0.222072/2=1.1516, +15.16% against the sample's 14.97%. The standard deviation is 0.2589.

6The Student-t Distribution

state the Student-t density, variance and kurtosis with their conditions, and fit a Student-t by matching kurtosis and by maximum likelihood

LM5 wrote the Student-t density with ν=3 "up to a constant" and quoted its kurtosis 3+6/(ν−4) without proof. This section supplies the constant, proves the moments and fits the distribution to TAIEX. LM14's Chebyshev and Cantelli bounds cap a tail probability but predict no count; a count needs a model with a shape.

The gamma function

DefinitionGamma function

For a>0,

Γ(a):=∫0∞​ya−1e−ydy,Γ(a+1)=aΓ(a),Γ(21​)=π​.​(15.8)

The integral converges for a>0: near 0 by LM5's power test, and at infinity because an exponential beats any power. Integration by parts (5.6), differentiating ya and integrating e−y, gives Γ(a+1)=[−yae−y]0∞​+aΓ(a)=aΓ(a). With Γ(1)=1, the gamma function extends the factorial: Γ(2)=1, Γ(3)=2, Γ(4)=6. For Γ(21​), substitute y=z2/2 by (5.4) and use (5.7):

Γ(21​)=∫0∞​2​e−z2/2dz=2​⋅22π​​=π​.

The density and its moments

DefinitionStudent-t distribution

A random variable Y has the standard Student-t distribution with ν>0 degrees of freedom, Y∼tν​, when

fY​(y)=νπ​Γ(2ν​)Γ(2ν+1​)​(1+νy2​)−2ν+1​(15.9)
for every real y.

The constant makes the total area 1. It follows from the beta integral and is stated here without proof (SciPy's t reference; Blitzstein and Hwang 2019, §10.4, treat the distribution). The numbers script checks the area numerically. The density is symmetric about 0, and its tails fall like ∣y∣−(ν+1). By LM5's power test, the moments of order below ν exist and the others do not, so the mean, 0, exists for ν>1.

Two limits frame the family. At ν=1 the density is 1/(π(1+y2)), the Cauchy law of LM5's tails exhibit, with no mean. As ν→∞, (1+y2/ν)ν→ey2 by (1.6), so the shape tends to e−y2/2 and tν​ tends to N(0,1); Example 7 checks the constant numerically.

Var(Y)=ν−2ν​  (ν>2),Kurt(Y)=3+ν−46​  (ν>4).​(15.10)

Chords of a power tail

Counts and quantiles need no moments. Recall LM5's power tail: a probability that falls like x−α is a line of slope −α on log–log axes.

PropositionTail index from two levels

Let 0<x1​<x2​, and suppose that Pr(R≤−x) is a constant multiple of x−α for x1​≤x≤x2​. Then

α=ln(x2​/x1​)ln[Pr(R≤−x1​)/Pr(R≤−x2​)]​.(15.13)
For any other law, the right side, the chord between the two levels, is a reading of the tail, not a parameter.

Proof. The constant cancels in the ratio, which equals (x2​/x1​)α; take logarithms. ■

  5. By (15.6), the 1% quantile is 0.0625%−1.2084%×2.3263=−2.749% and the 99% quantile +2.874%. The normal misses the lower tail more: by 0.82 points, against 0.25 in the upper tail. Large falls are larger than large rises: TR23's skewness is −0.34 (LM14).

  12. TAIEX has 67 days at or above +3% and 13 at or above +5%. The normal expects 44.0 and 0.128. The maximum-likelihood Student-t expects 94.2 and 22.0, so it overstates the upper tail at both levels. In the lower tail it understates at −3% (79.0 against 108) and overstates at −5% (19.6 against 15).

    A symmetric model mirrors its tails about its centre. Centred at +0.0997%, the Student-t even expects more large rises than large falls, while TAIEX has 108 large falls and 67 large rises.

Glossary

Term 中文 Meaning
Bernoulli distribution 柏努利分布 Law of one trial: success with probability p; mean p, variance p(1−p).
Binomial distribution 二項分布 Number of successes in n independent trials with one p, (15.1); mean np, variance np(1−p).
Poisson distribution 卜瓦松分布 Law of a count with Pr(X=j)=e−λλj/j!, (15.3); mean and variance λ.
Overdispersion 過度離散 A count's variance above its mean: a sign that a Poisson does not fit.
Independent and identically distributed (iid) 獨立同分布 Independent random variables that share one distribution.
Quantile function 分位數函數 FX−1​(u), the smallest value at which FX​ reaches u; for a normal, μ+σΦ−1(u), (15.6).

Empirical distribution 經驗分布 Mass 1/T on each observation; its quantiles are order statistics.
Lognormal distribution 對數常態分布 Law of G=eX for a normal X, (15.7); median eμX​, mean eμX​+σX2​/2.
Gamma function 伽瑪函數 Γ(a)=∫0∞​ya−1e−ydy, (15.8), with Γ(a+1)=aΓ(a).
