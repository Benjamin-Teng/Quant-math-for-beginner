Learning Module Overview
  • Growth multiplies and logarithms add. A logarithm in any base is the natural log rescaled: 0050's growth from 2014 to 2026 was lnG=2.3048, which is 3.325 doublings or 1.001 tenfold increases.
  • On a log axis equal distances are equal percentage moves, so constant growth plots as a straight line. On a linear axis 0050's January–April 2025 drawdown looks five times larger than its 2015 decline; in log points it was 0.32 against 0.24.
  • A stated rate r(n) compounded n times a year has an effective annual rate of (1+r(n)/n)n−1. For a fixed quote r(n)=r it rises with n toward er−1. The number e=2.71828… is the value of 1 unit after a year at 100% compounded continuously.
  • Frequency matters more as rates rise. At Bank of Taiwan's 1.725% rate for one-year time savings deposits, monthly compounding adds 1.4 bp; at the 15% legal cap on credit-card revolving interest, daily compounding would add 118 bp, and Taiwan's rules forbid it.

  • Annualizing is extrapolation. A 90-day return of 10.33% annualizes to anything from 39.9% to 50.1% depending on the convention, and Taiwan's market held about 244 sessions a year in 2015–2025, not 252.
  • Log returns add across time; simple returns add across positions. Averaging the log returns of 0050 and 00631L understates a 50/50 portfolio's twelve-year return by 615 percentage points.
  • A drawdown DD needs a gain of DD/(1−DD) to recover, but in log points the two are equal. After its January–April 2025 drawdown 0050 needed +37.91% and regained its peak in 99 days.

1Introduction

Every return in finance, from a deposit rate to a fund's lifetime performance, describes one quantity: the growth factor, ending value divided by starting value. Growth compounds, so it multiplies, and the mathematics of repeated multiplication is the exponential function and its inverse, the logarithm. This module builds both from first principles, derives the number e from compound interest, and turns them into conversions that practitioners perform every day. The same tools recur in every later module, from the Taylor approximations of LM3 to the lognormal prices of LM29.

Motivating CaseOne investment, three numbers

Suppose you bought Yuanta Taiwan 50 (0050) at the close of 31 October 2014 and reinvested every cash dividend until 8 October 2026. NT$100,000 became NT$1,002,247.

Three reports describe the holding. A fact sheet says the fund returned +902%. A performance review says it earned 21.3% a year. A backtest log says it grew at 19.3% a year.

All three are correct, and they describe the same money. Yet 21.3% and 19.3% cannot both be "the" annual return, and neither equals 902% divided by twelve years. Which number belongs in a client report, which belongs in a model, and how does each convert into the others? Section 4 resolves the case; Sections 2 and 3 build the tools.

Section 2 develops exponents, logarithms and log-scale charts. Section 3 turns periodic interest into effective annual rates, takes the limit of continuous compounding and arrives at e. Section 4 defines the three return measures, converts among them and resolves the case.

Section 5 shows how returns add across time and across positions. Section 6 solves growth equations for time, required return and drawdown recovery, using 0050's drawdowns of 2024 and 2025. Section 7 assembles the calculations in Python.

2Exponents and Logarithms

apply the laws of exponents and logarithms, including the change-of-base rule, to growth problems, and interpret a logarithmic chart axis

Growth multiplies

Wealth grows by factors. Returns R1​ in the first period and R2​ in the second turn W0​ into W0​(1+R1​)(1+R2​). With the same return R in each of T periods,

WT​=W0​(1+R)T.(1.1)

The count T sits in the exponent, so wealth is an exponential function of time. Doubling the number of periods squares the growth factor instead of doubling it, which is why long-horizon numbers defy intuition.

Laws of exponents

For a base β>0 and real exponents x and z,

βxβz=βx+z,βzβx​=βx−z,(βx)z=βxz,β0=1,β−x=βx1​.

For whole-number exponents these are counting rules: β2β3 contains 2+3 factors of β. Defining β1/k as the positive k-th root, and fractional powers as powers of such roots, extends them to rational exponents. Continuity extends them to all real exponents (stated without proof; see Stewart, Clegg and Watson, and LM2 for continuity). Exhibit 2 gives each law a financial reading.

Logarithms

DefinitionLogarithm

For a base β>0 with β=1 and a number x>0, the logarithm logβ​x is the exponent to which β must be raised to give x:

logβ​x=z⟺βz=x.
The natural logarithm lnx:=loge​x uses the base e=2.71828…, a constant whose origin in compound interest is the subject of Section 3.

Because logβ​ undoes βx, every law of exponents becomes a law of logarithms. For x,z>0, write x=βlogβ​x and z=βlogβ​z. The product law gives xz=βlogβ​x+logβ​z, which by the definition means

logβ​(xz)=logβ​x+logβ​z.(1.2)

The same steps give logβ​(x/z)=logβ​x−logβ​z and logβ​1=0. From xα=(βlogβ​x)α=βαlogβ​x follows the power rule logβ​(xα)=αlogβ​x for every real α. A logarithm turns multiplication into addition and powers into multiples. That is why log returns add over time (Section 5) and why growth equations are solved with logs (Section 6).

Change of base

Any logarithm is the natural logarithm rescaled. Since x=βlogβ​x, taking natural logs of both sides and applying the power rule gives lnx=logβ​x⋅lnβ, so

logβ​x=lnβlnx​.(1.3)

The base is a unit of measurement, like kilometers and miles. For 0050, lnG=ln10.0225=2.3048. In base 2 that is 2.3048/ln2=3.325 doublings; in base 10 it is 2.3048/ln10=1.001 tenfold increases. The fund grew almost exactly tenfold, which is why its log growth is close to ln10=2.3026.

Example 1Splitting 0050's growth into price and dividends

0050 closed at NT$65.30 on 31 October 2014 and at NT$114.95 on 8 October 2026, after a 4-for-1 split on 18 June 2025. It paid 22 cash dividends in between, and its total-return growth factor was G=10.0225. Split the log growth into a price part and a dividend part, and express each in doublings.

Solution

One 2014 share became four 2026 shares, so the price-only growth factor is GP​=4×114.95/65.30=459.80/65.30=7.0413, a price return of +604.13%.

For each daily return, restate the previous close per current share: P~t−1​=Pt−1​/4 on the split day and P~t−1​=Pt−1​ on every other day. The total return then factors exactly as

1+Rt​=P~t−1​Pt​+Dt​​=P~t−1​Pt​​(1+Pt​Dt​​),
where Dt​ is the cash dividend on an ex-date and zero otherwise.

The price factors Pt​/P~t−1​ multiply to GP​; without the restatement they would multiply to GP​/4=1.7603. Applying the product rule (1.2) to the product over all days,

lnG=lnGP​+ex-dates∑​ln(1+Pt​Dt​​)=1.9518+0.3530=2.3048.
Dividends supplied 15.3% of the log growth. In doublings, dividing each term by ln2: price 2.816 plus dividends 0.509 equals 3.325.

In simple returns the decomposition is multiplicative. Reinvested dividends multiplied ending wealth by e0.353030=1.423374, a dividend effect of +42.34%. The two factors compound to the total, 7.041348×1.423374=10.0225, or +902.25%. The price return of +604.13% and the dividend effect do not add; logs make the split exact and additive.

PitfallLog of what, in which base

Take logs of growth factors or prices, never of returns: ln(1+R) exists for every R>−100%, while lnR fails for every loss. Check the base too: in Excel, LOG(x) is base 10 unless a base is given and LN(x) is natural. In Python, math.log and np.log are natural and np.log10 is base 10. Mixing them scales every result by ln10=2.3026, an error that changes no signs and is easy to miss.

Log-scale charts

A logarithmic axis places each value W at a height that rises in step with lnW. Equal changes in lnW are equal distances on the page, with two consequences.

By the quotient rule, lnW2​−lnW1​=ln(W2​/W1​), so equal vertical distances are equal ratios. The step from 1 to 2 is as tall as the step from 4 to 8. Constant growth Wt​=W0​egt, with t in years, plots as the straight line lnWt​=lnW0​+gt, by the product rule and lnegt=gt. Its slope is the log growth rate g.

Exhibit 3 plots 0050's total-return index on both axes, with gridlines at successive doublings and the constant-rate path that ends at the same value.

On the linear axis the first six years look flat and the end looks explosive. Even the constant-rate path, which by construction grows at the same rate every year, appears to accelerate. On the log axis that path is a straight line, so only genuine changes in the growth rate bend the index away from it.

The doublings put numbers on the picture. The index first closed above 2 on 11 November 2020, above 4 on 4 July 2024 and above 8 on 27 April 2026. The three doublings took 6.0, 3.6 and 1.8 years.

Declines are distorted as well. The January–April 2025 decline cost 1.18 units of the index and the April–August 2015 decline 0.23 units, a ratio of five. In percentage terms they were −27.5% and −21.0%, or 0.32 and 0.24 log points.

導讀對數座標：看倍數，不看差額

對數把乘法變成加法，把「倍數」變成「距離」。在對數座標上，從 1 漲到 2 和從 4 漲到 8 的高度一樣，因為都是翻一倍；固定成長率的路徑是一條直線，斜率就是對數成長率 g。底數只是單位：ln 是自然單位，log2​ 數「翻了幾番」（翻倍幾次），log10​ 數「成長了幾個十倍」，彼此只差一個常數倍。0050 在 2025 年 1 至 4 月的回撤，在線性圖上看起來是 2015 年 4 至 8 月那次的五倍，換成對數其實只是 0.32 對 0.24。看長期走勢、比較不同年代的漲跌，請用對數座標。

3Compounding Frequency and the Number e

calculate effective annual rates from stated rates at any compounding frequency, and derive the continuous-compounding limit (1+r/n)n→er and the number e

Simple and compound interest

Under simple interest, interest accrues on the principal only, so wealth grows linearly: Wτ​=W0​(1+iτ) after τ years at an interest rate i per year. Under compound interest credited yearly, interest joins the balance and earns interest itself, which gives the exponential growth of (1.1): Wτ​=W0​(1+i)τ. For a positive rate the two agree at τ=0 and τ=1; simple interest is higher within the first year, compound interest after it. Both appear in Taiwan retail finance, as the examples below show.

Periodic, stated and effective rates

Banks quote rates per year but credit interest more often. A stated annual rate r(n) (名目年利率) credited n times a year pays the periodic rate i:=r(n)/n each period; yearly crediting, n=1, gives the annual rate i above. After one year a unit has grown to (1+i)n.

DefinitionEffective annual rate

The effective annual rate (EAR, 有效年利率) of a stated rate r(n) compounded n times a year is the annual simple return it delivers:

R=(1+nr(n)​)n−1=(1+i)n−1.(1.4)
Conversely, the stated rate with frequency n that delivers a given EAR R is r(n)=n[(1+R)1/n−1].

The EAR is the common currency for comparing quotes: two rates are equivalent when their EARs match. Exhibit 4 applies (1.4) to two Taiwan benchmarks: Bank of Taiwan's rate for one-year time savings deposits (定期儲蓄存款) and the legal cap on credit-card revolving interest.

Two features stand out. The EAR rises with n but by ever smaller steps, and it levels off at a finite limit. And frequency matters far more at high rates: moving from annual to continuous compounding adds 1.5 bp at 1.725% but 118 bp at 15%. The gap grows roughly with the square of the rate, a second-order effect that LM3 quantifies.

Example 2A Taiwan time deposit, two ways

Bank of Taiwan posts a fixed rate of 1.725% a year for one-year time savings deposits (定期儲蓄存款). DBS Bank Taiwan, for example, offers them as 整存整付, with interest compounded monthly and paid at maturity, or as 存本取息, with interest paid monthly. On its terms 存本取息 interest accrues daily, on actual days over a 365-day year. Compare the interest on NT$1,000,000 over one year (365 days).

Solution

整存整付 compounds the periodic rate i=0.01725/12=0.14375% twelve times: R=1.001437512−1=1.7387%, or NT$17,387 of interest at maturity.

存本取息 pays 1,000,000×1.725%×d/365 for a month of d days: NT$1,465.07 for 31 days, NT$1,417.81 for 30 days and NT$1,323.29 for 28 days. Over a 365-day year the payments total NT$17,250, an effective 1.7250% unless the saver reinvests each payment.

The same posted rate yields NT$137 more under monthly compounding, 1.37 bp of principal. At deposit rates near 2% the compounding frequency is almost a rounding error. The next example shows where it is not.

Example 3The credit-card cap and the ban on compounding

Since 1 September 2015, Article 47-1 of Taiwan's Banking Act has capped credit-card revolving interest at 15% a year. Issuers accrue it as balance × rate × days/365, their own day count. Article 48 of the Regulations Governing Institutions Engaging in Credit Card Business forbids compounding it.

Suppose a revolving balance of NT$100,000 runs for a 365-day year at the cap, with no repayments and no new purchases. Compute the interest, and compare it with daily compounding at the same stated rate.

Solution

Without compounding, interest accrues on the principal only: 100,000×15%×365/365=NT$15,000, an EAR of exactly 15.000%.

With daily compounding the periodic rate is i=0.15/365=0.041096% a day (about 萬分之 4.11), and (1.4) gives R=1.00041096365−1=16.180%, or NT$16,180. Continuous compounding would give e0.15−1=16.183%.

The no-compounding rule is worth NT$1,180 a year on this balance. It also makes the legal 15% an honest effective rate: a lender allowed to compound daily could quote 13.979% and still collect a 15% EAR.

From discrete to continuous: the number e

Push the frequency to its limit. In 1683 Jacob Bernoulli asked what happens to one unit at 100% interest a year when interest is credited ever more often (O'Connor and Robertson). With n credits the year-end value is (1+1/n)n, and Exhibit 5 shows it rising but leveling off.

A limit is the value a sequence approaches, as closely as we like, once n is large enough. LM2 makes limits and continuity precise; this section uses them informally.

4Holding-Period, Annualized and Continuously Compounded Returns

Holding-period return

DefinitionHolding-period return

The holding-period return (HPR) is the gain per unit invested over a holding period, income included. Over a single period,

Rt​=P~t−1​Pt​−P~t−1​+Dt​​,
where Dt​ is the cash dividend received in the period. P~t−1​ is the previous close restated per current share, equal to Pt−1​ except on a split day (Example 1). Over T periods with income reinvested, period returns chain by multiplication, and the HPR R0,T​ satisfies 1+R0,T​=∏t=1T​(1+Rt​)=G.

The HPR carries no time unit: +902.25% describes twelve years, while +10% might describe a week. A price return omits income; a total return includes it. Fund comparisons should use total returns, because funds differ in how much they distribute.

For 0050 over the case period the price return was +604.13% and the total return +902.25% (Example 1). Taking the dividends in cash instead, NT$42.19 per 2014 share, and holding the cash idle would have returned (459.80−65.30+42.19)/65.30=+668.74%.

Annualized return

To compare holdings of different lengths, express each as a constant annual rate. Measure the horizon in years as τ= calendar days ÷ 365.25, which averages the leap-year cycle. The annualized return RG​ is the return which, compounded once a year for τ years, produces the same growth factor:

(1+RG​)τ=G⟹RG​=G1/τ−1.(1.9)

The root law of Exhibit 2 does the work. For τ>1 the exponent 1/τ pulls the growth factor toward 1, shrinking gains and losses alike. For τ<1 it pushes the factor away from 1 and compounds a short-period return forward, which is extrapolation rather than measurement. LM3 calls RG​ the geometric mean return and compares it with the arithmetic mean.

Continuously compounded return

The log return over the holding period is r0,T​=ln(1+R0,T​)=lnG. Divided by the horizon, it gives the continuously compounded return per year, also called the log growth rate:

g=τlnG​=ln(1+RG​).(1.10)

The second equality is the power rule, lnG/τ=ln(G1/τ)=ln(1+RG​). Comparing (1.10) with (1.8) shows what g is: the continuously compounded rate equivalent to the effective annual rate RG​. The annualized return and the continuously compounded return are one growth rate quoted under two compounding conventions. 5Adding Returns Across Time and Across Assets

explain why log returns add across time while simple returns add across assets, and aggregate returns correctly in each direction

Across time, log returns add

Over T consecutive periods growth factors multiply, G=∏t​(1+Rt​). The product rule (1.2) turns the product into a sum:

lnG=t=1∑T​ln(1+Rt​)=t=1∑T​rt​.(1.12)

The identity is exact for any path. Simple returns do not add over time, and their sum has no meaning. Exhibit 8 splits 0050's twelve years into six two-year blocks.

The table also shows how simple returns distort comparisons. The last block's 149.606% looks more than ten times the second block's 14.063%; in log points it is seven times, 0.9147 against 0.1316. The final two blocks supplied 71% of the twelve-year log growth, the steep end of the log chart in Exhibit 3. Averaging the six simple returns would also overstate the typical compound return; LM3 derives that gap, volatility drag, and LM14 its general form, Jensen's inequality.

Across assets, simple returns add

A portfolio is a sum of money, not a product. Let J positions start with values Vj,0​, total value V0​>0 and weights wj​=Vj,0​/V0​, which sum to 1; a short position has Vj,0​<0 and wj​<0. If position j returns Rj​ over the period, the portfolio ends at ∑j​Vj,0​(1+Rj​), so

Rp​=j=1∑J​wj​Rj​(1.13)

exactly, with weights measured at the start of the period, whatever their signs. Log returns do not aggregate this way. The portfolio's log return is rp​=ln(∑j​wj​erj​).

Consider a long-only portfolio, with every wj​≥0 and ∑j​wj​=1. For positive xj​, the weighted arithmetic–geometric mean inequality states that ∑j​wj​xj​≥∏j​xjwj​​, with equality only when all xj​ with wj​>0 are equal. It is stated here without proof; LM14 derives it from Jensen's inequality. Applied with xj​=erj​ and followed by logs, it gives

rp​=ln(j∑​wj​erj​) ≥ j∑​wj​rj​(wj​≥0, ∑j​wj​=1),(1.14)

with equality only when every position with wj​>0 has the same log return. Averaging log returns across a long-only portfolio therefore understates its return, and the error grows with the dispersion of the positions' returns. With short positions the inequality can fail: weights (1.5,−0.5) and log returns (0.10,0.20) give rp​=ln(1.5e0.10−0.5e0.20)=0.0460, below ∑j​wj​rj​=0.0500.

Across multiplicative factors: currency and inflation

Some returns combine by multiplication rather than by weighting. A Taiwan investor in a US-dollar asset earns the asset's dollar return and the change in the TWD price of a dollar:

1+RTWD=(1+RUSD)(1+RFX)⟹rTWD=rUSD+rFX(1.15)

exactly in logs. In simple returns, RTWD=RUSD+RFX+RUSDRFX carries a cross term. Inflation works the same way: a real return satisfies 1+Rreal=(1+R)/(1+π), so rreal=r−ln(1+π) exactly. The familiar shortcut Rreal≈R−π drops a second-order term, which LM3 measures.

ResultAggregation rules
  • Across time: log returns add, (1.12); simple returns compound.
  • Across positions over one period: simple returns add with start-of-period weights of any sign, (1.13). Log returns do not; for long-only weights their average understates the portfolio, (1.14).
  • Across multiplicative factors such as currency and inflation: log returns add exactly, (1.15); simple returns need a cross term.
PitfallAveraging log returns across positions

Index and portfolio returns are weighted averages of simple returns with start-of-period weights. In a long-only sector or factor portfolio, the members' average log return is the log of a weighted geometric mean of their growth factors. By (1.14) it lies below what an investor holding the members earned. Aggregate simple returns across names first; take logs of the portfolio afterwards if a log-return series is needed.

6Solving Growth Equations with Logarithms

determine the time needed to reach a goal, the return required to reach it, and the gain and time needed to recover from a drawdown

One equation, four unknowns

With an annual rate R, equation (1.1) over τ years reads Wτ​=W0​(1+R)τ. It links four quantities, and any three determine the fourth. The ending value needs only multiplication; the starting value, a present value, is the subject of LM6. The other two need logarithms or roots:

τ=ln(1+R)ln(Wτ​/W0​)​=gln(Wτ​/W0​)​,R=(W0​Wτ​​)1/τ−1,g=τln(Wτ​/W0​)​.(1.16)

The time formula takes logs of both sides and applies the power rule, ln(Wτ​/W0​)=τln(1+R). In log terms every growth problem is a division: the log growth required, divided by the growth rate available or by the time available.

Doubling time

Setting Wτ​/W0​=2 gives the doubling time τ2​=ln2/ln(1+R)=ln2/g. With a continuously compounded rate the formula is exact and simple, τ2​=ln2/g≈0.6931/g. The familiar rule of 72 approximates ln2/ln(1+R) for annual rates; LM3 derives it from a Taylor expansion and explains why it works best near 8%. Drawdowns and recovery

A drawdown is the percentage decline from the highest value reached so far, DDt​:=1−Wt​/max(W0​,W1​,…,Wt​); the running peak includes the starting value W0​. Recovering from a drawdown DD requires a gain Rrec​ with (1−DD)(1+Rrec​)=1:

Rrec​=1−DD1​−1=1−DDDD​.(1.17)

The required gain grows faster than the loss: a 10% drawdown needs +11.1%, a 50% drawdown +100% and a 90% drawdown +900%. In logs the asymmetry disappears. A drawdown is a log return of ln(1−DD), and recovery is the same distance back, −ln(1−DD). At a log growth rate g, recovery therefore takes τrec​=−ln(1−DD)/g years by (1.16); Exhibit 10 tabulates both views.

7Return Calculations in Python

implement a total-return pipeline in Python that computes and converts these measures from prices and dividends

Three habits keep return code honest. Store prices and cash flows exactly as delivered and apply corporate actions explicitly. Compute log returns with log1p, which stays accurate for tiny returns. Keep every convention, such as the base date, the day count and A, in a named variable instead of a hard-coded number.

The first block rebuilds 0050's total-return series and reproduces the case. Run from the repository root, it reads the raw FinMind files that data/fetch_finmind.py downloads into data/raw/ (see the companion lab below).

Python
from datetime import date

import numpy as np
import polars as pl

# 1. FinMind closes (TaiwanStockPrice), dividends (TaiwanStockDividendResult)
px = pl.scan_csv("data/raw/0050__*.csv").select("date", "close").collect()
div = (pl.read_csv("data/raw/0050_dividends.csv")
         .select("date", pl.col("cash_dividend").alias("D")))
SPLIT, RATIO = "2025-06-18", 4.0                # 4-for-1, effective date

# 2. R_t = (P_t + D_t) / (P_{t-1} / ratio_t) - 1, close restated per new share
P, D = pl.col("close"), pl.col("D")
ratio = pl.when(pl.col("date") == pl.lit(SPLIT)).then(RATIO).otherwise(1.0)
ret = (px.join(div, on="date", how="left")
         .sort("date")          # never rely on row order after a join
         .with_columns(D.fill_null(0.0), ratio.alias("ratio"))
         .with_columns(R=(P + D) / (P.shift(1) / pl.col("ratio")) - 1)
         .drop_nulls("R")
         .with_columns(pl.col("date").str.to_date(), r=pl.col("R").log1p()))

# 3. growth factor G, annualized R_G and continuous g: (1.9), (1.10), (1.12)
BASE, YEAR = date(2014, 10, 31), 365.25   # base close; days per year
G = float(np.exp(ret["r"].sum()))
tau = (ret["date"][-1] - BASE).days / YEAR
print(f"G = {G:.4f}  HPR = {G - 1:.2%}  "
      f"R_G = {G ** (1 / tau) - 1:.2%}  g = {np.log(G) / tau:.2%}")
# G = 10.0225  HPR = 902.25%  R_G = 21.30%  g = 19.31%

The second block computes drawdowns from the running peak and finds the 2025 recovery date of Example 10.

Python
# 4. drawdown DD_t = 1 - W_t / running peak (incl. W_0 = 1), gain needed (1.17)
W = pl.col("W")
dd = (ret.select("date", W=(1 + pl.col("R")).cum_prod())
         .with_columns(DD=1 - W / pl.max_horizontal(W.cum_max(), 1.0)))
in_2025 = pl.col("date").is_between(date(2025, 1, 1), date(2025, 6, 30))
trough = dd.filter(in_2025).sort("DD", descending=True).row(0, named=True)
back = dd.filter(pl.col("date") > trough["date"], pl.col("DD") == 0)["date"][0]
need = trough["DD"] / (1 - trough["DD"])
print(trough["date"], f"DD {trough['DD']:.2%}", f"gain needed {need:.2%}",
      "recovered", back, (back - trough["date"]).days, "days")

On 18 June 2025 one old share became four new ones, so the previous close must be restated per new share, P~t−1​=Pt−1​/4. Without the adjustment the split would register as a fall of about 75%. Dropping the day would lose a real return, 47.57/(188.65/4)−1=+0.86% from the 10 June close. Every growth factor spanning it would be wrong: G would be 9.937 instead of 10.0225.

That return covers six market sessions, because 0050 was suspended from 11 to 17 June. Deleting the row before computing returns is no better. The 19 June close would then be measured against the 10 June close, and the spurious fall would simply move there: 47.10/188.65−1=−75.0%.

Summary

  • Growth multiplies and logarithms add: the product rule (1.2) turns products into sums and powers into multiples. Any logarithm is the natural log rescaled, logβ​x=lnx/lnβ; 0050's log growth of 2.3048 equals 3.325 doublings.
  • On a log axis equal distances are equal ratios and constant growth is a straight line. Linear axes make early history look flat and recent moves look extreme.
  • A stated rate r(n) compounded n times a year has an EAR of (1+r(n)/n)n−1. For a fixed quote r(n)=r it rises with n toward er−1. The number e=lim(1+1/n)n=2.71828… is one unit compounded continuously at 100% for a year.
  • Compounding frequency matters little at deposit rates (1.4 bp at 1.725%, monthly) and a great deal at high rates (118 bp at 15%, daily). Taiwan caps card revolving interest at 15% and forbids compounding it.
  • The holding-period return, the annualized return RG​=G1/τ−1 and the continuously compounded return g=lnG/τ express one growth factor three ways. Here τ is in years, calendar days ÷ 365.25. RG​ and g are one rate quoted annually and continuously, RG​=eg−1; for 0050 they were 21.30% and 19.31%.
  • Annualizing short-period returns is extrapolation and depends on conventions: a 90-day return of 10.33% annualizes to anything from 39.9% to 50.1%. Taiwan's market held about 244 sessions a year in 2015–2025, and the case sample has A=243.1, not 252.
  • Log returns add across time and simple returns add across positions. For long-only weights, averaging log returns across positions understates the portfolio; for a 50/50 mix of 0050 and 00631L the error was 615 percentage points.
  • Time to goal and required return come from one equation solved with logs. A drawdown DD needs a gain of DD/(1−DD) but only −ln(1−DD) log points. 0050 regained its January–April 2025 drawdown of 27.49%, which needed +37.91%, in 99 days.
