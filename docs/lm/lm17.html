Law of Large Numbers and Central Limit Theorem

Averages settle and sums turn normal. Compounded wealth follows the median path, not the mean path, and TAIEX shows how slowly the laws can work.

Learning Module Overview
  • The average of n uncorrelated draws keeps their mean and has variance σX2​/n, (17.1): its standard deviation falls like 1/n​. Identical copies do not average out at all.
  • Chebyshev's inequality turns (17.1) into the weak law of large numbers, (17.2). A fair coin's frequency misses one half by 0.1 or more with probability 0.7539, 0.05689 and 2.7×10−10 after 10, 100 and 1,000 tosses; Chebyshev's bound is 2.5, 0.25 and 0.025. The strong law speaks about whole paths, with probability one.
  • Realized log growth settles at g=E[ln(1+R)], (17.3), the median path. Expected wealth compounds faster, at ln(1+μ), and the chance of ending above it tends to zero, (17.4), slowly. Under an iid model of TAIEX's total-return days that chance is 0.32 after 23.8 years, and about 0.028 by the normal approximation at the in-sample Kelly leverage of 4.14.
  • TAIEX's average day, 0.06251%, compounds to 38.7 times the start over 5,849 days, while the index rose 25.2-fold. The factor 1.534 (38.66/25.20) between them is the daily Jensen gap of 0.7316 bp, accumulated.
  • The central limit theorem makes a standardized sum of iid draws with finite variance approximately normal, (17.5). The chance of being behind after 100 trades of the Kelly bet of LM4 is 0.04431 exactly, 0.04464 by the normal approximation and 0.04457 with a continuity correction.
  • Wealth is therefore approximately lognormal. Under the in-sample iid model of TAIEX, the normal approximation (17.6) puts the chance of a loss at 23.74% over one year and 1.19% over ten years. Of the 23 calendar years 2003–2025, 5 lost.
  • Excess kurtosis of a sum of m iid days falls like 1/m, (17.7): from 5.428 to 0.27 at 20 days. TAIEX's overlapping 20-day log-return sums show 2.29, because large moves cluster.
  • Without a mean, Cauchy averages never settle; without a variance, the n​ normal limit fails; without a fourth moment, a sample kurtosis never settles. TAIEX's running excess kurtosis jumped from 4.25 to 4.94 on 7 April 2025.

1Introduction

This module asks what happens to averages and sums of many independent random variables, such as a year's 246 daily returns. The law of large numbers says that an average settles at the expectation; the central limit theorem says that a standardized sum is approximately normal. The case shows what goes wrong when they are applied to the wrong quantity.

Motivating CaseCompounding the average day

From 2 January 2003 to 8 October 2026 the TAIEX total-return index, with dividends reinvested and no costs, rose 25.2-fold over 5,849 trading days. Its average daily return was 0.06251%. Compounding that average 5,849 times gives 38.7 times the start, against the index's 25.2 (Exhibit 1).

Now apply a constant leverage f to the same days, rebalanced daily with free borrowing (a hypothetical). Compounding the average day then promises more for every f: 2.2 million times the start at f=4. Realized wealth grows only up to f=4.14, to 2,147 times, and is back at the start at f=8.04. Which path does an investor live through, and why does the average day overstate it?

Sections 2 to 4 develop the laws of large numbers and resolve the case. Sections 5 and 6 add the central limit theorem and the lognormal approximation, and Sections 7 to 9 test the laws on TAIEX.

NoteNotation for this module

They span 8,680 calendar days from the first close, 2 January 2003, that is 8,680/365.25 = 23.7645 years, so A=246.12 sessions a year. Simple returns are Rt​; log returns rt​=ln(1+Rt​) enter every sum. Moments Rˉ, s, rˉ, sr​ divide by T. A horizon of τ years is m=round(Aτ) sessions, with wealth Wm​ at its end.

2Sums and Averages of Independent Variables

calculate the mean and variance of sums and averages of independent random variables, and explain why the standard deviation of an average falls like 1/n​

Take two independent trades of LM3's bet, each returning +2% or −1% with equal probability. By (13.6) and (13.7) the outcomes ++, +−, −+ and −− each have probability 41​. The average return of the two trades is 2%, 0.5%, 0.5% or −1%. Its mean is 0.5%, as for one trade, and its variance, in %², is

41​(1.52+02+02+1.52)=1.125,

half of one trade's 21​(1.52+1.52)=2.25. Averaging kept the mean and halved the variance.

Let X1​,…,Xn​ have a common mean μX​ and a common variance σX2​<∞. They are iid, independent and identically distributed, when they are independent ((13.13), (16.1)) and share one distribution. Their sum and average are

Sn​:=X1​+⋯+Xn​,Xˉn​:=nSn​​.

By (14.3), ESn​=nμX​. Independent draws with finite variance are uncorrelated (LM16), so the sum rule (16.3) gives VarSn​=nσX2​, and (14.4) with η=1/n divides it by n2.

ResultMean and variance of an average

Let X1​,…,Xn​ be iid with mean μX​ and variance σX2​<∞, or uncorrelated with a common mean μX​ and a common variance σX2​<∞. Then

EXˉn​=μX​,VarXˉn​=nσX2​​.(17.1)

The standard deviation σX​/n​ of the average is its standard error. LM4 used it with s in place of σX​, and LM20 derives that estimate. A hundred times as many draws cut the standard error by a factor of ten.

Example 1How many trades make the edge visible?

LM3's bet has mean μ=0.5% and standard deviation σ=1.5% per trade. Find the standard error of the average after 100 and after 10,000 independent trades, and the smallest n at which it is at most 0.25%, half the edge.

Solution

By (17.1), the standard error is 1.5%/n​: 0.15% after 100 trades and 0.015% after 10,000. It is at most 0.25% when n​≥1.5/0.25=6, that is, from n=36.

PitfallCorrelated days do not average out

Formula (17.1) needs uncorrelated draws. For identical copies, Xi​=X1​ for every i, the average is X1​ itself and keeps the variance σX2​. In the sum rule (16.3) the n(n−1) covariances all equal σX2​, so VarSn​=n2σX2​. Positively correlated days lie in between. Whether daily variances add up over months is a question about data, which LM19 examines.

Knowledge Check 1

Four independent days each have a standard deviation of 1.2%. What is the standard deviation of their average return? What is it if the four days are identical copies of one day?

Answer

By (17.1), 1.2%/4​=0.6%. For identical copies the average is the single day's return, so its standard deviation stays 1.2%.

3The Law of Large Numbers

derive the weak law of large numbers from Chebyshev's inequality, state the conditions of the weak and strong laws, and apply them to frequencies and to the binary bet of LM4

Toss a fair coin n times, with j heads. How fast does the frequency j/n approach 21​? The chance of a miss by 0.1 or more is an exact binomial sum, (15.1): 0.7539, 0.05689 and 2.7×10−10 at n = 10, 100 and 1,000.

Chebyshev's inequality (14.8) bounds the same chance without the pmf. By (17.1) the frequency has variance 41​/n, so the bound is (41​/n)/0.12=25/n: 2.5, 0.25 and 0.025. The bound is crude, but it tends to zero.

DefinitionConvergence in probability

Random variables Xˉn​ converge in probability to a number x when Pr(∣Xˉn​−x∣≥ε)→0 as n→∞, for every tolerance ε>0.

TheoremWeak law of large numbers

Let X1​,…,Xn​ be iid with mean μX​ and variance σX2​<∞, or uncorrelated with a common mean μX​ and a common variance σX2​<∞. Then for every ε>0

Pr(∣Xˉn​−μX​∣≥ε)≤nε2σX2​​,(17.2)
so Xˉn​ converges in probability to μX​.

Proof. If σX​=0, the average equals μX​ with probability one. Otherwise, by (17.1) it has mean μX​ and standard deviation σX​/n​. A miss of ε is εn​/σX​ standard deviations, so by (14.8) its probability is at most σX2​/(nε2). ■

Only (17.1) was used, so uncorrelated draws suffice (Durrett 2019, Theorem 2.2.3). For iid draws the weak law is Grinstead and Snell's (1997, §8.1, Theorem 8.2) and Wasserman's (2004, Theorem 5.6). For such draws a finite mean is even enough, with a longer proof (Durrett 2019, Theorem 2.2.14).

For independent trials that succeed with probability p, the indicator of the event Ai​ that trial i succeeds, 1Ai​​, has mean p by (14.1) and variance p(1−p)≤41​ by (14.4). The count j is their sum, so (17.2) gives

Pr(​nj​−p​≥ε)≤nε2p(1−p)​≤4nε21​.

A frequency converges in probability to its probability: this is the j/N→p of LM4's binary bet.

Example 2How many trials pin down a frequency?

By Chebyshev's bound, how many independent trials guarantee a frequency within one percentage point of p with probability at least 95%, whatever p is?

Solution

The worst case is p=21​, where p(1−p)=41​. The bound is at most 0.05 when n≥0.25/(0.012×0.05)=50,000. It is a property of a model of independent trials, not of trading days.

On TAIEX, 3,205 of TR23's 5,849 days rose and 2,644 fell (LM13), and exact arithmetic on the closes finds no unchanged day. Exhibit 2 plots the running share of up days: 0.5163 after 246 days, 0.5378 after 1,231 and p^​=0.5480 at the end.

The weak law speaks about each n separately, so the weak law alone does not exclude a frequency that wanders away from p again and again. The strong law speaks about a whole path.

TheoremStrong law of large numbers

Let X1​,X2​,… be iid with E∣X1​∣<∞ and μX​=EX1​. Then Xˉn​→μX​ with probability one: the paths along which the averages converge to μX​ have probability one. Stated without proof (Wasserman 2004, Theorem 5.18; proved in Durrett 2019, Theorem 2.4.1).

Convergence with probability one, or almost surely, is stronger than convergence in probability, and the strong law needs only a finite mean.

Deep DiveWhat "with probability one" addsoptional · click to expand

Toss a fair coin forever. The all-heads path exists, yet its probability is at most 2−n for every n, hence zero, an event of the kind LM13 described. The strong law says that all paths along which j/n fails to converge to 21​ have probability zero together. The weak law, about one n at a time, cannot say this. Grinstead and Snell (1997, §8.1, Exercise 16) give a sequence that obeys the weak law but not the strong.

LM3's Deep Dive made a weak-law argument: the sum of squared increments has mean τ and variance 2τΔt→0, so by (14.8) it converges in probability to τ. LM18 simulates random paths; here every probability is exact or an analytic approximation.

PitfallThe law does not correct runs

After ten up days, independent days make a down day no more likely; believing otherwise is the gambler's fallacy (Blitzstein and Hwang 2019, §10.2). A frequency converges because early surpluses are diluted, not repaid: the surplus of heads j−n/2 even has a growing standard deviation, n​/2.

導讀大數法則：偏差被稀釋，不會被扳回

大數法則（law of large numbers）說的是：獨立（independent）抽樣的樣本平均數（sample mean）會靠近母體（population）的期望值（expectation）。弱大數法則（weak law of large numbers）由謝比雪夫不等式（Chebyshev's inequality）證明：平均數的變異數（variance）是 σX2​/n，偏離超過 ε 的機率不超過 σX2​/(nε2)，這叫依機率收斂（convergence in probability）。平均數的標準差（standard deviation）σX​/n​ 稱為標準誤（standard error），樣本加大一百倍，標準誤只縮小為十分之一。強大數法則（strong law of large numbers）講的是整條路徑：平均數不收斂的路徑，機率合起來是零，這叫幾乎必然收斂（almost sure convergence）。頻率會收斂，是因為早期的偏差被稀釋，不是因為之後會補回來；以為連漲之後比較容易跌，就是賭者謬誤（gambler's fallacy）。

4Growth: the Mean Path and the Median Path

explain why realized log growth converges to g=E[ln(1+R)] while expected wealth compounds at ln(1+μ), and why the chance of ending above expected wealth tends to zero

LM3 warned that +100% and −50% leave wealth where it started. Play that bet ten times.

Example 3Ten rounds of +100% or −50%

Each round independently doubles or halves wealth with probability 21​. Find EW10​, the median of W10​, and the chances of ending above EW10​ and below W0​.

Solution

With j wins, W10​/W0​=22j−10, and j∼Bin(10,21​). Each round multiplies expected wealth by 21​(2+0.5)=1.25, and independent factors multiply in expectation, (16.1), so EW10​/W0​=1.2510=9.3132.

Five wins return exactly to the start: Pr(W10​=W0​)=63/256=0.2461. Wealth is at most W0​ with probability 638/1,024, and at least W0​ with the same probability, so the median is W0​.

Only j≥7 ends above the mean, since 24=16>9.3132>22. Those paths have probability 11/64=0.1719 but carry 8.1875 of the 9.3132, 87.9% of the mean (Exhibit 3). With j≤4 the investor loses: Pr(W10​<W0​)=193/512=0.3770. After 100 rounds, EW100​/W0​=4.91×109, yet Pr(W100​>EW100​)=0.00044 and Pr(W100​<W0​)=0.4602.

Peters (2019) makes the same point with a coin paying +50% or −40%. Expected wealth grows by a factor 1.05 a round. Expected log wealth changes by 21​ln0.9=−0.0527 a round, a typical factor of 0.9​=0.949.

Realized growth: the median path

Over m days, by (1.12),

lnW0​Wm​​=t=1∑m​ln(1+Rt​)=t=1∑m​rt​,

so the strong law applies to the average log return.

TheoremRealized growth rate

Let the daily returns R1​,R2​,… be iid with 1+Rt​>0 and E∣ln(1+Rt​)∣<∞, and let g=E[ln(1+R)]. Then, with probability one,

m1​lnW0​Wm​​→g.(17.3)

For LM4's binary bet at a position f, the log return is ln(1+fb) with probability p and ln(1−fa) with probability q, so (4.7) is the two-outcome case of (17.3).

For two positions with g(f)>g(f~​) on the same trades, the average log growths differ in the limit by g(f)−g(f~​)>0. The ratio of their wealths thus grows without bound, with probability one (Breiman 1961; Thorp 2006). General bets, and what maximizing the median path assumes, are LM41's and LM42's subjects.

Expected wealth: the mean path

When the Rt​ are independent with mean μ and E∣Rt​∣<∞, the factors multiply in expectation by (16.1):

EWm​=W0​t=1∏m​E(1+Rt​)=W0​(1+μ)m.

Call W0​(1+μ)m the mean path and W0​emg the median path; Section 6 shows that the latter is approximately the median of Wm​. By strict Jensen, (14.10) and (14.12), g<ln(1+μ) unless R is constant. The difference δ:=ln(1+μ)−g>0 is LM14's daily Jensen gap, and over m days the mean path pulls ahead by the factor emδ.

TheoremThe mean path runs away from the median path

Let R1​,…,Rm​ be independent with mean μ and E∣Rt​∣<∞. Then EWm​=W0​(1+μ)m. If, in addition, the Rt​ are iid with 1+Rt​>0 and 0<σr​<∞, then

Pr(Wm​>EWm​)→0(m→∞).(17.4)

Proof of the limit. The event {Wm​>EWm​} says that the average log return m1​ln(Wm​/W0​) exceeds its mean g by more than δ, which is positive because σr​>0. By (17.2) its probability is at most a constant divided by m (Problem 8). ■

Expected wealth is carried by rare paths far above the median, as in Exhibit 3; the iid bootstrap of LM18 has exactly this mean path.

Example 4Resolving the case: compounding the average day

TR23 has Rˉ=0.062505% and rˉ=0.055170% a day. Explain the gap between the mean path's 38.66 and the realized 25.20. Then estimate the chance of ending above the mean path after m=T days, in an iid model that draws days from the sample.

Solution

The mean path is (1+Rˉ)5849=38.66, and the realized wealth is eTrˉ=25.20 by (1.12), the fitted median path. LM14 found the daily Jensen gap δ=ln(1+Rˉ)−rˉ=0.7316 bp, which needs six significant figures: Rˉ=0.0625051%, rˉ=0.0551696%. Over the sample, 5,849×0.7316 bp =0.4279, and e0.4279=1.534=38.66/25.20.

With σr​=sr​=1.2107%, Section 6 justifies the normal approximation

Pr(Wm​>EWm​)5849​×1.2107%0.7316 bp​Φ(−0.462)​≈Φ(−m​δ/σr​),=0.462,=0.32.​
After 23.8 years the chance is still 0.32. A chance of 5% needs m​δ/σr​≥1.645: about 74,000 days, or 300 years. Chebyshev's bound, σr2​/(Tδ2)=4.68, is empty.

Example 5Leverage on TAIEX

Hold a constant leverage f on TR23's days, rebalanced daily with free borrowing and no costs (a hypothetical). Compare realized wealth with the mean path (1+fRˉ)T for f = 1, 2, 4, 6 and 8, and find the growth-maximizing leverage.

Solution

Realized wealth is WT​/W0​=eTgˉ​(f), and annual log growth Agˉ​(f)=ln(WT​/W0​)/23.7645:

Leverage f WT​/W0​ Agˉ​(f) Mean path (1+fRˉ)T
1 25.20 13.58% 38.66
2 267.7 23.52% 1,491
4 2,127.8 32.24% 2.20×106
6 405.6 25.27% 3.23×109

The mean path rises with every f; realized wealth peaks. The root of gˉ​′(f)=0, (4.5), is f∗=4.1381: 32.28% a year, 2,147 times the start and 85 times the unlevered end. Growth is zero again at 8.04, or 1.94 times f∗, LM4's "about twice" (Exhibit 4).

Leverage makes the mean path hard to beat. At the Kelly leverage, δ(f∗)=12.72 bp and sr​(f∗)=5.079%, so after 5,849 days

z=5849​×5.079%12.72 bp​=1.915,Pr(Wm​>EWm​)≈Φ(−z)=0.028.​
The realized path is hard to live through. At f∗ it fell 98.5% from its peak by 20 November 2008, a risk LM41 returns to.

PitfallThe law fixes the rate, not the level

Formula (17.3) says that m1​ln(Wm​/W0​) settles at g, not that Wm​ settles near W0​emg. The log of their ratio has standard deviation σr​m​, which grows. Over TR23's 5,849 days sr​T​=0.9259, so one standard deviation is a factor e0.9259=2.52 either way.

導讀期望值路徑與中位數路徑

財富取對數後是每日對數報酬（log return）的和，所以依強大數法則（strong law of large numbers），實現的每日對數成長率會收斂到 g=E[ln(1+R)]。期望值（expectation）卻以 ln(1+μ) 的速度複利，比 g 快，差距 δ 來自 Jensen 不等式（Jensen's inequality）。期望值靠少數極端好的路徑撐起來，一般投資人走的是中位數（median）路徑 W0​emg。期數拉長，贏過期望值路徑的機率會趨近於零，但很慢：用加權股價報酬指數的獨立同分布（iid）模型，23.8 年後仍有 0.32。加上 Kelly 槓桿就快得多，只剩約 0.028，代價是那條路徑在 2008 年一度跌掉 98.5%。

5The Central Limit Theorem

state the central limit theorem with its conditions, and apply the normal approximation to binomial counts

The central limit theorem says how an average is spread on its way to the mean. LM4 held LM3's +2%/−1% bet at its Kelly position f∗=25. Call this the Kelly bet: each trade multiplies wealth by 1.5 or 0.75 with equal probability, with growth g∗=5.889% per trade. After 100 trades the number of wins is Bin(100,21​). Its histogram in Exhibit 5 is close to the normal density with mean 50 and standard deviation 5.

DefinitionConvergence in distribution

The standardized sums Zn​:=(Sn​−nμX​)/(σX​n​) converge in distribution to a standard normal Z when Pr(Zn​≤z)→Φ(z) for every z. In general, distribution functions must converge wherever the limit's is continuous.

TheoremCentral limit theorem

Let X1​,X2​,… be iid with mean μX​ and variance 0<σX2​<∞. Then, as n→∞, for every real z

Pr(σX​n​Sn​−nμX​​≤z)→Φ(z).(17.5)
Stated without proof (Wasserman 2004, Theorem 5.8; Grinstead and Snell 1997, §9.1–§9.3).

So for large n the average is approximately N(μX​,σX2​/n). The theorem gives no error for a given n, and error bounds are not in this book.

A binomial count is a sum of n iid indicators, so it has mean np and variance np(1−p), (15.2). For an integer j0​, (17.5) with the continuity correction gives

導讀中央極限定理：和會變成常態

中央極限定理（central limit theorem）說：獨立同分布（iid）的抽樣只要變異數（variance）有限，標準化之後的和會依分布收斂（convergence in distribution）到標準常態分布（standard normal distribution）。所以 n 夠大時，和與平均數（mean）都「近似」常態；這是極限定理，並沒有說某個 n 的誤差多大。二項分布（binomial distribution）的計數是 n 個指示函數（indicator）的和，常態逼近（normal approximation）加上連續性校正（continuity correction），把面積算到 j0​+21​，通常相當準。在遠端尾部，絕對誤差雖小，相對誤差卻可能很大。Kelly 賭局 400 次後落後的機率約 0.0004：加上連續性校正的常態逼近高估 2.6%（100 次時只高估 0.6%），未校正的則低估 14%。

6Wealth Is Approximately Lognormal

compute the median, quantiles and loss probability of wealth under the lognormal approximation, with and without leverage

By (17.5), the sum ln(Wm​/W0​) of m iid log returns is approximately N(mg,mσr2​), so Wm​/W0​ is approximately lognormal, (15.7). The exponential is increasing, so the quantiles of Wm​ are the exponentials of the normal quantiles of (15.6).

ResultLognormal approximation of wealth

Let the daily returns be iid with 1+R>0 and 0<σr​<∞, and let m be large. Then

Pr(Wm​<W0​)≈Φ(−σr​gm​​),FWm​−1​(u)≈W0​emg+Φ−1(u)σr​m​,​(17.6)
so the median is about W0​emg and the 5% and 95% quantiles about W0​emg∓1.645σr​m​. Each "≈" is the error of (17.5), large in relative terms far in the tail. For iid normal log returns, (17.6) is exact.

Likewise {Wm​>EWm​} is {ln(Wm​/W0​)−mg>mδ}, so Pr(Wm​>EWm​)≈Φ(−m​δ/σr​), as used in Example 4.

7How Fast the Normal Arrives on TAIEX

measure how fast sums of TAIEX log returns approach the normal shape, and explain how dependence slows the approach

A sum of fat-tailed iid days becomes normal only as m grows. Excess kurtosis, Kurt−3 by (14.5), measures the progress: it is 0 for a normal law by (14.6).

Let X1​,…,Xm​ be iid with EX4<∞ and σX​>0, and let Yi​=Xi​−μX​. In the expansion of (Y1​+⋯+Ym​)4, a product in which some index appears once has expectation 0, by (16.1) and EYi​=0. The m products Yi4​ have expectation Kurt(X)σX4​ each, by (14.5), and the 3m(m−1) products Yi2​Yk2​ with i=k have σX4​ each (Problem 9). Dividing by (VarSm​)2=m2σX4​,

Kurt(Sm​)=m2mKurt(X)+3m(m−1)​,Kurt(Sm​)−3=mKurt(X)−3​.​(17.7)

Sample kurtosis and its divisor are LM19's subject; here the divisor is T, as in LM5.

8When the Laws Fail

identify when the laws fail (no mean, infinite variance, infinite fourth moment, dependence) and what each failure does to a sample statistic

Exhibit 8 lists what happens when a condition is missing, for the laws of LM5's tails exhibit and for dependent draws.

No mean. The standard Cauchy density 1/(π(1+x2)) is the t density (15.9) with ν=1. Its tails fall like x−2, so E∣X∣=∞ and the mean does not exist (LM5).

Example 10Averages that never settle

Compare Pr(∣Xˉn​∣>1) for averages of n independent standard Cauchy draws and of standard normal draws, at n = 1, 4 and 25.

Solution

The average of n independent standard Cauchy draws is again standard Cauchy (stated without proof; Grinstead and Snell 1997, §8.2, Example 8.8). Its distribution function is 21​+π1​arctanx, so Pr(∣Xˉn​∣>1)=1−π2​arctan1=21​ for every n. A normal average has standard deviation 1/n​, so the chance is 2Φ(−n​): 0.317, 0.0455 and 5.7×10−7. Without a finite mean, iid averages converge with probability zero (Durrett 2019, Theorem 2.3.8).

No variance. A Pareto draw with α=1.5, or a t draw with 1<ν≤2, has a mean but no variance. The strong law holds, but sums need a larger scale than n​ and tend to stable laws (stated without proof; that theorem is not in this book). Mandelbrot (1963) modelled cotton prices with such laws; Cont (2001) puts the tail index of most return series between 2 and 5.

No fourth moment. With a tail index near 3, as LM5 reported for index returns, the mean and the variance exist but the fourth moment does not. The running mean and variance settle, while the sample kurtosis estimates an infinite quantity. Exhibit 9 shows TR23's running excess kurtosis, which 7 April 2025 alone moved from 4.25 to 4.94 (LM15).

A few days decide it. The ten largest of TR23's 5,849 terms supply 35.0% of ∑t​(Rt​−Rˉ)4 but only 6.7% of ∑t​(Rt​−Rˉ)2 (LM15). For 0050, LM5 found 62.8% and 14.1%, and a jump of the excess kurtosis from 5.84 to 7.63 on the same day.

Dependence and change. Identical copies are the extreme of dependence. Milder dependence, such as Section 7's clustering, slows the approach to the normal shape. Laws for dependent sequences, the ergodic theorems, are not in this book. A changing distribution also breaks iid: TR23's daily log returns had excess kurtosis 3.44 before 1 June 2015 and 8.41 after, when the daily limit was 10% instead of 7%.

PitfallA finite sample kurtosis shows no finite fourth moment

Every sample has a finite kurtosis: TR23's excess kurtosis is 5.27 (kurtosis 8.27, LM14), whatever the population. What signals trouble is a running value that jumps with single days, as in Exhibit 9. Quote a sample kurtosis with its period, and prefer tail measures that need fewer moments (LM15).

導讀定理失效的時候

這些定理都有條件。沒有平均數（mean）時，例如 Cauchy 分布，樣本平均數（sample mean）永遠不會收斂：n 個獨立（independent）Cauchy 抽樣的平均仍是 Cauchy 分布。變異數（variance）無限大時，強大數法則（strong law of large numbers）仍然成立，但以 n​ 標準化的中央極限定理（central limit theorem）失效，極限是穩定分布（stable law）。四階動差（fourth moment）無限大時，樣本平均數與樣本變異數會收斂，樣本峰態（sample kurtosis）卻永遠穩定不下來；加權股價報酬指數的超額峰態（excess kurtosis）在 2025 年 4 月 7 日一天內從 4.25 跳到 4.94。獨立同分布（iid）的假設本身也可能不成立：大漲大跌會群聚，2015 年 6 月漲跌幅限制放寬以後，分布也變了。

9The Laws in Python

compute running averages, sums over windows of m days and normal approximations in Python, counting threshold days with the book's guard

Log returns enter every sum, simple returns the count of up days. The guard R > 1e-12 keeps a return that is zero up to rounding out of the up days (LM5).

Python
import numpy as np
import polars as pl
from scipy import stats

tr = pl.read_csv("data/taiex_tr.csv")["price"].to_numpy()
R = tr[1:] / tr[:-1] - 1                  # T = 5,849 simple returns
r = np.log1p(R)                           # log returns, for every sum
n = np.arange(1, len(R) + 1)
up_share = np.cumsum(R > 1e-12) / n       # running share of up days
mean_r = np.cumsum(r) / n                 # running mean log return


def window_sums(x, m):                    # all len(x) - m + 1 sums of m days
    c = np.concatenate([[0.0], np.cumsum(x)])
    return c[m:] - c[:-m]


for m in (1, 5, 20, 60):                  # 5.43, 3.63, 2.29, 3.20
    print(m, stats.kurtosis(window_sums(r, m)))
print(stats.binom.cdf(41, 100, 0.5))      # 0.04431, exact
print(stats.norm.cdf((41.5 - 50) / 5))    # 0.04457, continuity-corrected

By default stats.kurtosis returns excess kurtosis with divisor T (fisher=True, bias=True); Polars' std divides by T−1 unless given ddof=0.

Summary

  • An average of n uncorrelated draws keeps the mean and divides the variance by n, (17.1).
  • Chebyshev's inequality gives the weak law (17.2); the strong law needs only a finite mean.
  • Realized growth settles at g, (17.3); the mean path compounds faster, and the chance of beating it tends to zero, (17.4), slowly.
  • Standardized sums are approximately normal when the variance is finite, (17.5), so wealth is approximately lognormal, (17.6).
  • Excess kurtosis of iid sums falls like 1/m, (17.7), but TAIEX's 20-day sums keep 2.29.
  • Without a mean, a variance or a fourth moment, the matching law fails.
