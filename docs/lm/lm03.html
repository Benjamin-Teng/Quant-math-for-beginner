Taylor Series and Approximation

How much is ≈ worth? Local polynomial models, their error, and the money they explain.

Learning Module Overview
  • A Taylor polynomial is the best local polynomial model of a smooth function: it matches the function's value and its first n derivatives at one point.
  • Three expansions — ex, ln(1+x) and (1+x)α — generate most approximations used in finance. The series for ln(1+x) converges only for −1<x≤1.
  • The Lagrange remainder turns “≈” into a statement with an error bound. The error of an order-n approximation shrinks like ∣x∣n+1.
  • Log and simple returns differ by R2/2 to second order. Over a sample, geometric return ≈ arithmetic mean − half the variance: this is volatility drag, and it is an approximation, not an identity.
  • A daily-reset L× fund earns approximately the L-th power of the index's growth, taxed by exp(−2L(L−1)​∑Rt2​). On twelve years of 0050 data, the second-order term matches the exact rebalancing effect to within 0.0001 log points.
  • “Leveraged ETFs decay” and “leveraged ETFs beat L×” are both true, against different benchmarks. In strong trends a 2× fund can more than double the index's return.
  • Duration–convexity, delta–gamma and Itô's lemma are all second-order Taylor expansions. Itô's lemma is the one in which the second-order term does not vanish.

1Introduction

Almost every formula a trader uses daily is an approximation in disguise: “log return ≈ simple return”, “geometric mean ≈ arithmetic mean − σ²/2”, “a bond's price change ≈ −duration × yield change”. Each is the first or second term of a Taylor expansion. Using them well requires knowing three things: where they come from, how wrong they are, and when they break. This module answers all three, and then uses the answers on real money.

Motivating CaseTwo numbers that cannot both be true

From the close of 31 October 2014 to 8 October 2026, Yuanta Taiwan 50 (0050) returned +902% including dividends: one unit grew to 10.02. Over the same days the daily-reset 2× fund on the same index, Yuanta Taiwan 50 Bull 2X (00631L), returned +4,354%: one unit grew to 44.54. “Twice the index's return” would have been +1,804%. The leveraged fund delivered about 2.4 times twice the index.

Then take calendar 2022 in the US. QQQ lost 32.6%; the 3× fund TQQQ lost 79.1%. Three times QQQ's loss is −97.7%, so TQQQ lost less than three times the index.

Yet every regulator and textbook warns that leveraged ETFs decay. How can a fund decay and beat its multiple at the same time? How large is the decay, and what sets its size? By Section 7 you will be able to split each fund's return into three numbers and reproduce the result to four decimals.

The tool that resolves the paradox is a second-order Taylor expansion of ln(1+x). Sections 2–4 build the tool and its error bound. Sections 5–7 apply it to returns, means and leverage. Section 8 shows the same idea in bond pricing, option hedging and, as a preview of LM28, Itô's lemma.

NoteNotation used throughout the book

R is a simple return for one period, R=Pt​/Pt−1​−1 (plus income). r=ln(1+R) is the corresponding log return. μ and σ are the mean and standard deviation of R. g is a log growth rate per period and G a growth factor (end value ÷ start value). We write = only for exact statements, ≈ for approximations whose neglected order is stated, and := for definitions.

2The Taylor Polynomial: A Local Model

construct the Taylor polynomial of order n of a smooth function about a point, and interpret its terms as level, slope and curvature

Tangent line, then curvature

Near a point a, a smooth function looks like a straight line: its tangent. The tangent line is the first-order model

f(x)≈f(a)+f′(a)(x−a).

For f(x)=ln(1+x) at a=0, f(0)=0 and f′(0)=1, so ln(1+x)≈x. That is the familiar “log return ≈ simple return”. It is exact at x=0 and drifts away as x grows, because ln(1+x) bends downward. A better model adds the bend, measured by the second derivative:

f(x)≈f(a)+f′(a)(x−a)+21​f′′(a)(x−a)2.

With f′′(x)=−1/(1+x)2 and f′′(0)=−1 this gives ln(1+x)≈x−x2/2. The −x2/2 term is the curvature correction, and it is the source of every “−σ2/2” in this book.

DefinitionTaylor polynomial

Let f have n derivatives at a. The Taylor polynomial of order n of f about a is

Tn​(x):=k=0∑n​k!f(k)(a)​(x−a)k=f(a)+f′(a)(x−a)+2!f′′(a)​(x−a)2+⋯+n!f(n)(a)​(x−a)n.(3.1)
When a=0 it is also called a Maclaurin polynomial. Letting n→∞ gives the Taylor series, when that series converges.

Why the coefficients are f(k)(a)/k!

Tn​ is built so that it agrees with f at a in value, slope, curvature and every higher derivative up to order n. Write a general polynomial p(x)=∑k=0n​ck​(x−a)k and differentiate it j times. Every term with k<j disappears, every term with k>j still contains a factor (x−a) and vanishes at x=a, and the term k=j becomes j!cj​. So

p(j)(a)=j!cj​.

Requiring p(j)(a)=f(j)(a) for j=0,…,n forces cj​=f(j)(a)/j!. The factorial is not decoration: it undoes the factorial that repeated differentiation of (x−a)j produces.

Each term has a job. The constant fixes the level, the linear term the slope, the quadratic term the curvature, and higher terms correct asymmetry and tails. In finance the terms through second order usually suffice, which is why “level, slope, curvature” recurs in duration–convexity, delta–gamma and the volatility drag.

Example 1Approximating ln(1.05) and e0.05

A stock returns +5% in a month. Approximate its log return ln(1.05) and the growth factor e0.05 of a 5% continuously compounded rate with Taylor polynomials of order 1, 2 and 3 about 0, and report each error.

Solution

For ln(1+x) with x=0.05: T1​=0.05, T2​=0.05−0.00125=0.04875, T3​=0.04875+0.0000417=0.0487917. The exact value is ln1.05=0.0487902, so the errors f−Tn​ are −0.0012098, +0.0000402 and −0.0000015.

For ex: T1​=1.05, T2​=1.05125, T3​=1.0512708 against the exact 1.0512711, with errors +0.0012711, +0.0000211 and +0.0000003.

The error after Tn​ is roughly the first omitted term, so each extra order shrinks it by the ratio of successive terms. For ln(1+x) that ratio is x(n+1)/(n+2), here about 1/30 and 1/27. For ex it is x/(n+2), about 1/60 and 1/80, because the factorials make its terms shrink faster. That ratio is the practical rule for how many terms you need.

導讀泰勒多項式在做什麼

把一個彎曲的函數，在某一點附近用多項式來近似：常數項對準高度，一次項對準斜率，二次項對準彎曲程度。在收斂範圍內，階數越高、離展開點越近，近似得越好。金融裡最重要的是二次項，因為 ln(1+x) 往下彎，所以「對數報酬 ≈ 簡單報酬」永遠有一個 −x2/2 的修正，後面所有的 −σ2/2 都從這裡來。

Knowledge Check 1

In T2​(x)=f(a)+f′(a)(x−a)+21​f′′(a)(x−a)2, which coefficient would you change to make the approximation bend upward instead of downward, and what property of f does it encode?

Answer

The quadratic coefficient 21​f′′(a). Its sign is the sign of the curvature: f′′(a)>0 (convex) bends up, f′′(a)<0 (concave) bends down. ln(1+x) is concave, so its quadratic term is negative.

3Four Expansions That Run Finance

derive the expansions of ex, ln(1+x) and (1+x)α, and state where each one converges

Three Maclaurin series, plus the geometric series they all lean on, generate most approximations in this book.

The exponential. Every derivative of ex is ex, so f(k)(0)=1 for all k and

ex=k=0∑∞​k!xk​=1+x+2x2​+6x3​+⋯for all real x.(3.2)

The logarithm. Differentiate f(x)=ln(1+x) repeatedly: f′(x)=(1+x)−1, f′′(x)=−(1+x)−2, f′′′(x)=2(1+x)−3, and in general f(k)(x)=(−1)k+1(k−1)!(1+x)−k. At 0 this gives f(k)(0)/k!=(−1)k+1/k, so

ln(1+x)=k=1∑∞​k(−1)k+1xk​=x−2x2​+3x3​−4x4​+⋯for −1<x≤1.(3.3)

A second derivation explains the range. For ∣t∣<1 the geometric series gives 1+t1​=1−t+t2−t3+⋯. Integrating from 0 to x term by term yields (3.3) for ∣x∣<1. At x=1 the alternating series still converges, to ln2 (by Abel's theorem), which is why the range is −1<x≤1.

The binomial series. For any real exponent α, f(x)=(1+x)α has f(k)(0)=α(α−1)⋯(α−k+1), so

(1+x)α=k=0∑∞​(kα​)xk=1+αx+2α(α−1)​x2+⋯for ∣x∣<1,(3.4)

where (kα​)=α(α−1)⋯(α−k+1)/k!. With α=−1 it is the alternating geometric series 1/(1+x)=1−x+x2−⋯. With α=21​ it gives 1+x​≈1+2x​−8x2​. When α is a positive integer the series stops and (3.4) is the ordinary binomial theorem.

The radius of convergence has a financial meaning

The series (3.3) diverges for x>1 and the function itself does not exist at x=−1. Both edges matter. At x=−1, a return of −100%, the log of wealth is −∞: that is ruin, and it is why log-utility investors such as Kelly bettors (LM41) never accept a position that can lose everything. For x>1, ln(1+x) is perfectly finite, but no number of series terms will reach it. A truncated series is a local tool; for large moves, compute the exact function.

PitfallSeries approximations for large returns

For a +150% year, x−x2/2=1.5−1.125=0.375, while ln2.5=0.916. Adding terms makes it worse, because the series diverges for x>1. Use ln(1+R) itself whenever ∣R∣ is not small. A rough working limit for the second-order formula is ∣R∣≤0.1, where its error stays below 4 bp of log return (Section 4).

Example 2Real returns and the Fisher approximation

A one-year deposit pays a nominal rate i=1.725% while expected inflation is π=1.8%. Compute the real rate exactly and with first- and second-order approximations.

Solution

Exactly, 1+Rreal​=(1+i)/(1+π), so Rreal​=1.01725/1.018−1=−0.07367%.

Expanding 1/(1+π)=1−π+π2−⋯ gives Rreal​=(i−π)(1−π+π2−⋯). To first order Rreal​≈i−π=−0.07500%. Keeping the next term, Rreal​≈(i−π)−π(i−π)=−0.07365%, which matches the exact value to within 0.0001 percentage points. The first-order rule i−π is the familiar Fisher approximation.

4How Good Is “≈”? The Remainder Term

bound the truncation error with the Lagrange remainder, and decide when a truncated expansion can be trusted

An approximation without an error estimate is a guess. Taylor's theorem supplies the estimate.

TheoremTaylor's theorem with Lagrange remainder

Let f have n+1 continuous derivatives on an interval containing a and x. Then

f(x)=Tn​(x)+Rn​(x),Rn​(x)=(n+1)!f(n+1)(ξ)​(x−a)n+1(3.5)
for some ξ strictly between a and x.

Read (3.5) as: the error is the next term of the series, evaluated at an unknown point ξ. We rarely know ξ, but we usually know how large f(n+1) can be on the interval, and that bounds the error. In big-O notation we write Rn​(x)=O(∣x−a∣n+1): halve the distance to a and an order-n error shrinks by a factor of about 2n+1.

The error of “log return ≈ R−R2/2”

For f(x)=ln(1+x), f′′′(ξ)=2/(1+ξ)3, so with n=2 and a=0

ln(1+x)=x−2x2​+3(1+ξ)3x3​.

For x>0, ξ∈(0,x) gives 1+ξ>1, and the error lies in (0, x3/3). For −1<x<0, ξ∈(x,0) gives 1+ξ>1+x, and the error is negative with size at most ∣x∣3/(3(1+x)3). Losses are approximated less accurately than gains of the same size, because ln(1+x) bends harder on the left.

Example 3The two worst days in 0050's history

On 5 August 2024, 0050 fell 9.130%. On 7 April 2025, after the US tariff announcement, it fell the full daily limit of 10.000%. For each day, compute the log return exactly and with T1​ and T2​, and check the error against the Lagrange bound.

Solution

5 August 2024, R=−0.091299: exact ln(1+R)=−0.095740. T1​=−0.091299 is off by 44.4 bp; T2​=−0.095467 is off by 2.7 bp; the bound is 0.0912993/(3×0.9087013)=3.4 bp.

7 April 2025, R=−0.100000: exact −0.105361. T1​ is off by 53.6 bp; T2​=−0.105000 is off by 3.6 bp, inside the bound of 4.6 bp.

Even on record crash days, the second-order formula is good to a few basis points of log return. The first-order formula is not: treating a −10% day as a −10% log return understates the damage by more than half a percentage point.

Example 4Why the “rule of 72” works

Money growing at rate R per year doubles in T∗=ln2/ln(1+R) years. Expand the denominator to explain where 72 comes from and why it works best near 8%.

Solution

From (3.3), ln(1+R)=R(1−2R​+3R2​−⋯). Using 1/(1−u)≈1+u with u=2R​−3R2​:

T∗≈Rln2​(1+2R​)=R0.693​+0.347.
At R=6% this gives 11.899 years against the exact 11.896. Writing the formula as k/(100R) requires k=69.3+34.7R, which equals 72 at R≈7.8%. The rule of 72 is a second-order Taylor approximation tuned for returns near 8%.

5Simple Returns and Log Returns

relate simple and log returns, and arithmetic and geometric means, through second-order expansions, and quantify volatility drag

A holding-period (simple) return and a log return describe the same price move:

Rt​=Pt−1​Pt​+Dt​​−1,rt​=ln(1+Rt​).

From (3.3) and (3.2), each is a series in the other:

r=R−2R2​+3R3​−⋯,R=er−1=r+2r2​+6r3​+⋯.(3.6)

Because ln(1+x) is concave and touches the line y=x at the origin, ln(1+x)≤x for every x>−1, with equality only at 0. So the log return is always the smaller number, by about R2/2.

Two aggregation rules, two return types

Each definition is additive along a different dimension, which is why both are needed.

  • Across time, log returns add. ln(PT​/P0​)=∑t=1T​rt​, because the log of a product is a sum. Simple returns compound instead: 1+R0→T​=∏t​(1+Rt​).
  • Across assets, simple returns add. A portfolio with weights wi​ earns Rp​=∑i​wi​Ri​ exactly. Log returns do not: rp​=ln(∑i​wi​eri​)=∑i​wi​ri​.

The working rule follows. Use log returns for research along the time axis — trends, volatility, cross-asset comparisons. Use simple returns whenever money is aggregated across positions — portfolio accounting, settlement, drawdown in currency.

The gap in real data

Over 2,902 trading days from November 2014 to October 2026, 0050's mean daily simple return was 0.0871% and its mean daily log return 0.0794%. The difference, 0.0077% (0.77 bp) a day, is what (3.6) predicts: half the mean squared return is 0.771 bp, and half the variance is 0.767 bp. Summed over a year of about 243 trading days, this “small” daily gap is roughly 1.9 percentage points of annual return.

6Arithmetic Mean, Geometric Mean and Volatility Drag

relate simple and log returns, and arithmetic and geometric means, through second-order expansions, and quantify volatility drag

Given returns R1​,…,RT​, three averages answer three different questions.

Rˉ=T1​t=1∑T​Rt​,RG​=(t=1∏T​(1+Rt​))1/T−1,g=ln(1+RG​)=T1​t=1∑T​ln(1+Rt​).

Rˉ is the average one-period outcome. RG​ is the constant return that produces the same ending wealth. g is the same thing on the log scale, where it adds up over time. Wealth compounds, so RG​ and g, not Rˉ, describe what a buy-and-hold investor actually earned.

Deriving the drag

Apply (3.3) to each period and average:

g=T1​t∑​(Rt​−2Rt2​​+3Rt3​​−⋯)=Rˉ−21​⋅T1​t∑​Rt2​+31​⋅T1​t∑​Rt3​−⋯

The mean of squares needs no probability theory. With the population variance s2=T1​∑t​(Rt​−Rˉ)2, expanding the square gives the identity

T1​t=1∑T​Rt2​=s2+Rˉ2.(3.7)

Substituting and stopping at second order:

g≈Rˉ−2s2+Rˉ2​.(3.8)

Converting back with (3.2), RG​=eg−1≈g+2g2​, and g2≈Rˉ2 to this order, so the Rˉ2 terms cancel:

RG​≈Rˉ−2s2​.(3.9)

This is the familiar rule of thumb “geometric mean ≈ arithmetic mean − σ²/2”. The gap Rˉ−RG​≈s2/2 is called volatility drag. Equations (3.8) and (3.9) are not the same formula: (3.9) approximates the geometric simple return, while (3.8) approximates the log growth rate and keeps an extra −Rˉ2/2. Both drop third- and higher-order terms, so both are approximations.

ResultVolatility drag

To second order, the geometric mean return equals the arithmetic mean minus half the variance, RG​≈Rˉ−s2/2, and the log growth rate is g≈Rˉ−(s2+Rˉ2)/2. The neglected terms are of third order in the returns. They grow with the length of the period, with the size of the moves, and with skewness.

How accuracy depends on the measurement period

The approximation error depends on how large the per-period returns are, so it depends on the sampling frequency. Exhibit 5 measures it on the same 0050 history at five frequencies.

At daily frequency the approximation is exact to the displayed precision. At annual frequency, where single observations reach +48.7% and −21.3%, the error of (3.9) is 15 bp and that of (3.8) is 62 bp. The rule: second-order formulas are reliable when per-period standard deviations are a few percent, and need checking when they approach 20%.

Example 5Five years of 0050

0050's calendar-year total returns for 2020–2024 were 31.08%, 21.97%, −21.34%, 27.40% and 48.67%. Compute the arithmetic mean, the exact geometric mean and the approximation (3.9).

Solution

7Leveraged ETFs: Power, Drag and Residual

decompose the long-run return of a daily-reset leveraged ETF into a power of the index, a variance drag and a residual, and estimate each term from data

The daily-reset identity

A daily-reset fund with leverage L promises L times the index's daily simple return. Ignore costs for now and write the fund's daily return as LRt​. Its log return minus L times the index's log return is, by (3.3),

ln(1+LR)−Lln(1+R)=−2L2−L​R2+3L3−L​R3−4L4−L​R4+⋯(3.10)

The first-order terms cancel exactly — that is what “L× daily” buys you. What remains starts at second order. Summing over the T days of a holding period and writing G=ST​/S0​ for the index's growth factor and RV=∑t​Rt2​ for its realized variance:

V0​VT​​≈GLexp(−2L(L−1)​RV).(3.11)
ResultWhat a daily-reset fund delivers

Before costs, an L× daily fund returns approximately the L-th power of the index's growth factor, multiplied by a variance drag of exp(−2L(L−1)​RV). For L=2 the drag factor is e−RV; for L=3 it is e−3RV. The drag depends on the path only through realized variance (plus small third-order skewness terms).

This dissolves the paradox. “Twice the index's return” means 1+2(G−1). But for L>1 the power satisfies GL≥1+L(G−1), with equality only at G=1 (Bernoulli's inequality, itself a statement that xL lies above its tangent line at x=1). So the fund has two forces: compounding of the trend, GL versus 1+L(G−1), which favours the fund, and variance drag, which hurts it. Strong trends with low volatility favour the fund; choppy markets with high volatility punish it. Practice Problem 9 makes this precise: before costs, a 2× fund beats twice the index's simple return roughly when the trend ∣1−1/G∣ exceeds the realized volatility RV​.

Twelve years of 00631L

Exhibit 6 compares three quantities built from the same 2,902 days: 0050's total-return growth, the growth of an exact daily 2× of 0050 computed as ∏t​(1+2Rt​), and the Taylor formula G2e−RV evaluated along the path. 00631L's market price is plotted alongside.

Three conclusions follow. First, the second-order Taylor term reproduces the exact rebalancing effect to within 0.0001 log points over twelve years: (3.11) is not a rough rule. Second, the drag averaged 0.4473/11.94=3.75% a year, essentially 0050's realized annual variance (0.19312=3.73%). Third, 00631L still grew 44-fold against 10-fold for 0050, because G2=100 is a very large number: the trend term overwhelmed the drag.

The year-by-year view shows the two forces trading places.

  • 2017, a steady uptrend with the lowest variance in the sample, is the textbook case for the power term: an exact daily 2× of 0050 would have returned 38.3% against twice the index's 36.3%.
  • 2022 is the mirror image in a falling market: an exact daily 2× of 0050 would have lost 41.0%, less than twice 0050's 21.3% loss (42.7%). Compounding a downtrend also helps, because each day's 2× loss is taken on a smaller base. 00631L itself lost only 36.4%, helped by a positive residual.
  • 2015, a choppy, slightly negative year, is where drag wins: an exact daily 2× would have lost 14.5% and 00631L lost 16.5%, against twice the index's −12.6%.

What Taylor cannot see: the residual

The residual averaged −0.031 log points a year but swung from +0.117 (2021) to −0.267 (2024). It is not a constant fee. It contains the fund's management and trading costs, the financing embedded in its leverage, any gap between market price and NAV, and replication basis. 00631L builds most of its exposure with TAIEX futures rather than Taiwan 50 instruments. On 19 May 2026 it held TAIEX futures equal to 160.87% of net assets plus TSMC shares equal to 39.63%, while TSMC's weight was 43.7% in TAIEX and 60.9% in the Taiwan 50 index. When TSMC leads, as in 2024–2026, a futures-heavy fund lags twice the Taiwan 50; in years when the broader market does relatively better, the residual can turn positive. A model explains only the terms it contains: (3.11) prices the variance drag to the fourth decimal, and the residual needs its own model.

PitfallTwo true statements about leveraged ETFs

“Leveraged ETFs decay” is true relative to GL: for a given index growth G, more realized variance means less money. In fact, by Bernoulli's inequality 1+LRt​≤(1+Rt​)L every day, so before costs a daily L× fund can never beat GL. “Leveraged ETFs can beat L×” is true relative to 1+L(G−1), because of the convexity of GL. Arguments about leveraged funds usually go wrong because the two sides use different benchmarks. Neither statement says whether holding one is wise; that is a sizing question, answered by the Kelly analysis of LM42 and LM46.

The variance term in (3.11), −2L(L−1)​σ2 per unit time, will reappear in LM42 as the curvature of the Kelly growth curve g(f)=rf​+f(μ−rf​)−2f2σ2​. Leverage L and Kelly fraction f are the same variable seen from two sides.

8The Same Second-Order Idea Elsewhere

apply first- and second-order approximations to bond prices (duration and convexity), and recognise the same structure in option delta–gamma and in Itô's lemma

Bond prices: duration and convexity

A bond's price P(y) is a smooth, convex function of its yield. A second-order expansion about the current yield gives the standard approximation

PΔP​≈−Dmod​Δy+21​C(Δy)2,Dmod​:=−P1​dydP​,C:=P1​dy2d2P​.(3.12)

Modified duration is the slope term and convexity the curvature term: the same level–slope–curvature structure as Section 2.

At +300 bp the second-order error grows to 0.45 points: three times the shock gives roughly 25 times the error, close to the factor of 27 that an O(Δy3) remainder implies.

Options: delta and gamma

An option's value V(S,t) expanded to second order in the underlying price and first order in time gives the trader's daily P&L approximation

ΔV≈Δ⋅ΔS+21​Γ(ΔS)2+ΘΔt,

where Δ=∂V/∂S, Γ=∂2V/∂S2 and Θ=∂V/∂t. A delta-hedged book removes the first term and keeps the gamma term: it earns 21​Γ(ΔS)2 from realized moves and pays Θ for the privilege. LM31 develops this for TXO and warrants.

A preview of Itô's lemma

Apply a second-order expansion to lnS when the price follows a random walk with per-period return μΔt+σΔt​Z, with Z a standard random shock:

ΔlnS≈SΔS​−21​(SΔS​)2.

In ordinary calculus the squared term is negligible, because (Δx)2 is much smaller than Δx. Here it is not: (ΔS/S)2≈σ2Z2Δt is of the same order as the drift μΔt, and averages to σ2Δt. Keeping it yields

dlnS=(μ−2σ2​)dt+σdW.(3.13)

This is Itô's lemma for the logarithm, and the −σ2/2 in it is the volatility drag of Section 6. In discrete time the drag was an approximation; in continuous time it becomes exact, because every higher-order term vanishes as Δt→0. LM28 makes this rigorous, and LM29 uses it to solve geometric Brownian motion.

Deep DiveWhy (ΔW)2 behaves like Δtoptional · click to expand

Let ΔW=Δt​Z with Z standard normal. Then E[(ΔW)2]=Δt and Var[(ΔW)2]=Δt2Var(Z2)=2Δt2. Split [0,T] into n=T/Δt steps and add the squared increments: the sum has mean T and variance n⋅2Δt2=2TΔt, which goes to 0 as Δt→0. The sum of squared increments therefore converges to the deterministic number T: the quadratic variation of Brownian motion. A smooth path has quadratic variation 0, because its squared increments are of order Δt2. That single difference is why stochastic calculus keeps the second-order Taylor term and ordinary calculus discards it.

Knowledge Check 7

Why does a long-only bond portfolio with positive convexity gain from a large yield move in either direction, relative to the duration-only estimate?

Answer

The convexity term 21​C(Δy)2 is positive whatever the sign of Δy. Rates up: the loss is smaller than duration predicts. Rates down: the gain is larger. It is the bond-market analogue of a long-gamma option position.

9Taylor Approximations in Python

implement and verify Taylor approximations in Python

Two habits make approximations safe in code. First, compute the exact quantity alongside the approximation and report the gap. Second, prefer numerically stable library functions: np.log1p(x) and np.expm1(x) are accurate for tiny x, where np.log(1 + x) loses digits to rounding.

Python
import math
import numpy as np

def taylor_log1p(x, n):
    """Order-n Maclaurin polynomial of ln(1+x), eq. (3.3)."""
    return sum((-1) ** (k + 1) * x**k / k for k in range(1, n + 1))

def lagrange_bound_log1p(x, n):
    """Upper bound on |ln(1+x) - T_n(x)| from eq. (3.5)."""
    base = 1.0 if x >= 0 else 1.0 + x
    return abs(x) ** (n + 1) / ((n + 1) * base ** (n + 1))

for x in (-0.10, -0.0913, 0.05, 0.50):
    err = math.log1p(x) - taylor_log1p(x, 2)
    print(f"x={x:+.4f}  error={err:+.2e}  bound={lagrange_bound_log1p(x, 2):.2e}")

The decomposition of Section 7 takes a few vectorized lines for any index–fund pair with aligned daily simple returns:

Python
import numpy as np
import polars as pl

def leverage_decomposition(df: pl.DataFrame, idx: str, fund: str, L: float) -> dict:
    """Split a daily-reset fund's log growth into power, drag and residual (eqs. 3.10-3.11)."""
    R, RF = df[idx].to_numpy(), df[fund].to_numpy()
    power = L * np.log1p(R).sum()                               # ln G^L
    exact_drag = (np.log1p(L * R) - L * np.log1p(R)).sum()      # exact rebalancing effect
    taylor2 = -L * (L - 1) / 2 * (R**2).sum()                   # second-order term
    taylor3 = taylor2 + (L**3 - L) / 3 * (R**3).sum()           # plus third-order term
    actual = np.log1p(RF).sum()
    return {"power": power, "exact_drag": exact_drag, "taylor2": taylor2,
            "taylor3": taylor3, "residual": actual - power - exact_drag, "actual": actual}

df = pl.read_parquet("etf_daily_returns.parquet")
print(leverage_decomposition(df, "R_0050", "R_00631L", L=2))
# power 4.6097, exact_drag -0.44729, taylor2 -0.44735, taylor3 -0.44461, residual -0.3659, actual 3.7965

Summary

  • A Taylor polynomial (3.1) matches a function's value and first n derivatives at one point. Its coefficients are f(k)(a)/k! because differentiating (x−a)k k times produces k!.
  • The expansions of ex, ln(1+x) and (1+x)α generate most financial approximations. The series for ln(1+x) converges only on (−1,1]; for large moves, use the exact function.
  • The Lagrange remainder (3.5) makes every “≈” checkable: the error is the next term at an unknown point. For ln(1+x)≈x−x2/2 the error stays below 4 bp of log return for daily moves up to 10%, including the 2024 and 2025 crash days.
  • Log returns add across time; simple returns add across assets. The log return is smaller than the simple return by about R2/2.
  • Volatility drag: RG​≈Rˉ−s2/2 and g≈Rˉ−(s2+Rˉ2)/2. Both are second-order approximations whose error grows with the size of per-period moves.
  • A daily-reset L× fund earns approximately GLe−L(L−1)RV/2 before costs. On 0050 over 2014–2026 the Taylor term (−0.44735) matched the exact rebalancing effect (−0.44729). 00631L's further residual of −3.1% a year reflects costs and futures-based replication.
  • Duration–convexity, delta–gamma and Itô's lemma share the level–slope–curvature structure. In Itô's lemma the squared term survives because (ΔW)2 is of order Δt.
