Learning Module Overview
  • A Taylor polynomial is the best local polynomial model of a smooth function: it matches the function's value and its first n derivatives at one point.
  • Three expansions — ex, ln(1+x) and (1+x)α — generate most approximations used in finance. The series for ln(1+x) converges only for −1<x≤1.
  • The Lagrange remainder turns “≈” into a statement with an error bound. The error of an order-n approximation shrinks like ∣x∣n+1.
  • Log and simple returns differ by R2/2 to second order. Over a sample, geometric return ≈ arithmetic mean − half the variance: this is volatility drag, and it is an approximation, not an identity.
  • A daily-reset L× fund earns approximately the Lth power of the index's growth, taxed by exp(−2L(L−1)​∑Rt2​). On twelve years of 0050 data the second-order term is accurate to a few thousandths of a log point. Over the full sample it comes within 0.0001, helped by cancelling third- and fourth-order terms.
  • “Leveraged ETFs decay” and “leveraged ETFs beat L×” are both true, against different benchmarks. In strong trends a 2× fund can more than double the index's return.
  • Duration–convexity, delta–gamma and Itô's lemma are all second-order Taylor expansions. Itô's lemma is the one in which the second-order term does not vanish.

1Introduction

Almost every formula a trader uses daily is an approximation in disguise. Examples are “log return ≈ simple return”, “geometric mean ≈ arithmetic mean − σ²/2” and “bond price return ≈ −duration × yield change”. Each is the first or second term of a Taylor expansion. This module shows where they come from, how wrong they are and when they break, and then uses them on real money.

Motivating CaseTwo numbers that cannot both be true

From the close of 31 October 2014 to 8 October 2026, Yuanta Taiwan 50 (0050) returned +902% including dividends: one unit grew to 10.02. Over the same days the daily-reset 2× fund on that index, Yuanta Taiwan 50 Bull 2X (00631L), returned +4,354%: one unit grew to 44.54. “Twice the index's return” would have been +1,804%. The leveraged fund delivered about 2.4 times twice the index.

Then take calendar 2022 in the US. QQQ lost 32.6%; the 3× fund TQQQ lost 79.1%. Three times QQQ's loss is −97.7%, so TQQQ lost less than three times the index.

Yet every regulator and textbook warns that leveraged ETFs decay. How can a fund decay and beat its multiple at the same time? How large is the decay, and what sets its size? By Section 7 you will split each fund's return into three numbers: a power of the index, a variance drag and a residual.

Exhibit 1 collects the numbers.

The tool that resolves the paradox is a second-order Taylor expansion of ln(1+x). Sections 2–4 build the tool and its error bound. Sections 5–7 apply it to returns, means and leverage. Section 8 shows the same idea in bond pricing, option hedging and, as a preview of LM37, Itô's lemma.

2The Taylor Polynomial: A Local Model

construct the Taylor polynomial of order n of a smooth function about a point, and interpret its terms as level, slope and curvature

Tangent line, then curvature

Near a point a, a smooth function looks like a straight line: its tangent. The tangent line is the first-order model

f(x)≈f(a)+f′(a)(x−a).

For f(x)=ln(1+x) at a=0, f(0)=0 and f′(0)=1, so ln(1+x)≈x. That is the familiar “log return ≈ simple return”. It is exact at x=0 and drifts away as x grows, because ln(1+x) bends downward. A better model adds the bend, measured by the second derivative:

f(x)≈f(a)+f′(a)(x−a)+21​f′′(a)(x−a)2.

With f′′(x)=−1/(1+x)2 and f′′(0)=−1 this gives ln(1+x)≈x−x2/2. The −x2/2 term is the curvature correction, and it is the source of every “−σ2/2” in this book.

DefinitionTaylor polynomial

Let f have n derivatives at a. The Taylor polynomial of order n of f about a is

Tn​(x):=k=0∑n​k!f(k)(a)​(x−a)k=f(a)+f′(a)(x−a)+2!f′′(a)​(x−a)2+⋯+n!f(n)(a)​(x−a)n.(3.1)
When a=0 it is also called a Maclaurin polynomial. Letting n→∞ gives the Taylor series, when that series converges.

Why the coefficients are f(k)(a)/k!

Tn​ is built so that it agrees with f at a in value, slope, curvature and every higher derivative up to order n. Write a general polynomial h(x)=∑k=0n​ck​(x−a)k and differentiate it m times. Every term with k<m disappears, every term with k>m still contains a factor (x−a) and vanishes at x=a, and the term k=m becomes m!cm​. So

h(m)(a)=m!cm​.

Requiring h(m)(a)=f(m)(a) for m=0,…,n forces cm​=f(m)(a)/m!. The factorial is not decoration: it undoes the factorial that repeated differentiation of (x−a)m produces.

Each term has a job. The constant fixes the level, the linear term the slope, the quadratic term the curvature, and higher terms correct asymmetry and tails. In finance the terms through second order usually suffice, which is why “level, slope, curvature” recurs in duration–convexity, delta–gamma and the volatility drag.

Solution

Knowledge Check 1

In T2​(x)=f(a)+f′(a)(x−a)+21​f′′(a)(x−a)2, which coefficient would you change to make the approximation bend upward instead of downward, and what property of f does it encode?

Answer

The quadratic coefficient 21​f′′(a). Its sign is the sign of the curvature: f′′(a)>0 (convex) bends up, f′′(a)<0 (concave) bends down. ln(1+x) is concave, so its quadratic term is negative.

3Four Expansions That Run Finance

derive the expansions of ex, ln(1+x) and (1+x)α, and state where each one converges

Three Maclaurin series, plus the geometric series they all lean on, generate most approximations in this book.

The exponential. Every derivative of ex is ex, so f(k)(0)=1 for all k and

ex=k=0∑∞​k!xk​=1+x+2x2​+6x3​+⋯for all real x.(3.2)

The logarithm. Differentiate f(x)=ln(1+x) repeatedly: f′(x)=(1+x)−1, f′′(x)=−(1+x)−2, f′′′(x)=2(1+x)−3, and in general f(k)(x)=(−1)k+1(k−1)!(1+x)−k. At 0 this gives f(k)(0)/k!=(−1)k+1/k, so

ln(1+x)=k=1∑∞​k(−1)k+1xk​=x−2x2​+3x3​−4x4​+⋯for −1<x≤1.(3.3)

A second derivation explains the range. For ∣t∣<1 the geometric series gives 1+t1​=1−t+t2−t3+⋯. Integrating from 0 to x term by term yields (3.3) for ∣x∣<1. At x=1 the alternating series still converges, to ln2 (by Abel's theorem), which is why the range is −1<x≤1.

The binomial series. For any real exponent α, f(x)=(1+x)α has f(k)(0)=α(α−1)⋯(α−k+1), so

(1+x)α=k=0∑∞​(kα​)xk=1+αx+2α(α−1)​x2+⋯for ∣x∣<1,(3.4)

where (kα​)=α(α−1)⋯(α−k+1)/k!. With α=−1 it is the alternating geometric series 1/(1+x)=1−x+x2−⋯. With α=21​ it gives 1+x​≈1+2x​−8x2​. When α is a positive integer the series stops and (3.4) is the ordinary binomial theorem.

Exhibit 2 collects the four series with their ranges and typical uses.

The radius of convergence has a financial meaning

The series (3.3) diverges for x>1 and the function itself does not exist at x=−1. Both edges matter. At x=−1, a return of −100%, the log of wealth is −∞: that is ruin. It is why log-utility investors such as Kelly bettors (LM41) never accept a position that can lose everything.

For x>1, ln(1+x) is perfectly finite, but no number of series terms will reach it. A truncated series is a local tool; for large moves, compute the exact function.

PitfallSeries approximations for large returns

For a +150% year, x−x2/2=1.5−1.125=0.375, while ln2.5=0.916. Adding terms makes it worse, because the series diverges for x>1. Use ln(1+R) itself whenever ∣R∣ is not small. A rough working limit for the second-order formula is ∣R∣≤0.1, where its error stays below 4 bp of log return (Section 4).

4How Good Is “≈”? The Remainder Term

bound the truncation error with the Lagrange remainder, and decide when a truncated expansion can be trusted

An approximation without an error estimate is a guess. Taylor's theorem supplies the estimate.

TheoremTaylor's theorem with Lagrange remainder

Let f have n+1 continuous derivatives on an interval containing a and x. Then

f(x)=Tn​(x)+En​(x),En​(x)=(n+1)!f(n+1)(ξ)​(x−a)n+1(3.5)
for some ξ strictly between a and x.

Read (3.5) as: the error is the next term of the series, evaluated at an unknown point ξ. We rarely know ξ, but we usually know how large f(n+1) can be on the interval, and that bounds the error. In big-O notation we write En​(x)=O(∣x−a∣n+1): halve the distance to a and an order-n error shrinks by a factor of about 2n+1.

The error of “log return ≈ R−R2/2”

For f(x)=ln(1+x), f′′′(ξ)=2/(1+ξ)3, so with n=2 and a=0

ln(1+x)=x−2x2​+3(1+ξ)3x3​.

For x>0, ξ∈(0,x) gives 1+ξ>1, and the error lies in (0, x3/3). For −1<x<0, ξ∈(x,0) gives 1+ξ>1+x, and the error is negative with size at most ∣x∣3/(3(1+x)3). Losses are approximated less accurately than gains of the same size, because ln(1+x) bends harder on the left. Exhibit 3 compares the actual errors with these bounds.

5Simple Returns and Log Returns

relate simple and log returns, and arithmetic and geometric means, through second-order expansions, and quantify volatility drag

A holding-period (simple) return and a log return describe the same price move:

Rt​=Pt−1​Pt​+Dt​​−1,rt​=ln(1+Rt​).

From (3.3) and (3.2), each is a series in the other:

r=R−2R2​+3R3​−⋯,R=er−1=r+2r2​+6r3​+⋯.(3.6)

Because ln(1+x) is concave, it lies below its tangent line at the origin, which is x itself. Hence ln(1+x)≤x for every x>−1, with equality only at 0 (Practice Problem 5 proves it with (3.5)). So the log return is always the smaller number, by about R2/2 (Exhibit 4).

Two aggregation rules, two return types

Each definition is additive along a different dimension, which is why both are needed.

  • Across time, log returns add. Over T periods the growth factor is G=∏t​(1+Rt​), so lnG=∑t=1T​rt​, because the log of a product is a sum. Simple returns compound instead: 1+R0→T​=G.
  • Across assets, simple returns add. A portfolio with weights wj​ earns Rp​=∑j​wj​Rj​ exactly. Log returns do not: rp​=ln(∑j​wj​erj​)=∑j​wj​rj​.

The working rule follows. Use log returns for research along the time axis — trends, volatility, cross-asset comparisons. Use simple returns whenever money is aggregated across positions — portfolio accounting, settlement, drawdown in currency.

The gap in real data

The sample is the 2,902 days on which both 0050 and 00631L traded, from November 2014 to October 2026. Each fund was halted for a few sessions around its split, so two of these returns span several sessions.

Over these days, 0050's mean daily simple return was 0.0871% and its mean daily log return 0.0794%. The difference, 0.0077% (0.77 bp) a day, is what (3.6) predicts. Half the mean squared return is 0.771 bp, and half the variance is 0.767 bp. Summed over the sample's A=243.1 returns a year, this “small” daily gap is roughly 1.9 percentage points of annual return.

6Arithmetic Mean, Geometric Mean and Volatility Drag

relate simple and log returns, and arithmetic and geometric means, through second-order expansions, and quantify volatility drag

Given returns R1​,…,RT​, three averages answer three different questions.

Rˉ=T1​t=1∑T​Rt​,RG​=(t=1∏T​(1+Rt​))1/T−1,g=ln(1+RG​)=T1​t=1∑T​ln(1+Rt​).

Rˉ is the average one-period outcome. RG​ is the constant return that produces the same ending wealth. g is the same thing on the log scale, where it adds up over time. Wealth compounds, so RG​ and g, not Rˉ, describe what a buy-and-hold investor actually earned.

Deriving the drag

Apply (3.3) to each period and average:

g=T1​t∑​(Rt​−2Rt2​​+3Rt3​​−⋯)=Rˉ−21​⋅T1​t∑​Rt2​+31​⋅T1​t∑​Rt3​−⋯

The mean of squares needs no probability theory. With the population variance s2=T1​∑t​(Rt​−Rˉ)2, expanding the square gives the identity

T1​t=1∑T​Rt2​=s2+Rˉ2.(3.7)

Substituting and stopping at second order:

g≈Rˉ−2s2+Rˉ2​.(3.8)

Converting back with (3.2), RG​=eg−1≈g+2g2​, and g2≈Rˉ2 to this order, so the Rˉ2 terms cancel:

RG​≈Rˉ−2s2​.(3.9)

This is the familiar rule of thumb “geometric mean ≈ arithmetic mean − σ²/2”. The gap Rˉ−RG​≈s2/2 is called volatility drag. Equations (3.8) and (3.9) differ: (3.9) approximates the geometric simple return, while (3.8) approximates the log growth rate and keeps an extra −Rˉ2/2. Both drop third- and higher-order terms, so both are approximations.

ResultVolatility drag

To second order, the geometric mean return equals the arithmetic mean minus half the variance, RG​≈Rˉ−s2/2, and the log growth rate is g≈Rˉ−(s2+Rˉ2)/2. The neglected terms are of third order in the returns. They grow with the length of the period and the size of the moves. They depend on the mean of the returns and on their skewness, the asymmetry of moves around the mean (LM19).

How accuracy depends on the measurement period

The approximation error depends on how large the per-period returns are, so it depends on the sampling frequency. Exhibit 5 measures it on the same 0050 history at five frequencies.

At daily frequency the approximation is exact to the displayed precision. At annual frequency, where single observations reach +48.7% and −21.3%, the error of (3.9) is 15 bp and that of (3.8) is 62 bp. The rule: second-order formulas are reliable when per-period standard deviations are a few percent, and need checking when they approach 20%.

7Leveraged ETFs: Power, Drag and Residual

decompose the long-run return of a daily-reset leveraged ETF into a power of the index, a variance drag and a residual, and estimate each term from data

The daily-reset identity

A daily-reset fund with leverage L promises L times the index's daily simple return. Ignore costs for now and write the fund's daily return as LRt​. Its log return minus L times the index's log return is, by (3.3),

ln(1+LR)−Lln(1+R)=−2L2−L​R2+3L3−L​R3−4L4−L​R4+⋯(3.10)

The series converges when ∣LR∣<1 and ∣R∣<1, which daily moves satisfy by a wide margin. The first-order terms cancel exactly — that is what “L× daily” buys you. What remains starts at second order.

Now sum over the T days of a holding period and keep terms to second order. With G the index's growth factor, Gfund​ the fund's and RV=∑t​Rt2​ the index's realized variance, this gives

Gfund​≈GLexp(−2L(L−1)​RV).(3.11)
ResultWhat a daily-reset fund delivers

Before costs, an L× daily fund returns approximately the Lth power of the index's growth factor, multiplied by a variance drag of exp(−2L(L−1)​RV). For L=2 the drag factor is e−RV; for L=3 it is e−3RV. The drag depends on the path only through realized variance, plus small third-order terms in ∑t​Rt3​. Those terms reflect both the trend and the skewness of daily moves.

This dissolves the paradox. “Twice the index's return” means 1+2(G−1), but for L>1 the power satisfies GL≥1+L(G−1), with equality only at G=1. This is Bernoulli's inequality, itself a statement that xL lies above its tangent line at x=1. So the fund has two forces: compounding of the trend, GL versus 1+L(G−1), which favours the fund, and variance drag, which hurts it.

Strong trends with low volatility favour the fund; choppy markets with high volatility punish it. Practice Problem 9 makes this precise. Before costs, a 2× fund beats twice the index's simple return roughly when the trend ∣1−1/G∣ exceeds the realized volatility RV​.

What Taylor cannot see: the residual

Over the whole sample the residual averaged −0.3659/11.94=−0.031 log points a year, about −3.0% of value. It is not a constant fee.

The residual contains management and trading costs, the financing embedded in the leverage, any gap between market price and NAV, and replication basis. 00631L builds most of its exposure with TAIEX futures rather than Taiwan 50 instruments. A model explains only the terms it contains. Equation (3.11) prices the variance drag to within a few thousandths of a log point; the residual needs its own model.

PitfallTwo true statements about leveraged ETFs

“Leveraged ETFs decay” is true relative to GL: for a given index growth G, more realized variance means less money. In fact, by Bernoulli's inequality 1+LRt​≤(1+Rt​)L every day, so before costs a daily L× fund can never beat GL. “Leveraged ETFs can beat L×” is true relative to 1+L(G−1), because of the convexity of GL.

Arguments about leveraged funds usually go wrong because the two sides use different benchmarks. Neither statement says whether holding one is wise; that is a sizing question, answered by the Kelly analysis of LM42 and LM46.

The variance term in (3.11), −2L(L−1)​σ2 per unit time, will reappear in LM42 as the curvature of the Kelly growth curve. A position held at a constant multiple of equity and rebalanced continuously gives up 21​σ2 times the square of that multiple in log growth. A fund's leverage and a Kelly investor's exposure are the same variable seen from two sides.

8The Same Second-Order Idea Elsewhere

Bond prices: duration and convexity

A bond's price P(y) is a smooth, convex function of its yield. A second-order expansion about the current yield gives the standard approximation

PΔP​≈−Dmod​Δy+21​C(Δy)2,Dmod​:=−P1​dydP​,C:=P1​dy2d2P​.(3.12)

Modified duration is the slope term and convexity the curvature term: the same level–slope–curvature structure as Section 2.

Options: delta and gamma

An option's value V(S,t) expanded to second order in the underlying price and first order in time gives the trader's daily P&L approximation

ΔV≈Δ⋅ΔS+21​Γ(ΔS)2+ΘΔt,

where Δ=∂V/∂S, Γ=∂2V/∂S2 and Θ=∂V/∂t. A delta-hedged book removes the first term and keeps the gamma term: it earns 21​Γ(ΔS)2 from realized moves and pays Θ for the privilege. LM40 develops this for TXO and warrants.

A preview of Itô's lemma

Apply a second-order expansion to lnS when the price follows a random walk with per-period return μΔt+σΔt​Z, with Z a standard random shock:

ΔlnS≈SΔS​−21​(SΔS​)2.

In ordinary calculus the squared term is negligible, because (Δx)2 is much smaller than Δx. Here it is not: (ΔS/S)2≈σ2Z2Δt is of the same order as the drift μΔt, and averages to σ2Δt. As Δt→0 the shocks Δt​Z become the increments dB of a standard Brownian motion Bt​ (LM36), and keeping the squared term yields

dlnS=(μ−2σ2​)dt+σdB.(3.13)

This is Itô's lemma for the logarithm, and the −σ2/2 in it is the volatility drag of Section 6. In discrete time the drag was an approximation; in continuous time it becomes exact, because every higher-order term vanishes as Δt→0. LM37 makes this rigorous, and LM38 uses it to solve geometric Brownian motion.

Deep DiveWhy (ΔB)2 behaves like Δtoptional · click to expand

Here τ is a horizon in years and N a number of steps. Let ΔB=Δt​Z with Z standard normal. Then E[(ΔB)2]=Δt and Var[(ΔB)2]=Δt2Var(Z2)=2Δt2. Split [0,τ] into N=τ/Δt steps and add the squared increments: the sum has mean τ and variance N⋅2Δt2=2τΔt, which goes to 0 as Δt→0.

The sum of squared increments therefore converges to the deterministic number τ: the quadratic variation of Brownian motion. A smooth path has quadratic variation 0, because its squared increments are of order Δt2. That single difference is why stochastic calculus keeps the second-order Taylor term and ordinary calculus discards it.

9Taylor Approximations in Python

implement and verify Taylor approximations in Python

Two habits make approximations safe in code. First, compute the exact quantity alongside the approximation and report the gap. Second, prefer numerically stable library functions: np.log1p(x) and np.expm1(x) are accurate for tiny x, where np.log(1 + x) loses digits to rounding.

Python
import math

def taylor_log1p(x, n):
    """Order-n Maclaurin polynomial of ln(1+x), eq. (3.3)."""
    return sum((-1) ** (k + 1) * x**k / k for k in range(1, n + 1))

def lagrange_bound_log1p(x, n):
    """Upper bound on |ln(1+x) - T_n(x)| from eq. (3.5)."""
    base = 1.0 if x >= 0 else 1.0 + x
    return abs(x) ** (n + 1) / ((n + 1) * base ** (n + 1))

for x in (-0.10, -0.0913, 0.05, 0.50):
    err = math.log1p(x) - taylor_log1p(x, 2)
    bound = lagrange_bound_log1p(x, 2)
    print(f"x={x:+.4f}  error={err:+.2e}  bound={bound:.2e}")

The decomposition of Section 7 takes a few vectorized lines for any index–fund pair with aligned daily simple returns:

Python
import numpy as np

def leverage_decomposition(R, RF, L):
    """Split a daily-reset fund's log growth into power, drag and residual
    (eqs. 3.10-3.11). R, RF: aligned daily simple returns of the index and
    the fund, as NumPy arrays."""
    power = L * np.log1p(R).sum()                           # ln G^L
    exact_drag = (np.log1p(L * R) - L * np.log1p(R)).sum()  # exact effect
    taylor2 = -L * (L - 1) / 2 * (R**2).sum()               # to 2nd order
    taylor3 = taylor2 + (L**3 - L) / 3 * (R**3).sum()       # to 3rd order
    taylor4 = taylor3 - (L**4 - L) / 4 * (R**4).sum()       # to 4th order
    actual = np.log1p(RF).sum()
    return {"power": power, "exact_drag": exact_drag,
            "taylor2": taylor2, "taylor3": taylor3, "taylor4": taylor4,
            "residual": actual - power - exact_drag, "actual": actual}

# ret: the lab's aligned daily returns, built in its Part 2
# from the files in its data/ folder
R, RF = ret["R_0050"].to_numpy(), ret["R_00631L"].to_numpy()
d = leverage_decomposition(R, RF, L=2)
print({name: round(float(v), 5) for name, v in d.items()})

  • Log returns add across time; simple returns add across assets. The log return is smaller than the simple return by about R2/2.
