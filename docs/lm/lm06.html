Learning Module Overview
  • Every annuity, level-payment loan, bond and dividend-discount formula in this module is the sum of a geometric series, a1​(1−xn)/(1−x). A discount factor sits in the ratio. An infinite series converges only when ∣x∣<1, which is why the Gordon model needs γ<k.
  • An annuity due is worth exactly 1+i times the matching ordinary annuity. At a 2% discount rate a perpetuity collects only 45% of its value in its first 30 years; at 8% it collects 90%.
  • The Gordon model reads a price as an implied return, k=D1​/P0​+γ. On 8 October 2026 0050's trailing yield was 1.39%. With its 10-year dividend growth of 8.43% the implied required return is 9.94%, and two thirds of the model value lies beyond year 30.
  • A bond's price falls strictly as its yield rises, so every price has exactly one yield to maturity. Bisection halves the bracket around it at each step and pins it down to 10−8 in 22 halvings.
  • In a level-payment loan the principal portions grow geometrically at 1+i and the balance equals the present value of the payments still due. On an illustrative NT$10 million, 30-year mortgage at 2.2%, equal-principal repayment pays NT$360,064 less interest (undiscounted) and a 3-year grace period NT$261,984 more. At the loan rate every schedule is worth exactly the principal.
  • No-arbitrage turns spot rates into forward rates, interest differentials into forward exchange rates, and the index into a futures fair value F=Se(rf​−q)τ.
  • On 27 June 2025, the most extreme session of the sample, the December TX contract settled 880 points below TAIEX. It implied dividends of qτ=4.92% before expiry; those that actually went ex came to 1.87%. Dividends explain the summer shape of the discount. Carry (+215 points) and dividends (−422) leave a residual of −673 (−461 on average over 23 June–3 July) that frictionless arbitrage would have removed.

1Introduction

A payment has a date as well as an amount: NT$1 next year is worth less than NT$1 today, because today's money earns interest. Discounting at a constant rate multiplies each payment by the same factor per period, so level or steadily growing payments form a geometric series. The sum of that series prices deposits, mortgages, bonds and dividend streams. Add one more idea, no-arbitrage, and futures can be priced too.

Motivating CaseWhy does TX trade below TAIEX every summer?

At the close on Friday 27 June 2025, TAIEX stood at 22,580.08. Every TX futures contract settled below it: July by 458 points, December by 880 and June 2026 by 1,087 (Exhibit 1). For the December contract this was the deepest discount of the 146 sessions from April to October.

Over June the near-month contract averaged 192 points below the index, against 65 points in April. By September its discount (逆價差) had vanished on average (+0.5 points), and in October TX traded above TAIEX on average (+67 points).

Interest says the opposite. A futures buyer keeps in the bank the cash a stock buyer must spend. With the central bank's discount rate at 2%, futures should sit above the index unless dividends going ex before expiry outweigh the interest.

The cost-of-carry formula F=Se(rf​−q)τ turns each price into the dividend yield q the market must expect. At rf​=2% the December contract implied dividends of qτ=4.92% before its expiry on 17 December. The dividends that went ex in the whole of calendar 2025 came to 2.93%.

Is the discount a dividend forecast, a bearish bet or a mispricing? Section 8 splits each contract's discount into carry, dividends and a residual, measured with TWSE's total-return index. It checks how much rests on one extreme day.

Sections 2–6 build the geometric sum into annuities, perpetuities, the Gordon model, bonds and loans. Section 7 adds no-arbitrage and the futures fair value used above; Section 8 resolves the case, and Section 9 collects the code.

2Sequences and Geometric Series

derive the sums of arithmetic and geometric sequences, and state when an infinite geometric series converges

A deposit earning simple interest grows by the same amount each year; one earning compound interest grows by the same factor. The first is an arithmetic sequence, the second a geometric one.

DefinitionArithmetic and geometric sequences

A sequence a1​,a2​,a3​,… is arithmetic with common difference d if aj+1​=aj​+d, so that aj​=a1​+(j−1)d. It is geometric with common ratio x if aj+1​=xaj​, so that aj​=a1​xj−1. A series is the sum of the terms of a sequence.

NT$100 at 2% simple interest is worth 100, 102, 104, … after 0, 1, 2, … years: an arithmetic sequence with d=2. At 2% compound interest it is worth 100, 102, 104.04, …: a geometric sequence with x=1.02. The discount factors v,v2,v3,… that convert future money into present money are geometric with ratio v=1/(1+i)<1.

Summing an arithmetic sequence

Write the sum forwards and backwards and add the two lines term by term:

Sn​Sn​​=a1​+a2​+⋯+an​,=an​+an−1​+⋯+a1​.​

Each pair aj​+an+1−j​=2a1​+(n−1)d=a1​+an​ has the same sum, and there are n pairs, so

j=1∑n​aj​=2n(a1​+an​)​.(6.1)

Section 6 uses (6.1) for equal-principal loans, whose payments fall arithmetically.

Summing a geometric sequence

Let Sn​=a1​+a1​x+⋯+a1​xn−1. Multiplying by x shifts every term one place, xSn​=a1​x+⋯+a1​xn, and subtracting cancels all but two terms: Sn​−xSn​=a1​−a1​xn.

ResultGeometric sums

For x=1,

j=0∑n−1​a1​xj=a1​1−x1−xn​,(6.2)
and for x=1 the sum is na1​. If ∣x∣<1, the infinite series converges:
j=0∑∞​a1​xj=1−xa1​​.(6.3)
If ∣x∣≥1 and a1​=0, the infinite series diverges.

Convergence follows from the gap between the full sum and a partial sum,

1−xa1​​−Sn​=1−xa1​xn​.

For ∣x∣<1, ∣x∣n=enln∣x∣→0 because ln∣x∣<0 (LM1), so the gap closes. For ∣x∣≥1 the terms a1​xj do not shrink to zero. A series whose terms do not vanish cannot settle on a finite sum (Stewart, Clegg and Watson 2021).

By the gap formula, the fraction of an infinite geometric sum beyond its first n terms is exactly xn. For x=v this is the share of a perpetuity's value paid after year n.

Example 1How much of a perpetuity is paid in the first 30 years?

A security pays NT$1 at the end of every year forever. At discount rates of 2%, 5% and 8%, compute its value and the share of it paid within 10, 30 and 50 years.

Solution

The payments form a geometric series with first term v and ratio v. By (6.3) the value is v/(1−v)=1/i: NT$50.00 at 2%, NT$20.00 at 5% and NT$12.50 at 8%. By the gap formula the share paid after year n is vn, so the share paid within n years is 1−vn:

Rate Value Within 10 years Within 30 years Within 50 years
2% 50.00 18.0% 44.8% 62.8%
5% 20.00 38.6% 76.9% 91.3%
8% 12.50 53.7% 90.1% 97.9%

At 8%, 90% of the value arrives within 30 years; at 2%, less than half does. Low discount rates make distant cash flows matter, so valuations at low rates hinge on assumptions about the far future (Section 4).

Every time-value problem is a recurrence

An account that earns i per period and receives a net cash flow CFt​ at the end of period t evolves as Bt​=(1+i)Bt−1​+CFt​. Deposits have CFt​>0; withdrawals and loan payments have CFt​<0. Unrolling the recursion from B0​ gives

Bn​=(1+i)nB0​+t=1∑n​(1+i)n−tCFt​,(6.4)

as substituting (6.4) for Bn−1​ into the recursion confirms. Level cash flows make the sum geometric with ratio 1+i; flows growing at rate γ make it geometric with ratio (1+γ)/(1+i) after factoring.

3Present Value, Future Value and Annuities

calculate present and future values of single sums, ordinary annuities and annuities due, deriving each formula from the geometric series

One period at rate i turns PV into (1+i)PV, and n periods compound to

FVn​=PV(1+i)n,PV=FVn​(1+i)−n=FVn​vn.

The rate must match the period; LM1 converts quoted annual rates into periodic and effective rates.

Discounting multiplies each cash flow by a number, so a stream's present value is the sum of its parts' present values, PV=∑t​CFt​vt. This cash-flow additivity lets us cut a complicated stream into simple pieces, value each piece and add. Section 7 turns the same principle into no-arbitrage pricing.

Ordinary annuities

An ordinary annuity pays PMT at the end of each of n periods, at t=1,…,n. Its present value is a geometric series with first term PMTv and ratio v, so by (6.2)

PV=PMTt=1∑n​vt=PMTv1−v1−vn​.

Since 1−v=i/(1+i)=iv, the factor v/(1−v) equals 1/i, and

PVord​=PMTi1−(1+i)−n​.(6.5)

Compounding (6.5) forward n periods gives the value on the date of the last payment:

FVord​=PMTi(1+i)n−1​.(6.6)

We write an,i​ and sn,i​ for the two fractions, the annuity factors.

Annuities due

An annuity due pays at the start of each period, at t=0,…,n−1 (Exhibit 2). Every payment arrives one period earlier than in the ordinary annuity, so every value is multiplied by 1+i:

PVdue​=(1+i)PVord​,FVdue​=(1+i)FVord​.(6.7)

Rent, insurance premiums and leases are usually paid in advance (annuities due); loan payments and bond coupons are paid in arrears (ordinary annuities).

Solving for the payment, the term or the rate

Each of (6.5)–(6.7) links a value, the payment PMT, the rate i, the term n and the timing convention. Given all but one, the last follows. The payment enters linearly. The term solves with logarithms: from (6.5), vn=1−PVi/PMT, so

n=−ln(1+i)ln(1−PVi/PMT)​,

which requires PMT>PVi.

A payment no larger than the interest PVi never reduces the balance, so the payments can continue forever (Section 4). The rate has no closed form in general; Section 5 finds it numerically.

Example 2A down payment and a retirement pot

(a) A couple wants NT$3,000,000 for a down payment in 8 years. Their account pays 2.4% a year, credited monthly at 0.2% (illustrative). What deposit at the end of each month reaches the target?

(b) A retiree holds NT$10,000,000 in an account paying 0.25% a month (3% a year, illustrative), withdrawing NT$60,000 at the end of each month. How long does the money last?

Solution

(a) Solve (6.6) for PMT with i=0.002 and n=96: PMT=3,000,000×0.002/(1.00296−1)= NT$28,377.08. The couple deposits NT$2,724,200 in total, and interest supplies the remaining NT$275,800. Depositing at the start of each month instead, an annuity due, divides the deposit by 1.002, to NT$28,320.44.

(b) Here PVi/PMT=10,000,000×0.0025/60,000=0.41667, so n=−ln(0.58333)/ln(1.0025)=215.87 months, about 18.0 years. Without interest the money would last 10,000,000/60,000=166.7 months (13.9 years). A withdrawal of NT$25,000, equal to the monthly interest, would last forever.

PitfallTiming and period mismatches

Two errors account for most wrong annuity answers. The first is timing: an,i​ assumes the first payment one period from now, so payments in advance need the extra factor 1+i. The second is the period: monthly payments need a monthly rate and a count of months. Under the nominal-rate convention, 2.4% a year over eight years means i=0.2% and n=96, not i=2.4% and n=8.

導讀一條等比級數，走遍所有現金流

折現就是每過一期乘上 v=1/(1+i)，所以一串固定金額的現金流，現值必然是等比級數：首項 PMTv、公比 v，套用 (6.2) 就得到年金公式。期初年金（房租、保費）只是把整串現金流往前移一期，所以剛好乘上 1+i。級數的「尾巴」xn 告訴你第 n 期之後還藏著多少價值：折現率 8% 時，永續年金九成的價值在 30 年內就收完；折現率 2% 時，30 年只收到 45%。利率越低，越遙遠的未來越重要。

4Perpetuities, Growth and the Gordon Model

value perpetuities, growing annuities and growing perpetuities, and calculate a stock's implied required return and implied growth rate with the Gordon growth model

Let the number of payments grow without bound, or let the payments themselves grow. Both limits are geometric series, and together they value preferred shares, common stocks and the terminal stage of any long-lived cash flow.

Perpetuities

As n→∞ in (6.5), (1+i)−n→0 and the value of a perpetuity appears:

PVperp​=iPMT​.(6.8)

A perpetual preferred share paying NT$2.40 a year is worth NT$60.00 at a required return of 4%. If the first payment comes at t=m+1 instead of t=1, the stream is worth PMT/i at t=m and vmPMT/i today. A first dividend in six years (m=5) gives NT$49.32. A perpetuity due, which also pays today, is worth (1+i)PMT/i= NT$62.40.

Growing annuities and perpetuities

Let the payment at t be PMT1​(1+γ)t−1, growing at rate γ per period. Factoring out PMT1​/(1+i),

PV=t=1∑n​(1+i)tPMT1​(1+γ)t−1​=1+iPMT1​​t=0∑n−1​(1+i1+γ​)t,

a geometric series with ratio x=(1+γ)/(1+i). Since 1−x=(i−γ)/(1+i), (6.2) gives

PVgrow​=i−γPMT1​​[1−(1+i1+γ​)n],γ=i.(6.9)

When γ=i every term equals PMT1​/(1+i) and the sum is nPMT1​/(1+i). As n→∞, (6.3) applies if and only if x<1, that is γ<i:

PVgrow perp​=i−γPMT1​​,γ<i.(6.10)

If γ≥i the series diverges: payments that grow at least as fast as the discount rate have no finite value. As a check, PMT1​=1, i=5%, γ=3% and n=20 give 15.9648, both by (6.9) and by summing the 20 terms. With γ=i the value is 20/1.05=19.0476.

The Gordon growth model

A share entitles its holder to dividends D1​,D2​,…. If they grow at a constant rate γ forever and investors require a return k>γ, (6.10) prices the share:

P0​=k−γD1​​=k−γD0​(1+γ)​.(6.11)

This is the Gordon growth model (Gordon 1959). Read backwards, a market price implies a required return or a growth rate. Solving (6.11) for k gives the implied required return, dividend yield plus growth:

k=P0​D1​​+γ.(6.12)

Solving instead for γ, kP0​−γP0​=D0​+γD0​ gives the implied growth rate

γ=P0​+D0​kP0​−D0​​.(6.13)

Dividing (6.11) by next year's earnings E1​ gives the justified forward P/E, P0​/E1​=(D1​/E1​)/(k−γ): the payout ratio divided by k−γ. A 40% payout with k=9% and γ=4% justifies a forward P/E of 8.0.

Changing growth

A company may grow fast for some years and then mature. A two-stage model values the first N dividends with (6.9) and the rest, growing at γ2​, with (6.11) applied at t=N:

P0​=t=1∑N​(1+k)tDt​​+(1+k)N1​⋅k−γ2​DN+1​​.

A company has just paid a dividend of NT$10. Analysts expect 12% growth for five years and 4% a year thereafter; the required return is 9% (illustrative). Value the share.

Example 4The required return priced into 0050

On 8 October 2026, 0050 closed at NT$114.95 with trailing cash dividends of NT$1.60, after a decade of 8.43% annual dividend growth. (a) What required return does the price imply if that growth continues? (b) What growth does it imply at a required return of 8%? (c) What share of the Gordon value comes from dividends paid after year 30?

Solution

(a) By (6.12), k=1.60×1.0843/114.95+0.0843=1.51%+8.43%=9.94%.

(b) By (6.13), γ=(0.08×114.95−1.60)/(114.95+1.60)=7.596/116.55=6.52%.

(c) The share after year n is xn with x=(1+γ)/(1+k): (1.0843/1.0994)30=0.66. Two thirds of the value lies beyond year 30, against 24% for a stock with k=9% and γ=4%.

The model is fragile here. Holding D1​ fixed, lowering k−γ by half a percentage point raises the model price from NT$114.95 to NT$171.90. Raising it by half a point lowers it to NT$86.34.

Between 2016 and 2026 the yield fell from 3.98% to 1.39%. At a constant 10% required return the implied growth rose from 5.79% to 8.49% (Exhibit 3). Either investors came to expect faster dividend growth or they accepted a lower return: the model identifies only k−γ=D1​/P0​, never its two parts.

PitfallGordon near its edge

Formula (6.11) sums a geometric series with ratio (1+γ)/(1+k), so as γ approaches k the price becomes hypersensitive to both inputs. Report a range of (k,γ) pairs, not a point. Never use price growth as γ: 0050's price grew 20.43% a year over the decade, and any γ≥k leaves the sum infinite. Put D1​=D0​(1+γ), not D0​, in the numerator, or the price is understated by the factor 1+γ.

高登模型 P0​=D1​/(k−γ) 說：預期股利殖利率 D1​/P0​ 剛好等於「要求報酬率減成長率」。0050 在 2026/10/8 的近 12 個月股利殖利率 D0​/P0​ 只有 1.39%，換成下一年的股利，D1​/P0​=k−γ 也只有 1.51%，代表市場定價隱含的 k 與 γ 非常接近：若股利維持過去十年 8.43% 的成長，隱含要求報酬率約 9.94%。但 k−γ 很小時，公比 (1+γ)/(1+k) 接近 1，級數收斂極慢，30 年後的股利占了價值的三分之二；k−γ 只要變動 0.5 個百分點，模型價格就從 114.95 跳到 171.90，或掉到 86.34。模型只能告訴你兩者的「差」，分不出是成長預期變高，還是要求報酬變低。

5Bond Pricing and Yield to Maturity

price zero-coupon, coupon and amortizing bonds, and solve for yield to maturity and internal rate of return numerically

A bond's price is the present value of its promised cash flows, so bond pricing is annuity pricing. The only new problem is solving for the rate.

Three kinds of bond

A zero-coupon bond pays only its face value M at maturity, so its price is P=M(1+y)−n: it is a discount instrument. A coupon bond pays a coupon C each period (coupon rate C/M) and M at maturity: an ordinary annuity plus a single sum,

P=Cy1−(1+y)−n​+M(1+y)−n.(6.14)

An amortizing bond pays a level amount PMT that covers interest and repays principal over its life. It is worth PMTan,y​: the loan of Section 6 seen from the lender's side.

Rearranging (6.14) shows when a bond trades above par. Because yMan,y​+M(1+y)−n=M, a bond whose coupon rate equals its yield is worth exactly par, and subtracting gives

Given a market price, the yield to maturity solves P(y)=Pmkt​, a polynomial equation of degree n in 1/(1+y). It has no closed-form solution in general but is well behaved. Every term of P(y) falls as y rises, so P is continuous and strictly decreasing. It falls from ∑t​CFt​ at y=0 towards 0 as y→∞, so any price in between has exactly one positive yield.

Bisection exploits this monotonicity. Start with a bracket [ylo​,yhi​] on which P(y)−Pmkt​ changes sign, and repeatedly keep the half whose ends still differ in sign. Each step halves the bracket and with it the bound on the error.

The error itself need not fall: it rises from step 3 to step 4 in Exhibit 4. After j halvings the midpoint lies within (yhi​−ylo​)/2j+1 of the yield. From a bracket 5 percentage points wide, 10−8 takes 22 halvings and 23 midpoints, since 223≥0.05/10−8>222. Newton's method, which also uses the slope P′(y), needs far fewer (LM4).

A level-payment loan is an ordinary annuity seen from the borrower's side. The lender hands over B0​ today in exchange for n payments of PMT, so B0​=PMTan,i​ and

Balance, interest and principal

Recurrence (6.4) with CFt​=−PMT gives the balance after t payments in two equivalent forms:

The first, retrospective, form accumulates the loan and subtracts the accumulated payments. The second, prospective, form says the balance is the present value of the payments still due. Substituting B0​=PMTan,i​ into the first form shows they agree:

PMTi(1−vn)(1+i)t−((1+i)t−1)​=PMTi1−vn−t​=PMTan−t,i​.

The principal portions form a geometric sequence with ratio 1+i, from PMTvn in the first payment to PMTv in the last. Interest portions fall as principal portions rise, and each pair sums to PMT.

Principal exceeds interest, Qt​>It​, exactly when vn−t+1>21​: when the n−t+1 payments remaining span less than the doubling time ln2/ln(1+i). At 2.2% a year money doubles in 378.4 months, longer than a 30-year term, so principal exceeds interest from the first payment. At 5% the doubling time is 166.7 months, and principal overtakes interest only at payment 195.

Under equal-principal repayment the borrower repays B0​/n each period plus interest on the remaining balance. The balance before payment t is B0​(1−(t−1)/n), so the payments fall by the constant amount iB0​/n: an arithmetic sequence. Total interest follows from (6.1):

A grace period (寬限期) of m periods charges interest only, iB0​ per period, and then amortizes B0​ over the remaining n−m periods with (6.16).

7Cash-Flow Additivity and No-Arbitrage

apply cash-flow additivity and no-arbitrage to derive implied forward rates, forward exchange rates and the fair value of an index future

The law of one price says that two portfolios with identical future cash flows must cost the same today. Otherwise buying the cheaper and selling the dearer locks in a riskless profit, an arbitrage. With cash-flow additivity it prices anything that can be replicated; this section applies the pair three times.

Implied forward rates

An investor with a two-year horizon can buy a two-year zero at the spot rate z2​. Alternatively, she can buy a one-year zero at z1​ and agree today to reinvest for the second year at a forward rate y1,2​. Both strategies are riskless and pay off on the same date, so they must grow money equally: (1+z2​)2=(1+z1​)(1+y1,2​). In general, the forward rate yt1​,t2​​ from year t1​ to year t2​ satisfies

(1+zt2​​)t2​=(1+zt1​​)t1​(1+yt1​,t2​​)t2​−t1​.(6.20)

With continuous compounding the relation is additive, yt1​,t2​​=(t2​zt2​​−t1​zt1​​)/(t2​−t1​). Taking logs of (6.20) and using ln(1+zt​)≈zt​ and ln(1+yt1​,t2​​)≈yt1​,t2​​, which drop second-order terms (LM3), gives the same formula, approximately, for annual rates.

Forward exchange rates

Quote the exchange rate S as TWD per USD. To hold TWD in τ years, invest TWD 1 at iTWD​. Alternatively, convert it into USD 1/S, invest at iUSD​ and sell forward at F. Both routes are riskless, so with simple annual money-market rates

F=S1+iUSD​τ1+iTWD​τ​,(6.21)

covered interest parity; with continuous rates, F=Se(rTWD​−rUSD​)τ. We call F−S the forward points and quote them in TWD per USD. The lower-rate currency trades at a forward premium: when TWD rates are below USD rates, a forward buys fewer TWD per USD than spot.

Index futures and the cost of carry

To replicate an index futures position, borrow Se−qτ at the continuously compounded rate rf​ and buy e−qτ units of the index basket. Reinvest the dividends, which arrive at the rate q, in the basket. At expiry the position has grown to exactly one unit, worth ST​, and the loan to Se−qτerf​τ. A futures contract bought at F pays ST​−F, the same payoff, so the delivery price must be

F=Se(rf​−q)τ.(6.22)

If F were higher, an arbitrageur would sell futures and buy the basket (cash-and-carry); if lower, buy futures and short the basket (reverse cash-and-carry). Futures are marked to market daily, but with a deterministic interest rate their fair value equals that of a forward (Hull 2022). With discrete dividends Dj​ going ex tj​ years after the pricing date (0<tj​<τ), and treated as received then, the same argument gives

F=(S−j∑​Dj​e−rf​tj​)erf​τ=Serf​τ−j∑​Dj​erf​(τ−tj​).(6.23)

The futures price is the index carried forward at rf​, minus the dividends carried forward to expiry.

The basis F−S=S(e(rf​−q)τ−1)≈S(rf​−q)τ, with a relative error of about (rf​−q)τ/2, is negative whenever q>rf​: dividends outweigh interest. With illustrative values S=20,000, rf​=2%, q=3% and τ=0.25, F=19,950.06 and the basis is −49.94 points, against −50.00 to first order. At expiry TX is cash-settled against the average of the TAIEX values published in the last 30 minutes of trading (TAIFEX). F converges to that average, not to the 13:30 close: the futures version of pull to par.

Reading the fair value backwards

Observed F and S pin down rf​−q, not rf​ and q separately. Fixing one gives the other:

qτ=rf​τ−lnSF​,rimpl​=q+τ1​lnSF​.(6.24)

The first is the implied dividend yield, here measured over the contract's life as qτ. The second is the implied financing rate, often called the implied repo rate. Two futures with expiries τ1​<τ2​ give, by the logic of (6.20), an implied forward dividend yield between the expiries: q12​(τ2​−τ1​)=rf​(τ2​−τ1​)−ln(F2​/F1​). It does not involve S at all.

Frictions turn (6.22) into a band. Cash-and-carry borrows cash at a rate rb​. Reverse cash-and-carry must borrow every share in the basket, pay lending fees and usually earn less than rf​ on the short-sale proceeds. Its effective rate is some rℓ​<rb​.

Arbitrage then holds the futures price only within

Se(rℓ​−q)τ≤F≤Se(rb​−q)τ.

The lower bound is the weak one, because shorting a whole index basket is harder than buying it.

導讀無套利：同樣的現金流，同樣的價格

遠期利率、遠期匯率、期貨合理價，背後都是同一個論證：兩個未來現金流完全相同的組合，今天的價格必須相同，否則就能低買高賣、無風險套利。指數期貨的合理價 F=Se(rf​−q)τ 來自「借錢買現股、領股利」的複製：期貨比現貨多了利息 rf​、少了股利 q。台指期每年夏天的逆價差，第一個原因就是除息旺季的 q 大於 rf​。但套利的兩個方向難易不同：期貨太貴時，買現股、賣期貨很容易；期貨太便宜時，要放空整籃股票，成本高、限制多，所以逆價差可以偏離合理價很久。

Why the residual can persist

The no-arbitrage band of Section 7 is asymmetric. A rich TX is arbitraged with a stock purchase and a futures sale. A cheap one needs a short sale of every stock in the index, at an effective rate rℓ​ possibly far below 2%. Yet even at rf​=0 the December contract sat below fair value on 43 of the 44 sessions in June and July (Example 9).

The 2025 pattern is consistent with hedging pressure against that weak lower bound, a hypothesis rather than a measured cause. The near-month residual was deepest just after the April tariff shock (−298 points on 8 April). Holders of Taiwan stocks then had reason to sell futures rather than shares. The longer contracts' residuals were deepest in May and June.

Near-month and December residuals both turned positive in a rising October market. MacKinlay and Ramaswamy (1988) found that S&P 500 futures mispricing grows, on average, with time to maturity.

For a basis trader the residual is the opportunity, but not a riskless one. Dividends must be forecast and the stock leg must track TAIEX. The residual can widen before expiry forces convergence (LM25 models it as a mean-reverting spread).

PitfallAnnualizing a noisy basis

TAIEX closes on a 13:25–13:30 call auction. The TX daily settlement is the volume-weighted average price of the last minute before the 13:45 close (TAIFEX). The daily basis therefore mixes two prices fifteen minutes apart.

On 30 June TAIEX fell 324 points (1.44%) to its low of the day, while the July contract settled 22 points higher. Its basis jumped from −458 to −112. About 40 of those points were ex-dividend: the total-return index fell only 1.26%, and an ex-date raises the basis by the dividend. The other 306 points were mostly timing noise: TAIEX rebounded 298 points on 1 July.

Annualizing magnifies such noise as expiry nears. On 15 April, one day before expiry, a −64-point basis implies a dividend yield of 119% a year. Average over several days, use calendar spreads, or measure qτ over the contract's life without annualizing.

9Time Value of Money in Python

implement time-value-of-money calculations in Python

Three habits keep TVM code honest. Write one function per formula, with its equation number in the docstring. Check the bracket before any numerical search. Use Polars expressions, not Python loops, for market data.

Python
def annuity_pv(pmt, i, n, due=False):
    """PV of n level payments PMT at periodic rate i, eq. (6.5); due: (6.7)."""
    pv = pmt * n if i == 0 else pmt * (1 - (1 + i) ** -n) / i
    return pv * (1 + i) if due else pv

def bond_price(y, c, n, M=100.0):
    """Annual-coupon bond with coupon rate c and face value M, eq. (6.14)."""
    return annuity_pv(c * M, y, n) + M * (1 + y) ** -n

def bisect(fn, lo, hi, tol=1e-8):
    """Root of fn on a sign-changing [lo, hi]; each pass halves the bracket."""
    f_lo = fn(lo)
    if f_lo * fn(hi) > 0:
        raise ValueError("root not bracketed")
    while (hi - lo) / 2 > tol:
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        f_mid = fn(mid)
        if f_lo * f_mid <= 0:
            hi = mid
        else:
            lo, f_lo = mid, f_mid
    return (lo + hi) / 2

ytm = bisect(lambda y: bond_price(y, 0.015, 10) - 97.20, 0.0, 0.05)
print(f"{ytm:.6%}")                                     # 1.808601%
print(f"{annuity_pv(1_000, 0.03, 10, due=True):,.2f}")  # 8,786.11

Summary

  • An arithmetic sequence adds a constant; a geometric sequence multiplies by a constant ratio. The geometric sum (6.2) tends to a1​/(1−x) only when ∣x∣<1, and the share of the infinite sum beyond term n is exactly xn.
  • Discounting makes every level stream a geometric series with ratio v=1/(1+i), giving the annuity factors (6.5)–(6.6). An annuity due is worth 1+i times as much, (6.7).
  • Growing payments give (6.9) and (6.10), which require γ<i. The Gordon model P0​=D1​/(k−γ) reads a price as an implied return. For 0050 on 8 October 2026 it gives k=9.94% at 8.43% dividend growth, with two thirds of the value beyond year 30.
  • A bond trades above par exactly when its coupon rate exceeds its yield, (6.15). The yield to maturity is unique for conventional cash flows. Bisection finds it by halving the bracket, and so the error bound, at each step.
  • A loan balance is the present value of the remaining payments, (6.17), and principal portions grow at the rate i, (6.18). On the illustrative NT$10 million mortgage, equal-principal repayment pays NT$360,064 less interest (undiscounted) and a 3-year grace period NT$261,984 more. Yet at the loan rate every schedule is worth exactly the principal.
  • No-arbitrage gives forward rates (6.20), covered interest parity (6.21) and the futures fair value F=Se(rf​−q)τ, (6.22); observed prices imply rf​−q, (6.24).
  • Dividends going ex in 2025 came to qτ=2.93% of TAIEX, 83% of it from June to September: the summer shape of the TX discount. On 27 June, the extreme session, the December contract's 880-point discount split into 215 points of carry, 422 of dividends and a −673 residual. The residual averaged −461 over 23 June–3 July. Even at a zero financing rate the contract sat below fair value on 43 of 44 June–July sessions.
