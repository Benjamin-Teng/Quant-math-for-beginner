## Introduction

Every number computed from data is an estimate: another sample would give another value. This module measures how far estimates move between samples, and how to choose an estimator.

> [!case] What 23.8 years pin down
> From 3 January 2003 to 8 October 2026 the TAIEX total-return index, dividends reinvested, had calendar years from −43.07% (2008) to +83.34% (2009) ([[LM14]]). Its average day, 0.0625%, is 15.38% a year at $A=246.12$ sessions. Its daily standard deviation, 1.2084%, is 18.96% a year ([[LM19]]).
>
> Treat the 5,849 days as independent draws from one distribution. They then pin the volatility to 18.3% to 19.6% a year, but the mean only to 7.8% to 23.0%, both at 95% ([[ex:case]]). Five-minute data would not narrow that range; only more years would. Why is the mean so much harder to estimate? And how far can the Kelly fraction of 4.28 that these numbers suggest be trusted?

## Estimators, Bias and Mean Squared Error {lo=1}

Before the data are drawn, the sample mean is a random variable: each possible sample gives its own value. [[ex:sampling]] draws 10,000 one-year samples of 246 days from two models with TR23's mean and standard deviation, a normal and [[LM15]]'s Student-t, and estimates each sample's annual mean and volatility. Whether TAIEX's days are independent is a question about data, which [[LM19]] answers; here the model assumes it.

> [!definition] Estimator, bias and mean squared error
> An **estimator** $\hat\theta$ of a parameter $\theta$ is a function of the sample, and its value on the data is the **estimate**. Its distribution across samples is its **sampling distribution**, and its standard deviation the **standard error** $\operatorname{SE}(\hat\theta)$. The **bias** is $\operatorname{Bias}(\hat\theta):=\E\hat\theta-\theta$; the estimator is **unbiased** when the bias is 0. The **mean squared error** is $\operatorname{MSE}(\hat\theta):=\E[(\hat\theta-\theta)^2]$.

> [!result] Mean squared error
> If $\E\hat\theta^2<\infty$, then
> $$\operatorname{MSE}(\hat\theta)=\Var(\hat\theta)+\operatorname{Bias}(\hat\theta)^2. \tag{20.1}$$

*Proof.* Write $\hat\theta-\theta=(\hat\theta-\E\hat\theta)+\operatorname{Bias}(\hat\theta)$ and square. The cross term is the constant bias times $\hat\theta-\E\hat\theta$, whose expectation is 0 by (14.3). $\blacksquare$

> [!example] Shrinking the sample mean {#scaled}
> Let $T$ iid days have mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$. Find the $\operatorname{MSE}$ of $\eta\bar R$ and the $\eta$ that minimizes it. Evaluate $\eta^*$ for one year ($T=246$) and for TR23's $T=5{,}849$, with TR23's daily $\bar R/s=0.051727$ as the model's $\mathrm{SR}=\mu/\sigma$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > By (14.3), (14.4) and (17.1), $\E(\eta\bar R)=\eta\mu$ and $\Var(\eta\bar R)=\eta^2\sigma^2/T$, so $\operatorname{Bias}=(\eta-1)\mu$. By (20.1),
> > $$\operatorname{MSE}(\eta\bar R)=\frac{\eta^2\sigma^2}{T}+(\eta-1)^2\mu^2.$$
> > The derivative in $\eta$ vanishes at
> > $$\begin{gathered}\eta^*=\frac{\mu^2}{\mu^2+\sigma^2/T}=\frac{T\,\mathrm{SR}^2}{1+T\,\mathrm{SR}^2},\\ \operatorname{MSE}(\eta^*\bar R)=\eta^*\,\frac{\sigma^2}{T}.\end{gathered}$$
> > For one year, $T\,\mathrm{SR}^2=246\times0.051727^2=0.658$ and $\eta^*=0.397$: the shrunk mean has 0.397 times the mean squared error of $\bar R$. For the whole sample, $T\,\mathrm{SR}^2=15.65$ and $\eta^*=0.940$. The best $\eta$ depends on the unknown $\mu$; [[LM24]] supplies it from a prior.

An estimator is **consistent** when it converges in probability to $\theta$ as $T\to\infty$ ([[LM17]]). By (17.2), $\bar R$ is consistent for iid days with finite variance. The strong law, applied to $R_t$ and to $R_t^2$, makes $s^2=\frac1T\sum_tR_t^2-\bar R^2$ converge to $\sigma^2$, so $s$ is consistent too.

> [!pitfall] Unbiased is not the same as best
> An unbiased estimator is right on average but can still spread widely: in [[eg:scaled]] the biased $\eta^*\bar R$ beats $\bar R$. Report the bias and the standard error separately.

> [!zh] 估計量是隨機變數
> 樣本抽出之前，樣本平均數（sample mean）本身就是隨機變數（random variable）；用樣本算參數（parameter）的公式叫估計量（estimator），它在不同樣本間的分布（distribution）叫抽樣分布（sampling distribution）。估計量的期望值（expectation）減去真值是偏誤（bias），偏誤為零的叫不偏估計量（unbiased estimator）。好壞常用均方誤差（mean squared error）衡量，它等於變異數（variance）加偏誤的平方，所以稍有偏誤、變異數卻小得多的估計量可能更好：一年的資料下，$0.397\bar R$ 的均方誤差只有 $\bar R$ 的 0.397 倍。樣本越大越接近真值的叫一致估計量（consistent estimator）。模擬一萬個一年的樣本，年化平均報酬（annualized mean return）的標準差（standard deviation）約 19 個百分點，與尾部（tails）無關；年化波動率（annualized volatility）的分散卻由尾部決定。

> [!kc]
> For iid days, show that $\tfrac12\bar R$ has a smaller $\operatorname{MSE}$ than $\bar R$ exactly when $\lvert\mu/\sigma\rvert<\sqrt{3/T}$. Does it, for one year of days with TR23's daily $\bar R/s=0.0517$ as $\mu/\sigma$?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > By [[eg:scaled]] with $\eta=\tfrac12$, the condition is $\sigma^2/(4T)+\mu^2/4<\sigma^2/T$, that is $\mu^2/\sigma^2<3/T$. For $T=246$, $\sqrt{3/246}=0.110$, above 0.0517, so yes, if the model's $\mu$ and $\sigma$ were TR23's.

## The Standard Error of a Mean {lo=2}

For uncorrelated days with a common mean and variance, the sum rule (16.3) and (17.1) give $\Var\bar R=\sigma^2/T$. A mean over $\tau=T/A$ years scales by $A$, so its standard error does too.

> [!result] Standard error of a mean
> Let $R_1,\dots,R_T$ be uncorrelated with a common mean $\mu$ and a common variance $\sigma^2<\infty$. Then
> $$\begin{gathered}\operatorname{SE}(\bar R)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt T},\\ \operatorname{SE}(A\bar R)=\frac{A\sigma}{\sqrt T}=\frac{\sqrt A\,\sigma}{\sqrt\tau},\end{gathered} \tag{20.2}$$
> estimated by replacing $\sigma$ with $s$.

> [!example] The standard error of TAIEX's mean {#semean}
> Compute the standard error of TR23's daily mean and of its annual mean $A\bar R=15.38\%$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > By (20.2), $s/\sqrt T=1.2084\%/\sqrt{5{,}849}=0.01580\%$ a day, the value that [[LM18]]'s (18.8) gave for its bootstrap model. Times $A=246.12$, it is 3.89 points a year, a quarter of the 15.38%. Equivalently, $18.96\%/\sqrt{23.7645}=3.89$. [[LM7]]'s 7.36 and 6.09 points over 4.95 years follow the same rule.

Merton (1980) drew the consequence: finer sampling does not help the mean. By (1.12), $\sum_tr_t=\ln(P_T/P_0)$, so the annual mean log return $A\bar r=\ln(P_T/P_0)/\tau$ depends only on the first and last price. Five-minute log returns over the same years give the same number.

[[ex:merton]] measures both standard errors at eight sampling intervals, from one day to one year. The mean's stays between 3.87 and 4.53 points, around the iid line. The volatility's grows from 0.34 points with daily data to 4.43 with annual sums, and the normal model's from 0.18 to 2.80. Fine sampling sharpens the volatility, never the mean. A mean known to ±1 point at 95% needs $(1.96\times18.96)^2=1{,}381$ years of data.

The mean's standard error survives volatility clustering if the days are uncorrelated: with unequal variances $\sigma_t^2$, (16.3) gives $\Var\bar R=\frac1{T^2}\sum_t\sigma_t^2$. Correlated days break (20.2). [[LM19]]'s variance ratios measure how far daily variances fail to add up; [[LM23]]'s HAC errors and [[LM22]]'s block bootstrap then repair the standard error.

An expansion around $\mu$ gives the bias of $s^2$. Since $\sum_t(R_t-\bar R)=0$,

$$\sum_{t=1}^T(R_t-\mu)^2=\sum_{t=1}^T(R_t-\bar R)^2+T(\bar R-\mu)^2.$$

The left side has expectation $T\sigma^2$, and by (20.2) the last term has $\sigma^2$, so $\E\sum_t(R_t-\bar R)^2=(T-1)\sigma^2$.

> [!result] The bias of the sample variance
> Under the conditions of (20.2),
> $$\begin{gathered}\E s^2=\frac{T-1}{T}\,\sigma^2,\\ s_{\mathrm u}^2:=\frac{T}{T-1}\,s^2,\qquad \E s_{\mathrm u}^2=\sigma^2.\end{gathered} \tag{20.3}$$

The conditions of (20.2) are those under which [[LM10]]'s divisor $T-1$ removes a small bias. NumPy's `ddof=1` and Polars' default compute $s_{\mathrm u}$, which [[LM19]] introduced.

> [!example] How much the divisor matters {#divisor}
> Compute $s_{\mathrm u}/s$ for TR23's 5,849 days and for the 23 annual log returns of 2003–2025, whose $s$ is 22.207% ([[LM15]]).
>
> > [!solution]
> > The ratio is $\sqrt{T/(T-1)}$: 1.0000855 for TR23, a change in the fifth significant figure. For 23 values it is $\sqrt{23/22}=1.0225$, so $s_{\mathrm u}=22.706\%$, and $\E s^2$ is only $22/23=0.9565$ of $\sigma^2$. [[LM10]]'s 4,382 returns gave 1.000114. The divisor matters for calendar years and other small samples, not for daily data.

> [!pitfall] Annualizing the mean's error by the square root
> A mean scales by $A$, so its standard error does too. The daily 0.0158% times $\sqrt A$ gives 0.25 points, $\sqrt A=15.69$ times less than the correct 3.89. Only a standard deviation scales by $\sqrt A$, and exactly only for log returns on uncorrelated days ([[LM19]]).

> [!zh] 平均數的標準誤只看年數
> 樣本平均數（sample mean）的標準誤（standard error）是 $\sigma/\sqrt T$，年化之後等於年化波動率（annualized volatility）除以資料年數的平方根，與抽樣頻率（sampling frequency）無關。加權股價報酬指數 23.8 年的資料，年化平均報酬（annualized mean return）的標準誤約 3.89 個百分點；改用五分鐘資料也不會變小，因為對數報酬（log return）的總和只取決於頭尾兩個價格。要讓年化平均報酬的 95% 信賴區間（confidence interval）縮到正負 1 個百分點，需要約 1,381 年的資料。樣本變異數（sample variance）除以 $T$ 時，期望值（expectation）只有 $\sigma^2$ 的 $(T-1)/T$ 倍，除以 $T-1$ 才不偏（unbiased）；日資料只差在第五位有效數字（significant figure），23 個年報酬的標準差（standard deviation）則差 2.2%。

> [!kc]
> Three years of independent daily returns have an annual volatility of 18.96%. What is the standard error of the annual mean? Ten years? What if the returns were monthly over the same years?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > By (20.2), $18.96/\sqrt3=10.9$ points and $18.96/\sqrt{10}=6.0$ points. Monthly data over the same years give the same, because only the years enter.

## Confidence Intervals {lo=3}

A standard error becomes an interval once the shape of the estimator's distribution is known.

> [!definition] Confidence interval
> A **95% confidence interval** for $\theta$ is an interval computed from the sample that contains $\theta$ with probability 0.95 for every $\theta$, exactly or approximately for large $T$. The probability is over samples, with $\theta$ fixed; it is the interval's **coverage**.

By (17.5), $\sqrt T(\bar R-\mu)/\sigma$ is approximately standard normal for iid days with finite variance. Replacing $\sigma$ by the consistent $s$ keeps the approximation (stated without proof; Wasserman 2004). With $\Phi^{-1}(0.975)=1.96$, (15.6), this gives a large-sample interval; for normal days an exact one exists.

> [!result] Confidence intervals for a mean
> Let the days be iid with mean $\mu$ and variance $0<\sigma^2<\infty$. For large $T$, an approximate 95% interval for $\mu$ is $\bar R\pm1.96\,s/\sqrt T$. If the days are iid normal and $T\ge2$, an exact 95% interval is
> $$\bar R\pm F_Y^{-1}(0.975)\,\frac{s_{\mathrm u}}{\sqrt T},\qquad Y\sim t_{T-1}. \tag{20.4}$$

*Proof of the exact form.* Let $Z=\sqrt T(\bar R-\mu)/\sigma$ and $V=(T-1)s_{\mathrm u}^2/\sigma^2$. For iid normal days, $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ and $V\sim\chi^2_{T-1}$ ([[LM16]]; Blitzstein and Hwang 2019, Example 10.4.3), and they are independent (their Example 7.5.9). By their Definition 10.4.4, whose density is (15.9) (they state it without proof),

$$\frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/(T-1)}}=\frac{\sqrt T(\bar R-\mu)}{s_{\mathrm u}}\sim t_{T-1},$$

so $\lvert\bar R-\mu\rvert\le F_Y^{-1}(0.975)\,s_{\mathrm u}/\sqrt T$ with probability 0.95. $\blacksquare$

> [!example] TAIEX's annual mean {#interval}
> Give 95% intervals for TR23's annual mean with the normal approximation and with the $t_{T-1}$ form. How wide is the interval from one year of data with the same volatility?
>
> > [!solution]
> > By (20.4) and [[eg:semean]], $15.38\%\pm1.96\times3.89$ runs from 7.8% to 23.0%. At $T-1=5{,}848$ the $t$ quantile is 1.96037 against $\Phi^{-1}(0.975)=1.95996$, and $s_{\mathrm u}/s=1.0000855$, so the $t$ interval differs by 0.002 points. One year of data gives a standard error of 18.96 points and a half-width of $1.96\times18.96=37.2$ points.

[[ex:coverage]] checks both intervals by simulation. With $s$ and 1.96, small samples cover too rarely, 84.5% at $T=5$, because the normal quantile ignores the error in $s$, and divisor $T$ shrinks $s$. The $t_{T-1}$ interval is exact for normal days and covers slightly too often for $t_3$ days. By $T=246$ every column is between 94.8% and 95.2%.

> [!pitfall] The 95% belongs to the procedure
> Once computed, 7.8% to 23.0% either contains $A\mu$ or does not. The 95% says that intervals built this way cover $A\mu$ in 95% of samples, if the days are iid. A probability for $A\mu$ itself needs [[LM24]]. An interval that excludes a value is the start of a test, [[LM21]]'s subject; this module runs none.

## Maximum Likelihood {lo=4}

[[LM15]] fitted a Student-t to TAIEX with SciPy's `t.fit`, by maximum likelihood. Let the days be iid with a density $f_R(x;\boldsymbol\theta)$, known up to the parameters $\boldsymbol\theta$.

> [!definition] Likelihood
> The **likelihood** of iid observations $R_1,\dots,R_T$ is $\mathcal L(\boldsymbol\theta):=\prod_{t=1}^Tf_R(R_t;\boldsymbol\theta)$, and the **log-likelihood** is $\ell(\boldsymbol\theta):=\ln\mathcal L(\boldsymbol\theta)=\sum_t\ln f_R(R_t;\boldsymbol\theta)$. The **maximum-likelihood estimator** (MLE) $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$ maximizes $\ell$ over the parameters the model allows.

The likelihood holds the data fixed and ranks parameter values by the density they give the sample. For the normal model, $f_R(x;\mu,\sigma)=\varphi\big((x-\mu)/\sigma\big)/\sigma$, and Section 3's expansion gives

$$\begin{aligned}\ell(\mu,\sigma)={}&-\frac T2\ln(2\pi)-T\ln\sigma\\&-\frac{Ts^2+T(\bar R-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}.\end{aligned}$$

> [!result] Normal maximum likelihood
> For iid normal days with $T\ge2$, not all equal, the MLE is
> $$\hat\mu=\bar R,\qquad\hat\sigma=s. \tag{20.5}$$
> For iid $\mathcal N_N(\boldsymbol\mu,\Sigma)$ vectors $\mathbf R_t$, the MLE is $\bar{\mathbf R}$ and [[LM10]]'s $\hat\Sigma$ of (10.2), whenever $\hat\Sigma$ is positive definite (Deep Dive 1).

*Proof (one variable).* For every $\sigma$, $\ell$ is largest at $\mu=\bar R$, where it is $-\tfrac T2\ln(2\pi)-T\ln\sigma-Ts^2/(2\sigma^2)$. Its derivative in $\sigma$, $-T/\sigma+Ts^2/\sigma^3$, is positive below $s$ and negative above. $\blacksquare$

So the maximum-likelihood variance $s^2$ divides by $T$ and is biased, by (20.3): maximum likelihood aims at large-sample precision, not at unbiasedness.

> [!example] [[LM5]]'s normal fit is maximum likelihood {#normalmle}
> [[LM5]] fitted $\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ to 0050's 2,902 daily total returns with $\hat\mu=0.0871\%$ and $\hat\sigma=s=1.2385\%$. Is that the MLE? What is TR23's?
>
> > [!solution]
> > Yes, by (20.5): [[LM5]]'s "population standard deviation" of the sample divides by $T$. The mean's standard error is 0.0230% ([[LM4]]). For TR23 the MLE is $\hat\mu=0.0625\%$ and $\hat\sigma=1.2084\%$, not $s_{\mathrm u}=1.2085\%$.

> [!deepdive]- Deep Dive 1: maximum likelihood for $\mathcal N_N$
> Here $K$ is a square matrix, $L$ a Cholesky factor, (12.8), and $\lambda_i$, $i=1,\dots,N$, the eigenvalues of $M$ below. By the density in (16.7), the log-likelihood of iid $\mathcal N_N(\boldsymbol\mu,\Sigma)$ vectors with $\Sigma$ positive definite is
> $$\begin{aligned}\ell={}&-\frac T2\big(N\ln2\pi+\ln\det\Sigma\big)\\&-\frac12\sum_{t=1}^T(\mathbf R_t-\boldsymbol\mu)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf R_t-\boldsymbol\mu).\end{aligned}$$
> As in Section 3, the sum splits into $\sum_t(\mathbf R_t-\bar{\mathbf R})^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf R_t-\bar{\mathbf R})$ plus $T(\bar{\mathbf R}-\boldsymbol\mu)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\bar{\mathbf R}-\boldsymbol\mu)$, since the cross terms sum to zero. The second part is positive unless $\boldsymbol\mu=\bar{\mathbf R}$, so $\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}=\bar{\mathbf R}$.
>
> With $\mathbf x^{\top}K\mathbf x=\operatorname{tr}(K\mathbf x\mathbf x^{\top})$ and (10.2), the first part equals $T\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}\hat\Sigma)$. Let $\hat\Sigma=LL^{\top}$ be positive definite, which needs $T>N$, and let $M=L^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}L$, symmetric with eigenvalues $\lambda_i>0$. Then $\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}\hat\Sigma)=\operatorname{tr}M=\sum_i\lambda_i$ and $\det\Sigma=\det\hat\Sigma/\prod_i\lambda_i$ ([[LM11]]), so, at $\boldsymbol\mu=\bar{\mathbf R}$,
> $$\begin{aligned}\ell=-\frac T2\Big[&N\ln2\pi+\ln\det\hat\Sigma\\&+\sum_{i=1}^N(\lambda_i-\ln\lambda_i)\Big].\end{aligned}$$
> By (2.1), $\ln x\le x-1$, so each $\lambda_i-\ln\lambda_i\ge1$, with equality only at $\lambda_i=1$. The maximum needs $M=I$, that is $\Sigma=LL^{\top}=\hat\Sigma$, with divisor $T$. $\blacksquare$

[[LM15]]'s Student-t is the density (15.9), shifted by a location and stretched by a scale. No formula gives its MLE, so a numerical search maximizes $\ell$. [[LM26]] fits GARCH models with the same machinery.

## Fisher Information and the Cramér–Rao Bound {lo=5}

A sharp peak in $\ell$ pins the parameter down; a flat top leaves many values almost as likely.

> [!definition] Score and Fisher information
> For one observation with density $f_R(x;\theta)$, the **score** is $\partial_\theta\ln f_R(R;\theta)$ and the **Fisher information** is
> $$\mathcal I(\theta):=-\E\Big[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\ln f_R(R;\theta)\Big].$$
> The **observed information** of a sample is $-\ell''(\hat\theta)$, and for a parameter vector the matrix $-H$ at $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$.

A sample of $T$ iid observations carries $T\mathcal I(\theta)$, which the observed information estimates. Deep Dive 2 shows that $\mathcal I(\theta)$ is also the variance of the score.

> [!theorem] Asymptotic normality of the MLE
> Let the following hold:
> - the observations are iid with density $f_R(x;\theta)$, the model is correct, and distinct $\theta$ give distinct densities;
> - the true $\theta$ is an interior point of the parameter set, and the support of $f_R$ does not depend on $\theta$;
> - the density is smooth in $\theta$, with $0<\mathcal I(\theta)<\infty$.
>
> Then, for large $T$,
> $$\begin{gathered}\hat\theta\approx\mathcal N\Big(\theta,\frac1{T\mathcal I(\theta)}\Big),\\ \operatorname{SE}(\hat\theta)\approx\big(-\ell''(\hat\theta)\big)^{-1/2},\end{gathered} \tag{20.6}$$
> and for a vector $\Cov(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta})\approx(-H)^{-1}$ at $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$. Stated without proof (Wasserman 2004).

> [!example] The normal model's information {#info}
> Compute $\mathcal I(\mu)$ and $\mathcal I(\sigma)$ for $\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, and the standard errors of TR23's $\hat\mu$ and $\hat\sigma$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > From $\ln f_R=-\ln\sigma-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)$ plus a constant, $\partial^2_\mu\ln f_R=-1/\sigma^2$ and $\partial^2_\sigma\ln f_R=1/\sigma^2-3(x-\mu)^2/\sigma^4$, whose expectation is $-2/\sigma^2$. The mixed derivative has expectation 0, so the two parameters can be treated one at a time: $\mathcal I(\mu)=1/\sigma^2$ and $\mathcal I(\sigma)=2/\sigma^2$. By (20.6),
> > $$\begin{gathered}\operatorname{SE}(\hat\mu)=\frac{s}{\sqrt T}=0.0158\%,\\ \operatorname{SE}(\hat\sigma)=\frac{s}{\sqrt{2T}}=0.0112\%.\end{gathered}$$
> > The first is (20.2) again, and the second is [[LM18]]'s $s_Y/\sqrt{2n}$ for near-normal draws.

The **profile log-likelihood** of one parameter maximizes $\ell$ over the others at each of its values. [[ex:profile]] draws it for $\nu$, beside the parabola of the observed information. Where the curve falls $1.96^2/2=1.92$ below its top it gives 2.80 to 3.37; the parabola gives $\hat\nu\pm1.96\times0.144$, 2.78 to 3.35. Comparing two maximized log-likelihoods is a likelihood-ratio test, outside this book (Wasserman 2004, §10.6).

> Note: TR23's daily returns; at each $\nu$ the location and scale are re-maximized. The dashed parabola has the curvature $1/0.144^2$ of the observed information. In-sample. Source: FinMind `TaiwanStockTotalReturnIndex` (TAIEX), retrieved 18 October 2026.

> [!theorem] Cramér–Rao bound
> Let the following hold:
> - the conditions of (20.6);
> - the identity $\int f_R(x;\theta)\,dx=1$ may be differentiated twice under the integral sign, and $\E\hat\theta$ once;
> - the estimator $\hat\theta$ of $\theta$ from $T$ iid observations is unbiased.
>
> Then
> $$\Var(\hat\theta)\ge\frac1{T\mathcal I(\theta)}. \tag{20.7}$$

Deep Dive 2 proves the bound (Rao 1992, a reprint). In the normal model $\bar R$ attains it: $\Var\bar R=\sigma^2/T=1/(T\mathcal I(\mu))$. Inside the Student-t model, $\bar R$'s standard error is the fit's $1.317\%/\sqrt T=0.0172\%$, and the location's is 0.0124%, a ratio of 0.72. That efficiency holds only if the days are Student-t, and the location is not the mean.

> [!deepdive]- Deep Dive 2: why no unbiased estimator beats $1/(T\mathcal I)$
> Here $\mathbf x=(x_1,\dots,x_T)$ is a possible sample, $g(\mathbf x;\theta)=\prod_tf_R(x_t;\theta)$ its joint density and $u=\partial_\theta\ln g$ the sample's score. Differentiating $\int g\,d\mathbf x=1$ under the integral gives $\E u=\int\partial_\theta g\,d\mathbf x=0$. Differentiating $\int(\partial_\theta\ln f_R)\,f_R\,dx=0$ once more gives $\E[(\partial_\theta\ln f_R)^2]=\mathcal I(\theta)$, so the sum $u$ of $T$ iid scores has $\Var u=T\mathcal I(\theta)$ by (16.3).
>
> Unbiasedness says $\int\hat\theta(\mathbf x)\,g\,d\mathbf x=\theta$; differentiating gives $\E[\hat\theta u]=1$, so $\Cov(\hat\theta,u)=1$ since $\E u=0$. For $\Var\hat\theta<\infty$, the bound $\lvert\rho\rvert\le1$ ([[LM16]]) gives $1=\Cov(\hat\theta,u)^2\le\Var(\hat\theta)\,T\mathcal I(\theta)$, which is (20.7). An infinite variance satisfies (20.7) trivially. $\blacksquare$

> [!pitfall] An information-based standard error assumes the model
> Formula (20.6) holds when the model is right. A wrong model can make $(-H)^{-1}$ far too small: Problem 8's Poisson standard error understates the spread of TAIEX's monthly counts of large moves.

> [!zh] 概度、訊息與下界
> 把資料固定、讓參數（parameter）變動，樣本的聯合密度（joint density）就成了概度函數（likelihood function），使它最大的參數值就是最大概度估計（maximum likelihood estimation）。常態模型（normal model）的最大概度估計正是樣本平均數（sample mean）與除以 $T$ 的標準差（standard deviation）；t 分布（Student-t distribution）則要用數值方法（numerical methods）求解，加權股價報酬指數的自由度（degrees of freedom）約為 3.065。單筆觀察的對數概度（log-likelihood）二階導數（second derivative）取負號後的期望值（expectation）是費雪訊息（Fisher information）；實務上用樣本對數概度在頂點的彎曲程度，即負的 Hessian 矩陣，也就是觀察訊息矩陣（observed information matrix），其反矩陣（inverse matrix）對角元素（diagonal entries）的平方根就是近似的標準誤（standard error）；樣本大時，估計量（estimator）具漸近常態性（asymptotic normality）。克萊姆-饒歐下界（Cramér–Rao lower bound）說不偏估計量（unbiased estimator）的變異數（variance）不會低於 $1/(T\mathcal I)$，達到下界的估計量最有效率（efficiency）。剖面概度（profile likelihood）比拋物線（parabola）更忠實：自由度的區間 2.80 到 3.37 並不對稱。

## The Delta Method {lo=6}

The annual volatility, the Kelly fraction and the Sharpe ratio are smooth functions of $\bar R$ and $s^2$. Their standard errors follow from a first-order expansion.

> [!theorem] Delta method
> Let $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$ be approximately normal with mean $\boldsymbol\theta$ and covariance matrix $\Cov(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta})$, and let $h$ be differentiable at $\boldsymbol\theta$ with $\nabla h\ne\mathbf 0$. Then $h(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta})$ is approximately normal with mean $h(\boldsymbol\theta)$ and
> $$\Var h(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta})\approx\nabla h^{\top}\Cov(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta})\,\nabla h. \tag{20.8}$$

*Why.* By (7.1), $h(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta})\approx h(\boldsymbol\theta)+\nabla h^{\top}(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}-\boldsymbol\theta)$, and (16.4) gives the variance of this linear function, as (10.4) gave a portfolio's (Wasserman 2004).

Up to a term of order $1/T$, $s^2$ averages the iid terms $(R_t-\mu)^2$, each with variance $(\operatorname{Kurt}-1)\sigma^4$ by (14.5). So $\Var(s^2)\approx(\operatorname{Kurt}-1)\sigma^4/T$ by (17.1), and $h(v)=\sqrt v$, with derivative $1/(2\sigma)$ at $\sigma^2$, gives by (20.8)

$$\operatorname{SE}(s)\approx\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{Kurt}-1}{4T}},$$

which needs a finite fourth moment. For normal days $\operatorname{Kurt}=3$, and this is [[eg:info]]'s $\sigma/\sqrt{2T}$.

> [!example] How precisely TAIEX's volatility is known {#vol}
> Give standard errors and 95% intervals for TR23's annual volatility, [[LM19]]'s 18.96%, and for the standard deviation of the 23 annual log returns, [[LM15]]'s 22.207%.
>
> > [!solution]
> > With [[LM14]]'s kurtosis 8.27, the volatility's standard error is $18.96\times\sqrt{7.27/(4\times5{,}849)}=0.33$ points, so the interval is 18.3% to 19.6%. For normal days it is $18.96/\sqrt{2\times5{,}849}=0.175$ points.
> >
> > For 23 normal years, $22.207/\sqrt{46}=3.27$ points gives 15.8% to 28.6%. [[LM17]]'s daily-based 18.563% (standard error 0.34, from the kurtosis 8.55 of its 5,664 log returns) lies 3.6 points, or 1.1 standard errors, below. So [[LM19]]'s gap is within the sampling error of 23 values.
> >
> > Under a normal model of the years, $23s^2/\sigma^2=22s_{\mathrm u}^2/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{22}$ (Blitzstein and Hwang 2019, Example 10.4.3). Its 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles are 10.98 and 36.78 (`stats.chi2.ppf`), so $22.207\%\times\sqrt{23/36.78}$ to $22.207\%\times\sqrt{23/10.98}$ gives 17.6% to 32.1%.

Unlike the mean's, the volatility's standard error does not survive clustering: large squared returns cluster ([[LM19]]), so the 0.33 is probably too small ([[LM22]]). By regime, $s$ is 1.257% (0.026) before 1 June 2015 and 1.152% (0.035) after: a stability check, not a test ([[LM21]]).

For the Kelly fraction and the Sharpe ratio, $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}=(\bar R,s^2)$, with $\Cov(\bar R,s^2)\approx\operatorname{Skew}\sigma^3/T$ by (14.5). The gradients of $\mu/\sigma^2$ and $\mu/\sigma$ in $(\mu,\sigma^2)$ are $(1/\sigma^2,-\mu/\sigma^4)$ and $(1/\sigma,-\mu/(2\sigma^3))$, and (20.8) gives the next result.

> [!result] Standard errors of the Kelly fraction and the Sharpe ratio
> Let the days be iid with $\E R^4<\infty$ and $\mathrm{SR}=\mu/\sigma$. For large $T$,
> $$\begin{aligned}\sigma^2\,T\Var(\hat f)&\approx1-2\,\mathrm{SR}\operatorname{Skew}\\&\quad+\mathrm{SR}^2(\operatorname{Kurt}-1),\\ T\Var\big(\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}\big)&\approx1-\mathrm{SR}\operatorname{Skew}\\&\quad+\tfrac14\mathrm{SR}^2(\operatorname{Kurt}-1).\end{aligned} \tag{20.9}$$

## The Hill Estimator of a Tail Index {lo=7}

[[LM15]] read TAIEX's tail from chords between loss levels, 2.09 to 5.03, and its Student-t fit implies a tail index of $\nu=3.065$. A tail index can also be estimated from the largest losses alone.

Let $X=-R$ be a day's loss and $x_0>0$ a loss level. Model the losses beyond $x_0$ as an exact power tail, $\Pr(X>x\mid X\ge x_0)=(x/x_0)^{-\alpha}$ for $x\ge x_0$. This is a **Pareto distribution**, with density $\alpha x_0^{\alpha}x^{-\alpha-1}$ on $x\ge x_0$. Its support does not depend on $\alpha$, so (20.6) applies. For $k$ iid losses $x_1,\dots,x_k\ge x_0$,

$$\begin{aligned}\ell(\alpha)&=k\ln\alpha+k\alpha\ln x_0\\&\quad-(\alpha+1)\sum_{i=1}^k\ln x_i,\\ \ell'(\alpha)&=\frac k\alpha-\sum_{i=1}^k\ln\frac{x_i}{x_0}.\end{aligned}$$

The root of $\ell'$ is a maximum, since $\ell''=-k/\alpha^2<0$. One loss carries the information $1/\alpha^2$.

> [!result] Hill estimator
> Let the losses at or beyond $x_0$ be iid with an exact power tail of index $\alpha$, and let $k$ of them be observed. The MLE of $\alpha$ and its standard error are
> $$\hat\alpha=\frac{k}{\sum_{i=1}^k\ln(x_i/x_0)},\qquad\operatorname{SE}(\hat\alpha)\approx\frac{\hat\alpha}{\sqrt k}. \tag{20.10}$$

Hill (1975) based tail inference on the conditional likelihood given the largest order statistics; here $x_0$ is a fixed loss level, as in [[LM15]]'s counts.

> [!example] TAIEX's tail index {#tailindex}
> Estimate the tail index of TR23's daily losses at $x_0=3\%$, and then at 2%, 4% and 5%.
>
> > [!solution]
> > At 3% the guard `R <= -0.03 + 1e-12` finds $k=108$ losses, [[LM13]]'s count. By (20.10), $\hat\alpha=3.63$ with standard error $3.63/\sqrt{108}=0.35$, so the interval is 2.9 to 4.3. At 2%, 4% and 5% the estimates are 2.49 (0.16), 4.93 (0.74) and 4.76 (1.23), from $k$ = 252, 44 and 15 ([[ex:hill]]). The estimate rises from 2% to 4%, as [[LM15]]'s chords did, then wanders once $k$ falls below 45. The Student-t's 3.065 sits near the low thresholds.

> [!pitfall] The threshold trades bias against variance
> A low threshold brings many losses and a small standard error, but losses near 2% belong to the body of the distribution, not to a power tail. A high one fits the tail model better but leaves few losses. Big losses also come in runs ([[LM19]]), so the iid $\hat\alpha/\sqrt k$ is likely too small. Value at risk from a tail model is [[LM32]]'s; extreme value theory beyond Hill's estimator is not in this book.

> [!zh] 希爾估計量：尾部指數
> 尾部指數（tail index）描述極端損失出現的頻率隨幅度遞減的速度，數值越小尾巴越厚。若門檻（threshold）$x_0$ 以上的損失服從帕累托分布（Pareto distribution），最大概度估計（maximum likelihood estimation）就是希爾估計量（Hill estimator）$k/\sum\ln(x_i/x_0)$，標準誤（standard error）約為 $\hat\alpha/\sqrt k$。加權股價報酬指數在 3% 門檻有 108 個損失日，估計值 3.63，標準誤 0.35；門檻 2% 時是 2.49，4% 時升到 4.93。門檻低則樣本多、標準誤小，卻混入不屬於尾部的損失而產生偏誤（bias）；門檻高則偏誤小、變異數（variance）大。大跌日又常成群出現，所以獨立（independent）假設下的標準誤可能偏小。

## Estimation in Python {lo=8}

Every estimate here takes a few library calls; the divisor, the tolerance and the seed must be explicit.

```python
import numpy as np
import polars as pl
from scipy import optimize, stats

tr = pl.read_csv("data/taiex_tr.csv")["price"].to_numpy()
R = tr[1:] / tr[:-1] - 1          # T = 5,849 simple returns
T = len(R)
s, s_u = R.std(ddof=0), R.std(ddof=1)   # 1.208364%, 1.208468%
se_mean = s / np.sqrt(T)                # 0.01580% a day
q = stats.t.ppf(0.975, T - 1)           # 1.96037

x = 100 * R                       # percent, for the optimizer


def nll(p):                       # minus the Student-t log-likelihood
    return -stats.t.logpdf(x, *p).sum()


fit = optimize.minimize(nll, stats.t.fit(x), method="Nelder-Mead",
                        options={"xatol": 1e-12, "fatol": 1e-12})


def hessian(f, p, h):             # central differences
    k = len(p)
    H = np.zeros((k, k))
    for i in range(k):
        for j in range(k):
            a, b = np.eye(k)[i] * h[i], np.eye(k)[j] * h[j]
            H[i, j] = (f(p + a + b) - f(p + a - b) - f(p - a + b)
                       + f(p - a - b)) / (4 * h[i] * h[j])
    return H


for h in ([1e-4, 1e-5, 1e-5], [1e-3, 1e-4, 1e-4]):   # two step sizes
    cov = np.linalg.inv(hessian(nll, fit.x, h))
    print(np.sqrt(np.diag(cov)))  # 0.144, 0.0124, 0.0138 both times

rng = np.random.default_rng([1, 20261011])   # experiment 1, once
sims = R.mean() + s * rng.standard_normal((10_000, 246))
vol = np.sqrt(T / 23.7645) * sims.std(axis=1, ddof=0)
print(np.quantile(vol, [0.025, 0.975], method="inverted_cdf"))
```

NumPy's `std` divides by $T$ by default and Polars' by $T-1$, so pass `ddof` every time. SciPy's Nelder–Mead stops at a tolerance of $10^{-4}$ unless told otherwise. The last lines repeat the normal block of [[ex:sampling]]: 17.3% to 20.6%.

## Summary {-}

- Mean squared error is variance plus squared bias, (20.1); a biased estimator can win.
- A mean's standard error is $\sigma/\sqrt T$, (20.2); annualized, the years of data decide it. Divisor $T$ biases $s^2$ by $(T-1)/T$, (20.3).
- The interval $\bar R\pm1.96\,s/\sqrt T$ needs a large $T$; the $t_{T-1}$ interval is exact for normal days, (20.4).
- Maximum likelihood gives $\bar R$ and $s$ for the normal model, (20.5), and fits a Student-t numerically.
- The observed information gives standard errors, (20.6); no unbiased estimator beats the Cramér–Rao bound, (20.7).
- The delta method, (20.8) and (20.9), gives the volatility, the Kelly fraction and the Sharpe ratio standard errors under iid days and a finite fourth moment.
- The Hill estimator, (20.10), estimates a tail index; its threshold trades bias against variance.

## Solutions {-}

1. **A** is correct. By (20.2), the annual mean's standard error is $20\%/\sqrt{10}=6.32$ points, whatever the sampling interval. B divides by $\sqrt{54}$, as if five-minute returns told more about the mean. C is $20\%/\sqrt{2{,}461}$: it treats each of the 2,461 days as a year.

2. **C** is correct: $\sqrt{23/22}-1=2.2\%$. A is the variance's factor, $23/22-1=4.5\%$. B ignores the divisors: $s_{\mathrm u}/s=\sqrt{23/22}$ in every sample.

3. **B** is correct: the 95% is the coverage of the procedure, with $A\mu$ fixed. A treats $A\mu$ as random, a posterior statement ([[LM24]]). C confuses the mean with single years, which spread about 22 points around it ([[LM15]]).

4. **C** is correct: by (20.5), the MLE is $s$ with divisor 23, 22.21%. A is $s_{\mathrm u}$, 22.71%, which is not the MLE. B is [[LM17]]'s daily-based 18.56%, another estimator on other data.

5. **B** is correct: $18.96/\sqrt{2\times5{,}849}=0.18$ points. A uses the sample kurtosis 8.27, not the stated normal model. C forgets the 2: $18.96/\sqrt{5{,}849}=0.25$.

6. (a) By (16.8) with unit variances, $Y=\rho X+\varepsilon$, with $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1-\rho^2)$ independent of $X$. So $\E[X^3Y]=\rho\E X^4=3\rho$ and $\E[X^2Y^2]=\rho^2\E X^4+\E X^2\,\E\varepsilon^2=3\rho^2+1-\rho^2=1+2\rho^2$, by (14.6).
   (b) The mean of $L_t$ is $\rho-\rho=0$. With (a) and $\E[(X^2+Y^2)^2]=8+4\rho^2$,
   $$\begin{aligned}\E L_t^2&=1+2\rho^2-6\rho^2+\tfrac{\rho^2}4(8+4\rho^2)\\&=(1-\rho^2)^2,\end{aligned}$$
   and (17.1) gives $\Var\hat\rho\approx(1-\rho^2)^2/T$.
   (c) The normal-theory standard error is $(1-0.5749^2)/\sqrt{4{,}382}=0.0101$, so the interval is 0.555 to 0.595. The plug-in one is 0.0153, with interval 0.545 to 0.605, 1.51 times wider. Fat tails and clustered volatility make $\E[X^2Y^2]$ larger than the normal pair's.

7. The regimes give $\nu=2.990$ (0.195) and 3.237 (0.223). The difference, 0.247, has standard error $\sqrt{0.195^2+0.223^2}=0.296$, so it is about 0.8 standard errors. It is not yet a test: that needs a null hypothesis, a reference distribution and a count of the tests run ([[LM21]]).

8. (a) For counts $j_1,\dots,j_{286}$, $\ell(\lambda)=\sum_i\big(j_i\ln\lambda-\lambda-\ln j_i!\big)$, so $\ell'(\lambda)=\sum_ij_i/\lambda-286=0$ gives the mean count. Per month $\mathcal I(\lambda)=1/\lambda$, so the standard error is $\sqrt{\hat\lambda/286}$.
   (b) There are 175 such days, so $\hat\lambda=175/286=0.61189$ and the standard error is 0.04625.
   (c) The counts' own standard deviation gives 0.0803, $\sqrt{3.0167}=1.737$ times larger: their variance is 3.0167 times their mean ([[LM15]]'s 3.02). Large moves cluster, the Poisson model is wrong, and its information understates the error.

9. With $j$ up days, $\ell(\theta)=j\ln\theta+(T-j)\ln(1-\theta)$, so $\hat\theta=j/T=3{,}205/5{,}849=0.5480$ and $\mathcal I(\theta)=1/\big(\theta(1-\theta)\big)$. The standard error is $\sqrt{\hat\theta(1-\hat\theta)/T}=0.0065$, so the interval is 0.535 to 0.561. [[LM17]]'s 9,604 was a model's trial count; this is an interval for a data frequency, under iid days. [[LM21]] tests $\theta=\tfrac12$.

10. At 2%, $k=252$ and $\hat\alpha=2.49$ (0.16), interval 2.2 to 2.8. At 4%, $k=44$ and $\hat\alpha=4.93$ (0.74), interval 3.5 to 6.4. If both thresholds lay in one exact power tail, the intervals would estimate the same $\alpha$. Losses near 2% belong to the body, which biases the low estimate down; the high one has the larger variance. Clustered losses make both iid standard errors too small (Section 8), so the intervals are also too narrow.

11. The 1.96 interval covers in 84.3% of samples (Monte Carlo standard error 0.26) and the $t_4$ interval in 94.8% (0.16). The exact coverage of the first is $2F_{t_4}(1.96\sqrt{4/5})-1=84.6\%$. Before rounding, the gap is 0.23 points (84.55% against 84.32%), about one Monte Carlo standard error: chance. The $t_4$ interval's 94.77% lies about one and a half standard errors from 95%, also chance.

12. **A** is correct. With $\mathrm{SR}=0.5/\sqrt{246.12}$ a day, (20.9) for normal days gives an annual standard error of $\sqrt{(1+\mathrm{SR}^2/2)/\tau}$, which is 0.1 at $\tau=100.05$ years. B forgets the square root, $1/0.1$. C takes 0.1 as the half-width of the interval, $1.96^2/0.1^2$.

## Glossary {-}

| Term | 中文 | Meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Estimator | 估計量 | A function of the sample that estimates a parameter; its value on the data is the estimate. |
| Sampling distribution | 抽樣分布 | The distribution of an estimator across samples. |
| Bias | 偏誤 | $\E\hat\theta-\theta$. |
| Unbiased estimator | 不偏估計量 | An estimator with bias 0. |
| Mean squared error | 均方誤差 | $\E[(\hat\theta-\theta)^2]$, variance plus squared bias, (20.1). |
| Consistent estimator | 一致估計量 | An estimator that converges in probability to the parameter. |
| Standard error | 標準誤 | The standard deviation of an estimator, (20.2) for a mean. |
| Confidence interval | 信賴區間 | An interval rule that covers the parameter with a stated probability across samples, (20.4). |
| Likelihood function | 概度函數 | $\mathcal L(\theta)$, the density of the observed sample as a function of the parameters. |
| Maximum likelihood estimation | 最大概度估計 | Choosing the parameters that maximize the likelihood, (20.5). |
| Fisher information | 費雪訊息 | $\mathcal I(\theta)$, the expected curvature of one observation's log-likelihood. |
| Observed information matrix | 觀察訊息矩陣 | $-H$, the negative Hessian of $\ell$ at the estimate; its inverse estimates the covariance, (20.6). |
| Asymptotic normality | 漸近常態性 | The approximately normal law of an estimator in large samples, (20.6). |
| Profile likelihood | 剖面概度 | The likelihood of one parameter, maximized over the others at each of its values; its logarithm is the profile log-likelihood. |
| Cramér–Rao lower bound | 克萊姆-饒歐下界 | $1/(T\mathcal I)$, below which no unbiased estimator's variance falls, (20.7). |
| Efficiency | 效率 | Precision of an estimator relative to the Cramér–Rao bound or to another estimator. |
| Delta method | delta 法 | Standard errors of smooth functions by a first-order expansion, (20.8). |
| Pareto distribution | 帕累托分布 | The exact power tail $\Pr(X>x\mid X\ge x_0)=(x/x_0)^{-\alpha}$. |
| Hill estimator | 希爾估計量 | The Pareto MLE of a tail index from the losses beyond a threshold, (20.10). |
