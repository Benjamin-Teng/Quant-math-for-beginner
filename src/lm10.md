## Introduction

[[LM7]] measured the risk of two assets with two variances and a covariance. A book of $N$ assets needs $N$ variances and $N(N-1)/2$ covariances, kept in a matrix. Its variance is a quadratic form in the weights. This module builds the sample covariance matrix from returns. It shows why the variance it gives is never negative, and how a risk system can still print a negative one.

> [!case] Twenty-eight volatilities, one number
> A desk holds U28, the 28 leaf sector indices that FinMind serves daily, in equal weights and rebalances the book every day. Over 4,382 daily returns, from 2 December 2008 to 8 October 2026, the sectors' annualized volatilities run from 13.67% (CommunicationsInternet) to 35.31% (GlassCeramic). They average 21.94% ([[ex:vols]]).
>
> The book's volatility was 15.75%, below every sector but one and below TAIEX's 17.82%. The 28 variance terms alone would give a volatility of only 4.22%. Which numbers account for the rest of the book's variance? A $28\times28$ covariance matrix holds 406 distinct entries. Why can the answer never fall below zero, and how can a risk system still print a negative variance?

## Quadratic Forms and Symmetric Matrices {lo=1}

Section 3 builds a covariance matrix from daily returns with (10.2). For S3, on the 4,382 returns of the case and annualized, the matrix is, in squared percentage points (%²),

$$A\hat\Sigma_3=\begin{pmatrix}570.34&261.83&162.26\\261.83&363.73&191.35\\162.26&191.35&283.54\end{pmatrix}.$$

Put a vector of positions on both sides of $A\hat\Sigma_3$. For $\mathbf e_1=(1,0,0)^{\top}$, all in Semiconductor, $\mathbf e_1^{\top}(A\hat\Sigma_3)\mathbf e_1$ selects the entry 570.34, a volatility of $\sqrt{570.34}=23.88\%$. For $(1,-1,0)^{\top}$, long Semiconductor and short FinancialInsurance, it gives $570.34+363.73-2(261.83)=410.41$, or 20.26%. For the mix $(0.5,0.3,0.2)^{\top}$ it gives 320.6, or 17.91%. Each result is the variance of a daily-rebalanced book, as Section 4 proves.

> [!definition] Quadratic form
> Let $M$ be an $N\times N$ matrix. The **quadratic form** of $M$ is the function
> $$h(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^{\top}M\mathbf x=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_{ij}x_ix_j,$$
> for $\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^N$.

The double sum meets $m_{ij}$ and $m_{ji}$ with the same product $x_ix_j$, so only their sum matters:

$$\begin{gathered}\mathbf x^{\top}M\mathbf x=\mathbf x^{\top}M_{\text{sym}}\mathbf x,\\ M_{\text{sym}}:=\tfrac12\big(M+M^{\top}\big).\end{gathered} \tag{10.1}$$

In matrix language, $\mathbf x^{\top}M\mathbf x$ is a number, so it equals its own transpose $\mathbf x^{\top}M^{\top}\mathbf x$, and (10.1) averages the two. From here on, a quadratic form has a symmetric matrix unless a sentence says otherwise. The form recovers its entries: $h(\mathbf e_i)=m_{ii}$, and $h(\mathbf e_i+\mathbf e_j)=m_{ii}+m_{jj}+2m_{ij}$ then gives $m_{ij}$.

The level curve $h(\mathbf x)=1$ of a form in two variables shows its type ([[ex:levels]]). A form that is positive away from $\mathbf 0$ gives an ellipse. A form that is never negative but vanishes along a line gives two parallel lines, and a form that takes both signs gives a hyperbola. The test (7.4) of [[LM7]] is the case $N=2$: completing the square shows that $m_{11}>0$ and $\det M>0$ make the form positive.

> [!example] The cross term decides {#cross}
> Let
> $$M=\begin{pmatrix}1&4\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$
> The matrix $M$ has positive diagonal entries and $\det M=1>0$, so a careless reading of (7.4) calls the form positive. Evaluate $h$ at $(1,-1)^{\top}$, and test $M_{\text{sym}}$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The form is $h(\mathbf x)=x_1^2+4x_1x_2+x_2^2$, so $h(1,-1)=1-4+1=-2<0$. The symmetric part is
> > $$\begin{gathered}M_{\text{sym}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix},\\ \det M_{\text{sym}}=1-4=-3<0,\end{gathered}$$
> > and by (7.4) the form takes both signs. The test reads a symmetric matrix; the determinant of $M$ itself says nothing about $h$.

> [!kc]
> Write $h(\mathbf x)=3x_1^2-4x_1x_2+x_2^2$ as $\mathbf x^{\top}M\mathbf x$ with a symmetric $M$, and decide its type.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > The diagonal is $m_{11}=3$ and $m_{22}=1$, and $m_{12}=m_{21}=-2$, half the coefficient of $x_1x_2$. Then $\det M=3-4=-1<0$, so the form takes both signs: $h(1,0)=3$ but $h(1,2)=3-8+4=-1$.

## The Sample Covariance Matrix {lo=2}

> [!example] A covariance matrix by hand {#byhand}
> Semiconductor and FinancialInsurance fell together after the 2025 Qingming holiday. The table gives their daily returns $R_{1,t}$ and $R_{2,t}$ on 7, 8 and 9 April 2025 and the deviations $\tilde x_{tj}=R_{j,t}-\bar R_j$ from the mean. The first return runs from the close of 2 April. Compute the sample covariance matrix, with divisor $T=3$, and the correlation.
>
> | Date | $R_{1,t}$ (%) | $R_{2,t}$ (%) | $\tilde x_{t1}$ | $\tilde x_{t2}$ |
> |:--|--:|--:|--:|--:|
> | 7 Apr | −9.95 | −9.89 | −3.8133 | −4.90 |
> | 8 Apr | −3.99 | +0.53 | 2.1467 | 5.52 |
> | 9 Apr | −4.47 | −5.61 | 1.6667 | −0.62 |
> | Mean | −6.1367 | −4.99 | 0 | 0 |
>
> > [!solution]
> > Each entry of $\hat\Sigma$ averages products of deviations over the three dates, in %²:
> >
> > | Date | $\tilde x_{t1}^2$ | $\tilde x_{t1}\tilde x_{t2}$ | $\tilde x_{t2}^2$ |
> > |:--|--:|--:|--:|
> > | 7 Apr | 14.5413 | 18.6852 | 24.0100 |
> > | 8 Apr | 4.6083 | 11.8498 | 30.4704 |
> > | 9 Apr | 2.7779 | −1.0334 | 0.3844 |
> > | Mean: $\hat\sigma_{11}$, $\hat\sigma_{12}$, $\hat\sigma_{22}$ | 7.3092 | 9.8339 | 18.2883 |
> >
> > So the correlation is
> > $$\rho=\frac{9.8339}{\sqrt{7.3092\times18.2883}}=0.851 .$$
> > Three days of a sell-off give 0.851; the 4,382 returns of the sample give 0.575 ([[eg:tocorr]]).

Stack the $T$ daily return vectors as the rows of a $T\times N$ matrix $X$. Row $t$ is $\mathbf R_t^{\top}$, and column $j$ holds asset $j$'s returns ([[LM8]]). The sample means form the vector $\bar{\mathbf R}=X^{\top}\mathbf 1/T$, and subtracting it from every row centres the data: $\tilde X=X-\mathbf 1\bar{\mathbf R}^{\top}$. [[eg:byhand]] averaged products of centred columns. In matrix form,

$$\hat\Sigma=\frac1T\tilde X^{\top}\tilde X=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\mathbf R_t-\bar{\mathbf R})(\mathbf R_t-\bar{\mathbf R})^{\top}. \tag{10.2}$$

The first form takes dot products of columns: entry $(i,j)$ is centred column $i$ dotted with centred column $j$, divided by $T$. The second form adds $T$ matrices, one for each date. Both give the same entries,

$$\hat\sigma_{ij}=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}(R_{i,t}-\bar R_i)(R_{j,t}-\bar R_j),$$

so the matrix is symmetric, and its diagonal holds the variances $s_i^2$. [[ex:shapes]] follows the shapes for U28. The matrix $\hat\Sigma$ summarizes the sample; the covariance matrix of a random vector, which it estimates, is defined in [[LM16]].

> [!exhibit] From returns to the sample covariance matrix, U28 {#shapes}
> | Object | Shape | Entry | Meaning |
> |:--|:--|:--|:--|
> | $X$ | $4{,}382\times28$ | $x_{tj}=R_{j,t}$ | return of sector $j$ on date $t$ |
> | $\bar{\mathbf R}=X^{\top}\mathbf 1/T$ | $28\times1$ | $\bar R_j$ | sample mean of sector $j$ |
> | $\tilde X=X-\mathbf 1\bar{\mathbf R}^{\top}$ | $4{,}382\times28$ | $\tilde x_{tj}=x_{tj}-\bar R_j$ | deviation from the mean; every column sums to 0 |
> | $\hat\Sigma=\tilde X^{\top}\tilde X/T$ | $28\times28$ | $\hat\sigma_{ij}$ | 28 variances and 378 covariances: 406 distinct entries |
> | $C$ | $28\times28$ | $\hat\sigma_{ij}/(s_is_j)$ | 378 distinct correlations, and 1 on the diagonal |
>
> Note: daily simple price returns, 2008-12-02 to 2026-10-08, with divisor $T=4{,}382$.

Dividing entry $(i,j)$ by $s_is_j$ gives the correlation matrix, and multiplying reverses the step:

$$\begin{gathered}C=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf s)^{-1}\,\hat\Sigma\,\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf s)^{-1},\\ \hat\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf s)\,C\,\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf s).\end{gathered} \tag{10.3}$$

A diagonal matrix on the left scales the rows, and on the right it scales the columns. Entry $(i,j)$ of $C$ is also the cosine of the angle between centred columns $i$ and $j$ ([[LM8]], (8.9)). Annualizing multiplies every entry of $\hat\Sigma$ by $A$ and every $s_i$ by $\sqrt A$, so it leaves $C$ unchanged. [[ex:corr]] shows $C$ for U28.

> [!example] From the covariance matrix to the correlation matrix {#tocorr}
> Find the volatilities and the correlation matrix of S3 from $A\hat\Sigma_3$ of Section 2.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The volatilities are the square roots of the diagonal: $\sqrt{570.34}=23.88\%$, $\sqrt{363.73}=19.07\%$ and $\sqrt{283.54}=16.84\%$. Dividing each off-diagonal entry by its two volatilities, for example $261.83/(23.88\times19.07)=0.575$, gives
> > $$C_3=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.575&0.403\\0.575&1&0.596\\0.403&0.596&1\end{pmatrix}.$$
> > The way back multiplies: $23.88\times19.07\times0.575=261.85$, close to 261.83 but not equal, because each factor was rounded. Practice Problem 4 rebuilds the whole matrix this way.

> [!pitfall] Divisor and orientation in software
> `np.cov` treats each row as a variable and, by default, divides by the number of observations minus 1. On a $T\times N$ array of returns, pass `rowvar=False, ddof=0`: with `rowvar=False` alone the divisor is $T-1$. Dividing by $T-1$ removes a small bias under assumptions that [[LM20]] states. With $T=4{,}382$ it multiplies a volatility by $\sqrt{T/(T-1)}=1.000114$: a relative change of 0.0114%, not 0.0114 percentage points.

> [!zh] 二次形式與樣本共變異數矩陣
> 二次形式（quadratic form）$\mathbf x^{\top}M\mathbf x$ 就是把每一格 $m_{ij}$ 乘上 $x_ix_j$ 再全部加總，所以只有對稱部分（symmetric part）有作用，判斷正負時一定要先把矩陣對稱化。樣本共變異數矩陣（sample covariance matrix）$\hat\Sigma$ 的第 $(i,j)$ 格，是中心化（centring）後第 $i$ 行與第 $j$ 行的內積（dot product）再除以 $T$，對角線是變異數（variance），其餘是共變異數（covariance）。28 個類股指數一共有 406 個不同的數字。每格除以兩個標準差（standard deviation）就得到相關矩陣（correlation matrix），乘回去又回到共變異數矩陣；年化只改變單位，不改變相關係數（correlation coefficient）。

> [!kc]
> A return matrix has $T=250$ rows and $N=5$ columns. Give the shapes of $\bar{\mathbf R}$, $\tilde X$, $\hat\Sigma$ and $\tilde X\tilde X^{\top}$. Which one is the covariance matrix, and how many distinct entries does it have?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > The mean vector $\bar{\mathbf R}$ is $5\times1$, the centred matrix $\tilde X$ is $250\times5$, and $\hat\Sigma=\tilde X^{\top}\tilde X/T$ is $5\times5$, with $5+10=15$ distinct entries. The product $\tilde X\tilde X^{\top}$ is $250\times250$: it compares dates, not assets, and it is not a covariance matrix.

## Portfolio Variance as a Quadratic Form {lo=3}

Hold weights $\mathbf w$ that sum to 1, and rebalance to them every day. The book's daily returns are then the vector $X\mathbf w$ ([[LM8]], (8.12)), with mean $\bar{\mathbf R}^{\top}\mathbf w$, so centring gives $\tilde X\mathbf w$. Its variance with divisor $T$ is

$$\begin{aligned}s_p^2&=\frac1T(\tilde X\mathbf w)^{\top}(\tilde X\mathbf w)\\&=\mathbf w^{\top}\Big(\frac1T\tilde X^{\top}\tilde X\Big)\mathbf w=\mathbf w^{\top}\hat\Sigma\mathbf w.\end{aligned} \tag{10.4}$$

The identity is exact: the sample variance of the rebalanced book equals the quadratic form of $\hat\Sigma$ at $\mathbf w$. It needs constant weights. A buy-and-hold book's weights drift with prices ([[LM9]]), so its returns are not $X\mathbf w$ for one $\mathbf w$. The first expression in (10.4) is a squared length divided by $T$, so

$$\mathbf w^{\top}\hat\Sigma\mathbf w=\frac{\lVert\tilde X\mathbf w\rVert^2}{T}\ge0. \tag{10.5}$$

In full, (10.4) is the double sum $\sum_i\sum_jw_iw_j\hat\sigma_{ij}$, with $N$ variance terms $w_i^2s_i^2$ on the diagonal and $N(N-1)$ covariance terms off it. For two assets it is the variance of [[LM7]], $w_1^2\hat\sigma_{11}+2w_1w_2\hat\sigma_{12}+w_2^2\hat\sigma_{22}$.

> [!example] A three-sector mix {#mix}
> Find the annualized volatility of the S3 mix $\mathbf w=(0.5,0.3,0.2)^{\top}$, rebalanced daily, and compare it with the weighted average of the three volatilities.
>
> > [!solution]
> > [[ex:grid]] lists the nine terms $Aw_iw_j\hat\sigma_{ij}$, which sum to 320.62 (%²), so the volatility is $\sqrt{320.62}=17.91\%$. The weighted average of the volatilities is $0.5(23.88)+0.3(19.07)+0.2(16.84)=21.03\%$. The mix is 3.12 percentage points less volatile, because every correlation is below 1.

The gap is general. For non-negative weights, the triangle inequality (8.8) gives $s_p\le\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf s$, which [[LM8]] states as (8.11): a long-only book is never more volatile than the weighted average of its parts.

> |:--|--:|--:|--:|
> | Semiconductor | 142.58 | 39.27 | 16.23 |
> | FinancialInsurance | 39.27 | 32.74 | 11.48 |
> | Food | 16.23 | 11.48 | 11.34 |
>
> Note: entry $(i,j)$ is $Aw_iw_j\hat\sigma_{ij}$, computed from unrounded entries; the nine sum to 320.62. The rounded matrix of Section 2 gives 320.63.

With equal weights $w_i=1/N$, the double sum splits into the $N$ diagonal entries and the $N(N-1)$ others:

$$s_p^2=\frac1{N^2}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\hat\sigma_{ij}=\frac{\overline{\operatorname{var}}}{N}+\Big(1-\frac1N\Big)\,\overline{\operatorname{cov}}. \tag{10.6}$$

As $N$ grows, the first term shrinks like $1/N$, while the second approaches $\overline{\operatorname{cov}}$.

> [!example] Equal weights on 28 sectors {#equal}
> In the case, the 28 daily variances average $\overline{\operatorname{var}}=2.0328\times10^{-4}$, and the 756 off-diagonal entries average $\overline{\operatorname{cov}}=9.7254\times10^{-5}$. Find the book's volatility and the share of its variance that comes from covariances. Then find the limit of (10.6) as $N$ grows with both averages held fixed.
>
> > [!solution]
> > By (10.6), the daily variance is
> > $$\begin{aligned}s_p^2&=\frac{2.0328\times10^{-4}}{28}\\&\quad+\frac{27}{28}\times9.7254\times10^{-5}\\&=7.260\times10^{-6}+9.378\times10^{-5}\\&=1.0104\times10^{-4}.\end{aligned}$$
> > Annualized, $As_p^2=0.024803$, and the volatility is $\sqrt{0.024803}=15.75\%$.
> >
> > The covariance term is $9.378/10.104=92.8\%$ of the variance, and the variance terms are the other 7.2%. On their own they would give a volatility of $\sqrt{A\times7.260\times10^{-6}}=4.22\%$, the case's 4.22%. Volatilities do not add, so 4.22% is not a part of 15.75%; the split holds for the variance only.
> >
> > With both averages fixed, $s_p^2$ tends to $\overline{\operatorname{cov}}$, so the volatility tends to $\sqrt{A\,\overline{\operatorname{cov}}}=15.45\%$. This limit is a thought experiment: each sector added to a real book brings its own variance and covariances.

> [!pitfall] A split of the form, not of the assets
> The diagonal and off-diagonal terms split the quadratic form, not the risk of each asset. Splitting $s_p^2$ into one share per asset, and judging which split is meaningful, is the subject of [[LM27]] and [[LM31]]. This module stops at the gradient (Section 7).

## Positive Semidefinite and Positive Definite Matrices {lo=4}

Every covariance matrix gives non-negative variances. Take $C_3$ of [[eg:tocorr]], the correlation matrix of S3: at $(1,-1,0)^{\top}$ its quadratic form equals $2-2(0.575)=0.85$, and at $(0,1,-1)^{\top}$ it equals $2-2(0.596)=0.808$. Positive values at a few vectors say nothing about the others. Which symmetric matrices give a non-negative value at every vector, and how can a test settle the question for $C_3$?

> [!definition] Positive semidefinite and positive definite matrices
> A symmetric $N\times N$ matrix $M$ is **positive semidefinite** (PSD) if $\mathbf x^{\top}M\mathbf x\ge0$ for every $\mathbf x$, and **positive definite** (PD) if $\mathbf x^{\top}M\mathbf x>0$ for every $\mathbf x\ne\mathbf 0$. It is **indefinite** if its quadratic form takes both signs.

> [!theorem] The sample covariance matrix is positive semidefinite
> For every matrix of returns $X$, the matrix $\hat\Sigma$ is PSD. It is PD exactly when $\tilde X$ has full column rank, $\operatorname{rank}\tilde X=N$.

*Proof.* By (10.5), $\mathbf w^{\top}\hat\Sigma\mathbf w=\lVert\tilde X\mathbf w\rVert^2/T\ge0$, with equality exactly when $\tilde X\mathbf w=\mathbf 0$. By [[LM9]], some $\mathbf w\ne\mathbf 0$ has $\tilde X\mathbf w=\mathbf 0$ exactly when $\operatorname{rank}\tilde X<N$. $\blacksquare$

The theorem answers the case's second question: the book's variance is a squared length divided by $T$, and no squared length is negative.

Checking every $\mathbf x$ is impossible, so practice uses determinants. A **principal minor** of $M$ is the determinant of a square submatrix that keeps the same rows as columns. A **leading** principal minor keeps the first $k$ rows and columns: $\det M_k$.

> [!theorem] Tests for positive (semi)definiteness
> Let $M$ be a symmetric $N\times N$ matrix.
> 1. If $M$ is PSD, every diagonal entry is non-negative, because $m_{ii}=h(\mathbf e_i)$; if $M$ is PD, every one is positive.
> 2. The matrix is PD if and only if its leading principal minors are all positive: $\det M_k>0$ for $k=1,\dots,N$ (Sylvester's criterion).
> 3. The matrix is PD if and only if Gaussian elimination without row exchanges runs with positive pivots, $d_k>0$ for every $k$.
> 4. The matrix is PSD if and only if every principal minor is non-negative (stated without proof; Horn and Johnson 2013, ch. 7).
> 5. If $M$ is PSD, so is $K^{\top}MK$ for every matrix $K$ with $N$ rows. If $M$ is PD and $K$ is square and invertible, $K^{\top}MK$ is PD.

Tests 2 and 3 are one test: elimination does not change the leading minors, and it makes each block $M_k$ triangular, so $\det M_k=d_1\cdots d_k$ ([[LM9]]). The Deep Dive below proves them by completing squares, as (7.4) did for $N=2$. The pivots are also the squares of the diagonal entries of the Cholesky factor of [[LM12]]. A third test, through the eigenvalues of $M$, arrives in [[LM11]], which also measures how close to singular $\hat\Sigma$ is. Test 5 follows from $\mathbf x^{\top}K^{\top}MK\mathbf x=(K\mathbf x)^{\top}M(K\mathbf x)$.

Test 5 with $K=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf s)^{-1}$, applied to (10.3), gives a rule: $C$ is PSD exactly when $\hat\Sigma$ is. Rescaling cannot create or remove a negative variance, provided every $s_i>0$. [[ex:tests]] compares the tests on $C_3$.

> [!exhibit] Ways to test a symmetric matrix {#tests}
> | Test | Proves | Work for an $N\times N$ matrix | On $C_3$ |
> |:--|:--|:--|:--|
> | $\mathbf x^{\top}M\mathbf x$ at chosen vectors | not PSD, if one value is negative | one product per vector | 0.85 and 0.808 at the two vectors tried; proves nothing |
> | Diagonal entries | not PSD, if one is negative | $N$ entries | 1, 1, 1 |
> | Leading principal minors | PD, if all are positive | $N$ determinants | 1, 0.669375, 0.427966: PD |
> | Pivots of elimination | PD, if all are positive | one elimination | 1, 0.6694, 0.6394: PD |
> | All principal minors | PSD, if none is negative | $2^N-1$ determinants | seven, all positive |
> | Eigenvalues | PSD and PD | [[LM11]] | — |
> | Cholesky factor | PD, if it exists | [[LM12]] | — |

> [!example] Three tests on one matrix {#three}
> Test $C_3$ of [[eg:tocorr]], with its entries as printed, by leading principal minors and by pivots.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The leading minors are 1, then $1-0.575^2=0.669375$, then the full determinant. Expanding along the first row,
> > $$\begin{aligned}\det C_3&=1+2(0.575)(0.403)(0.596)\\&\quad-0.575^2-0.403^2-0.596^2\\&=0.427966 .\end{aligned}$$
> > All three are positive, so $C_3$ is PD. The pivots are the ratios 1, $0.669375/1=0.6694$ and $0.427966/0.669375=0.6394$. Elimination gives the same numbers: subtracting 0.575 times row 1 from row 2 leaves $1-0.575^2$ in position $(2,2)$.

> [!example] When leading minors mislead {#minors}
> (a) Test $\operatorname{diag}(0,-1)$. (b) Round the Semiconductor and FinancialInsurance block of $A\hat\Sigma_3$ to whole %², and add their 50/50 mix, rebalanced daily, as a third asset. The mix's covariances are averages of the two rows, and its variance is $\tfrac14(570)+\tfrac12(262)+\tfrac14(364)$, so
> $$M=\begin{pmatrix}570&262&416\\262&364&313\\416&313&364.5\end{pmatrix}.$$
> Test $M$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > (a) The leading minors are 0 and 0, both non-negative, yet $h(\mathbf e_2)=-1$. The principal minor that keeps only row and column 2 is $-1<0$, and test 4 catches it.
> >
> > (b) The $1\times1$ principal minors are 570, 364 and 364.5. The $2\times2$ ones are $570(364)-262^2=138{,}836$, $570(364.5)-416^2=34{,}709$ and $364(364.5)-313^2=34{,}709$. The determinant is exactly 0, because column 3 is half the sum of columns 1 and 2. All seven minors are non-negative, so $M$ is PSD, but it is not PD: $\mathbf x=(0.5,0.5,-1)^{\top}$, half in each sector and short the mix, gives $\mathbf x^{\top}M\mathbf x=0$.

> [!deepdive]- Positive pivots make a matrix positive definite
> Let $M$ be symmetric with $m_{11}>0$, and write $\mathbf y=(x_2,\dots,x_N)^{\top}$. Completing the square in $x_1$ gives
> $$\begin{aligned}\mathbf x^{\top}M\mathbf x&=m_{11}\Big(x_1+\sum_{j\ge2}\frac{m_{1j}}{m_{11}}x_j\Big)^2\\&\quad+\mathbf y^{\top}K\mathbf y,\\ k_{ij}&=m_{ij}-\frac{m_{i1}m_{1j}}{m_{11}}\quad(i,j\ge2).\end{aligned}$$
> Here $K$ is the $(N-1)\times(N-1)$ matrix that the first step of elimination leaves, and its first diagonal entry is the second pivot. If $K$ is PD and $\mathbf x\ne\mathbf 0$, then either $\mathbf y\ne\mathbf 0$ and the second term is positive, or $\mathbf y=\mathbf 0$, which forces $x_1\ne0$ and makes the first term positive. So $m_{11}>0$ and $K$ PD make $M$ PD.
>
> Conversely, if $M$ is PD, then $m_{11}=h(\mathbf e_1)>0$. Choosing $x_1$ to make the bracket zero shows $\mathbf y^{\top}K\mathbf y=h(\mathbf x)>0$ for every $\mathbf y\ne\mathbf 0$, so $K$ is PD. Repeating the step on $K$ shows that $M$ is PD exactly when every pivot is positive. Because $\det M_k=d_1\cdots d_k$, that happens exactly when every leading principal minor is positive (Strang 2016, §6.5). $\blacksquare$

> [!zh] 半正定與正定的檢驗
> 樣本共變異數矩陣（sample covariance matrix）一定是半正定（positive semidefinite），因為 $\mathbf w^{\top}\hat\Sigma\mathbf w$ 是一個向量（vector）長度的平方除以 $T$，不可能小於 0；中心化（centring）後的報酬矩陣若行滿秩（full column rank），樣本共變異數矩陣就是正定（positive definite）。實務上用行列式（determinant）檢驗：所有首主子行列式（leading principal minor）都大於 0，或高斯消去法（Gaussian elimination）的每個樞軸元（pivot）都大於 0，就是正定。半正定則要檢查所有主子式（principal minor），只看左上角的那幾個，會被 $\operatorname{diag}(0,-1)$ 騙過。只要 $K$ 可逆（invertible），把矩陣換成 $K^{\top}MK$ 就不改變正定性與半正定性，所以相關矩陣（correlation matrix）和共變異數矩陣同時是或同時不是半正定。

> [!kc]
> A symmetric $2\times2$ matrix has $m_{11}=2$, $m_{22}=4$ and $m_{12}=3$. Is it PSD? Find a vector that shows the answer.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > No. Its determinant is $8-9=-1<0$, a negative principal minor. Completing the square as in (7.4) suggests $\mathbf x=(3,-2)^{\top}$, and indeed $h(\mathbf x)=2(9)+2(3)(3)(-2)+4(4)=-2<0$.

## When $\hat\Sigma$ Fails: Singularity and Pairwise Deletion {lo=5}

Centring costs one dimension. Every column of $\tilde X$ sums to zero, $\mathbf 1^{\top}\tilde X=\mathbf 0^{\top}$, so the $T$ rows of $\tilde X$ are linearly dependent and $\operatorname{rank}\tilde X\le T-1$. The matrices $\hat\Sigma$ and $\tilde X$ have the same null space: $\tilde X\mathbf w=\mathbf 0$ gives $\hat\Sigma\mathbf w=\mathbf 0$, and $\hat\Sigma\mathbf w=\mathbf 0$ gives $\lVert\tilde X\mathbf w\rVert^2=T\,\mathbf w^{\top}\hat\Sigma\mathbf w=0$. By (9.7) of [[LM9]] they have the same rank, so

$$\operatorname{rank}\hat\Sigma=\operatorname{rank}\tilde X\le\min(N,\,T-1). \tag{10.7}$$

With $T\le N$ returns, $\hat\Sigma$ is singular whatever the data.

> [!example] Twenty-eight sectors, twenty-eight days {#shortT}
> Compute $\hat\Sigma$ for U28 from the latest $T$ returns, for $T=20,\dots,35$. When does it become PD?
>
> > [!solution]
> > By (10.7) the rank is at most $T-1$ while $T\le28$, and [[ex:rank]] shows that it reaches that bound. On the latest 28 returns, from 28 August to 8 October 2026, the rank is 27, and $\hat\Sigma$ is singular. On the latest 29, from 27 August, it is 28: full rank, so $\hat\Sigma$ is PD.

Collinear returns also make $\hat\Sigma$ singular. Add to S5 a sixth column: the S5 equal-weight book, rebalanced daily. > [!pitfall] Rounding and floating point
> A matrix rounded entry by entry can stop being PSD. The covariance matrix of Semiconductor, FinancialInsurance and their 50/50 mix, computed from the data, is singular. Annualize it in %², like $A\hat\Sigma_3$, and round it to one decimal: its upper triangle reads 570.3, 261.8, 416.1; 363.7, 312.8; 364.4. Its determinant is then $-13{,}888.91$, a negative principal minor. Build a printed matrix from printed inputs, as [[eg:minors]] did.
>
> In code, an exact zero appears as a tiny residue that differs between machines, so compare with a tolerance. Its size scales with the entries: below $10^{-15}$ for the daily $6\times6$ matrix above, larger for the 50/50 matrix in %². `matrix_rank` decides rank with a tolerance too, which [[LM12]] explains.

### Pairwise deletion

Series that start on different dates force a choice. Add 0050, 00631L and 00679B to U28. It keeps more data, but it mixes samples.

The ETF returns here are one-session total returns: from one trading date to the next, with both closes present and cash dividends added. ## Matrix Calculus: Gradients, the Hessian and Convexity {lo=6,7}

[[LM7]] differentiated the two-asset variance and found the Hessian $H=2\Sigma$. The same calculus works for $N$ assets, one coordinate at a time. For a vector $\mathbf a$, the partial derivative $\partial(\mathbf a^{\top}\mathbf w)/\partial w_i$ is $a_i$. In $\mathbf w^{\top}M\mathbf w$, the variable $w_i$ appears in row $i$ and in column $i$ of the double sum, so its partial derivative is $\sum_jm_{ij}w_j+\sum_jm_{ji}w_j$. Stacked into vectors,

$$\begin{gathered}\nabla(\mathbf a^{\top}\mathbf w)=\mathbf a,\qquad H=M+M^{\top},\\ \nabla(\mathbf w^{\top}M\mathbf w)=(M+M^{\top})\,\mathbf w.\end{gathered} \tag{10.8}$$

For symmetric $M$ the gradient is $2M\mathbf w$ and the Hessian is $2M$. For $s_p^2$ they are $2\hat\Sigma\mathbf w$ and $2\hat\Sigma$: the Hessian $H=2\Sigma$ of [[LM7]], now for $N$ assets.

The third derivatives of a quadratic function are zero, so the expansion (7.2) of [[LM7]] ends at the second order with no remainder. For symmetric $M$, expanding the products gives

$$h(\mathbf w+\boldsymbol\Delta)=h(\mathbf w)+2\boldsymbol\Delta^{\top}M\mathbf w+\boldsymbol\Delta^{\top}M\boldsymbol\Delta. \tag{10.9}$$

The middle term, $2\boldsymbol\Delta^{\top}M\mathbf w=\boldsymbol\Delta^{\top}\nabla h(\mathbf w)$, is the gradient's linear prediction, and the last term corrects it exactly.

A fully invested book trades weight for weight, so $\mathbf 1^{\top}\boldsymbol\Delta=0$. Adding the same number to every entry of the gradient $\nabla h(\mathbf w)$ then leaves the linear term $\boldsymbol\Delta^{\top}\nabla h(\mathbf w)$ unchanged: only differences of entries matter. For the variance, a switch from asset $j$ to asset $i$ lowers $s_p^2$ to first order when $\partial s_p^2/\partial w_i<\partial s_p^2/\partial w_j$.

> [!example] Switching weight from one sector to another {#switch}
> In the equal-weight U28 book, move $\tfrac1{28}$ of capital from GlassCeramic, the most volatile sector, to CommunicationsInternet, the least volatile. So $\boldsymbol\Delta$ has $\tfrac1{28}$ in CommunicationsInternet, $-\tfrac1{28}$ in GlassCeramic and 0 elsewhere. Use the annualized gradient $2A\hat\Sigma\mathbf w$ and (10.9) to find the new variance and volatility.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The GlassCeramic entry of the gradient is 0.072303, the largest of the 28, and the CommunicationsInternet entry is 0.027641, the smallest. The linear term is
> > $$\begin{aligned}2A\boldsymbol\Delta^{\top}\hat\Sigma\mathbf w&=\frac{0.027641-0.072303}{28}\\&=-0.0015951.\end{aligned}$$
> > The quadratic term, $A\boldsymbol\Delta^{\top}\hat\Sigma\boldsymbol\Delta=+0.0001364$, uses the two variances and their covariance. So the annualized variance of 0.0248034 becomes
> > $$\begin{aligned}&0.0248034-0.0015951+0.0001364\\&=0.0233447,\end{aligned}$$
> > and the sample volatility would have fallen from 15.75% to 15.28%. The result is exact; the linear term alone would give 15.23%.

The gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent and is perpendicular to the level curve through its point ([[LM7]]). [[ex:gradient]] draws it for two sectors. The pitfall of Section 4 applies to it too: entry $i$ of $2\hat\Sigma\mathbf w$ is a rate of change, not asset $i$'s share of the risk ([[LM27]], [[LM31]]).

For symmetric $M$, any $\mathbf x$, $\mathbf y$ and $\omega\in[0,1]$, expanding the products gives

$$\begin{aligned}h\big(\omega\mathbf x+(1-\omega)\mathbf y\big)&=\omega h(\mathbf x)+(1-\omega)h(\mathbf y)\\&\quad-\omega(1-\omega)\,h(\mathbf x-\mathbf y).\end{aligned} \tag{10.10}$$

> [!theorem] Convexity of a quadratic function
> Let $M$ be symmetric and $h(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^{\top}M\mathbf x$. Then $h$ is convex exactly when $M$ is PSD, and strictly convex exactly when $M$ is PD.

*Proof.* If $M$ is PSD, the last term of (10.10) is not positive, so $h(\omega\mathbf x+(1-\omega)\mathbf y)\le\omega h(\mathbf x)+(1-\omega)h(\mathbf y)$: the chord lies above the graph. If $M$ is PD, $\mathbf x\ne\mathbf y$ and $0<\omega<1$, the inequality is strict. The converse is in the Deep Dive. $\blacksquare$

> [!deepdive]- A convex quadratic function has a PSD matrix
> Let $h$ be convex. For every $\mathbf x$, the midpoint of $\mathbf x$ and $-\mathbf x$ is $\mathbf 0$, so
> $$0=h(\mathbf 0)\le\tfrac12h(\mathbf x)+\tfrac12h(-\mathbf x)=h(\mathbf x),$$
> and $M$ is PSD. If $h$ is strictly convex and $\mathbf x\ne\mathbf 0$, the inequality is strict, and $M$ is PD.

Portfolio variance $\mathbf w^{\top}\hat\Sigma\mathbf w$ is therefore convex, with Hessian $2\hat\Sigma$. For functions that are not quadratic, the same conclusion needs the argument of [[LM29]]. Finding the portfolio of least variance is the subject of [[LM28]]; this module stops at the gradient.

> [!zh] 矩陣微積分與凸性
> 對 $\mathbf w^{\top}M\mathbf w$ 逐一對 $w_i$ 求偏導數（partial derivative），$w_i$ 同時出現在第 $i$ 列與第 $i$ 行，所以梯度（gradient）是 $(M+M^{\top})\mathbf w$，矩陣對稱（symmetric）時就是 $2M\mathbf w$，海森矩陣（Hessian）是 $2M$。二次函數（quadratic function）的三階導數（third derivative）全為 0，泰勒展開（Taylor expansion）到二次就完全精確：把 1/28 的資金從玻璃陶瓷移到通信網路，樣本期間的波動率會從 15.75% 降到 15.28%，一點誤差都沒有。總權重不變的換股只看梯度分量的差。二次函數是凸函數（convex function）若且唯若矩陣半正定（positive semidefinite），這是 [[LM28]] 與 [[LM29]] 求解最佳化的基礎。

## Quadratic Forms and Covariance in Python {lo=8}

The code below builds $\hat\Sigma$ and $C$ from a matrix of returns, checks (10.4), and tests a matrix by its pivots.

```python
import numpy as np

# X: T x N array of daily returns, rows = dates (Part 1 of the lab)
T, N = X.shape
Xc = X - X.mean(axis=0)               # centred: every column sums to 0
S = Xc.T @ Xc / T                     # (10.2), N x N
assert np.allclose(S, np.cov(X, rowvar=False, ddof=0))
s = np.sqrt(np.diag(S))
C = S / np.outer(s, s)                # (10.3)

w = np.full(N, 1 / N)                 # equal weights, rebalanced daily
print(np.var(X @ w), w @ S @ w)       # (10.4): equal up to rounding

def pivots(M):
    """Pivots of elimination without row exchanges (test 3)."""
    M = np.array(M, dtype=float)
    out = []
    for k in range(len(M)):
        out.append(M[k, k])
        if M[k, k] <= 0:
            break                     # not PD: stop at this pivot
        M[k + 1:] -= np.outer(M[k + 1:, k] / M[k, k], M[k])
    return np.array(out)

print(pivots(C).min() > 0, np.linalg.matrix_rank(S))
# a pivot that is 0 in exact arithmetic can appear slightly negative:
# judge its sign with a tolerance (LM12)

# pairwise deletion with missing values (NaN): one mask per pair
ok = ~np.isnan(X)
both = ok[:, 0] & ok[:, 1]            # dates on which assets 1 and 2 trade
a, b = X[both, 0], X[both, 1]
cov_12 = np.mean((a - a.mean()) * (b - b.mean()))   # T_12 dates
```

Software differs in orientation, divisor and missing values, and [[ex:python]] lists the calls this module uses. Polars computes `pl.cov` and `pl.corr` on the rows where both columns have values, which is pairwise deletion without a warning.

## Summary {-}

- A quadratic form $\mathbf x^{\top}M\mathbf x$ depends only on the symmetric part of $M$ (10.1); definiteness is tested on $M_{\text{sym}}$.
- The sample covariance matrix $\tilde X^{\top}\tilde X/T$ (10.2) has 406 distinct entries for U28; (10.3) converts it to correlations and back.
- A daily-rebalanced book's sample variance is exactly $\mathbf w^{\top}\hat\Sigma\mathbf w$ (10.4). In the case, covariances made 92.8% of it by (10.6). The variance terms alone, 7.2% of it, would give a volatility of only 4.22%, against the book's 15.75%.
- Every sample covariance matrix is PSD by (10.5), and PD exactly when the centred returns have full column rank.
- Positive leading principal minors or pivots prove PD; PSD needs every principal minor, and rounding can destroy it.
- The rank is at most $\min(N,T-1)$ (10.7). Pairwise deletion mixes samples, and with U28 and three ETFs it breaks PSD.
- The gradient of $\mathbf w^{\top}M\mathbf w$ is $(M+M^{\top})\mathbf w$ (10.8), the expansion (10.9) is exact, and a quadratic function is convex exactly when its matrix is PSD (10.10).

## Solutions {-}

1. **B** is correct. Its leading minors are 2, $4-1=3$ and $2(4-1)-1(2-0)=4$, all positive. Matrix $M_{\text{A}}$ has leading minors 2, 3 and $2(4-9)-1(2)=-12<0$, so it is not PD. Matrix $M_{\text{C}}$ has leading minors 1, 0 and 0, so it is not PD either. Its seven principal minors are 1, 1 and 1, then 0, 1 and 1, then 0; none is negative, so it is PSD by test 4. Indeed, its form is $(x_1+x_2)^2+x_3^2\ge0$, and $M_{\text{C}}(1,-1,0)^{\top}=\mathbf 0$, so it is singular.

2. **C** is correct. Only $M_{\text{sym}}$ matters, with $m_{11}=2$, $m_{22}=3$ and off-diagonal entries 3. Its determinant is $6-9=-3<0$, so the form takes both signs: $h(1,0)=2$ but $h(1,-1)=2-6+3=-1$. A reads the triangular $M$, whose determinant is 6.

3. **A** is correct. By (10.7) the rank is at most $\min(28,\,20-1)=19$, and [[ex:rank]] shows that U28's latest 20 returns reach it. B forgets that centring removes one dimension, and C counts only the columns.

4. By (10.3), entry $(i,j)$ is the product of the two volatilities and the correlation. The diagonal is $23.88^2=570.25$, $19.07^2=363.66$ and $16.84^2=283.59$. Off it, $23.88\times19.07\times0.575=261.85$, $23.88\times16.84\times0.403=162.06$ and $19.07\times16.84\times0.596=191.40$, in %². For $(0.2,0.3,0.5)$ the variance is
   $$\begin{aligned}&0.04(570.25)+0.09(363.66)\\&\quad+0.25(283.59)+2\big(0.06(261.85)\\&\quad+0.1(162.06)+0.15(191.40)\big)\\&=247.69,\end{aligned}$$
   and the volatility is $\sqrt{247.69}=15.74\%$.

5. By (10.6), with one fifth of $\overline{\operatorname{var}}$ and 0.8 times $\overline{\operatorname{cov}}$, the daily variance is
   $$\begin{aligned}s_p^2&=4.1480\times10^{-5}+7.2557\times10^{-5}\\&=1.14037\times10^{-4}.\end{aligned}$$
   Annualized, $245.48\times1.14037\times10^{-4}=0.027994$, a volatility of 16.73%. The limit is $\sqrt{245.48\times9.0696\times10^{-5}}=14.92\%$. Five sectors leave the book 1.81 points above its limit, and 28 sectors leave it 0.30 points above. The limit depends on the average covariance of the sectors chosen.

6. The first pivot is $d_1=570.34$. Subtract $261.83/570.34$ times row 1 from row 2, and $162.26/570.34$ times row 1 from row 3. Rows and columns 2 and 3 then hold the block $K$ of the Deep Dive in Section 5, with $k_{ij}=m_{ij}-m_{i1}m_{1j}/570.34$:
   $$K=\begin{pmatrix}243.5299&116.8602\\116.8602&237.3775\end{pmatrix}.$$
   For example, $k_{22}=363.73-261.83^2/570.34$. So $d_2=243.5299$, and one more step leaves
   $$d_3=237.3775-\frac{116.8602^2}{243.5299}=181.3010.$$
   The pivots 570.34, 243.53 and 181.30 are all positive, so the matrix is PD (test 3).

7. Because $M\operatorname{adj}M=(\det M)I$, the vector $\mathbf x$ satisfies $M\mathbf x=M\operatorname{adj}(M)\mathbf e_j=(\det M)\mathbf e_j$. So $\mathbf x^{\top}M\mathbf x=(\det M)\,\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf e_j=(\det M)\,x_j$, and $x_j=\operatorname{adj}(M)_{jj}$. That entry is the cofactor of $m_{jj}$: the $2\times2$ principal minor without row and column $j$. For the $C_{\text{pw}}$ of [[eg:negvar]] these minors are $1-0.444^2=0.802864$, $1-0.199^2=0.960399$ and $1-0.982^2=0.035676$, so the forms are $-0.374592$ times each: −0.3007, −0.3598 and −0.0134.

   By (10.9), applied to $-\tfrac12M$, the change is

   Directly, $u$ moves from 0.0925 to 0.0958. Where $\nabla u=\mathbf 0$ lie the Kelly exposures of [[LM43]].

   $$0.05\,(0.03905-0.07923)=-0.002009,$$

   $$\begin{aligned}&0.05^2\,(0.057034+0.028354\\&\quad-2\times0.016226)=+0.000132.\end{aligned}$$
   So the variance moves from 0.032063 to
   $$\begin{aligned}&0.032063-0.002009+0.000132\\&=0.030186,\end{aligned}$$

10. If $M$ is PSD, then $h(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)\ge0$, and (10.10) gives $h(\omega\mathbf x+(1-\omega)\mathbf y)\le\omega h(\mathbf x)+(1-\omega)h(\mathbf y)$ for every $\omega\in[0,1]$.
