## Introduction

A payment has a date as well as an amount: NT\$1 next year is worth less than NT\$1 today, because today's money earns interest. Discounting at a constant rate multiplies each payment by the same factor per period, so level or steadily growing payments form a **geometric series**. The sum of that series prices deposits, mortgages, bonds and dividend streams. Add one more idea, no-arbitrage, and it prices futures too.

> [!case] Why does TX trade below TAIEX every summer?
> At the close on Friday 27 June 2025, TAIEX stood at 22,580.08. Every TX futures contract settled below it: July by 458 points, December by 880 and June 2026 by 1,087 ([[ex:curve0627]]). Over June the near-month contract averaged 192 points below the index, against 65 points in April. By September the discount (逆價差) had vanished, and in October TX traded above TAIEX.
>
> Interest says the opposite should happen. A futures buyer keeps in the bank the cash that a stock buyer must spend. With the central bank's discount rate at 2%, a futures price should therefore sit *above* the index, unless dividends paid before expiry outweigh the interest.
>
> The cost-of-carry formula $F=Se^{(r-q)\tau}$ turns each price into the dividend yield $q$ the market must expect. At $r=2\%$ the December contract implied dividends worth 4.9% of the index before its expiry on 17 December. In the whole of calendar 2025, TAIEX constituents paid 2.9%.
>
> Is the discount a dividend forecast, a bearish bet or a mispricing? By Section 8 you will split each contract's discount into carry, dividends and a residual, and measure all three with TWSE's total-return index.

The tool that resolves the case is the sum of a geometric series: Sections 2 and 3 derive it and build present values and annuities. Section 4 lets payments grow, giving perpetuities and the Gordon model, and Sections 5 and 6 price bonds and loans. > [!note] Notation in this module
> $i$ is an interest or discount rate **per period** and $v:=1/(1+i)$ the one-period discount factor. $n$ counts periods, $A$ is a level payment, and $\mathrm{PV}$ and $\mathrm{FV}$ are present and future values. $y$ is a bond's yield to maturity and $k$ the required return on a stock. In Sections 4–6, $g$ is the simple growth rate of a cash flow; elsewhere in the book $g$ denotes a log growth rate.
>
> In Sections 7–8, $r$ and $q$ are continuously compounded interest and dividend yields: log rates, consistent with the book's use of $r$. $S$ is the index, $F$ the futures price, $\tau$ the time to expiry in years and $F-S$ the **basis**.

## Sequences and Geometric Series {lo=1}

A deposit earning simple interest grows by the same amount each year; one earning compound interest grows by the same factor. The first is an arithmetic sequence, the second a geometric one. Almost everything in this module is of the second kind.

> [!definition] Arithmetic and geometric sequences
> A sequence $a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots$ is **arithmetic** with common difference $d$ if $a_{k+1}=a_k+d$, so that $a_k=a_1+(k-1)d$. It is **geometric** with common ratio $x$ if $a_{k+1}=x\,a_k$, so that $a_k=a_1x^{k-1}$. A **series** is the sum of the terms of a sequence.

NT\$100 at 2% simple interest is worth 100, 102, 104, … after 0, 1, 2, … years: an arithmetic sequence with $d=2$. At 2% compound interest it is worth 100, 102, 104.04, …: a geometric sequence with $x=1.02$. The discount factors $v,v^2,v^3,\dots$ that convert future money into present money are geometric with ratio $v=1/(1+i)<1$.

### Summing an arithmetic sequence

Write the sum forwards and backwards and add the two lines term by term:

$$\begin{aligned} S_n&=a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n,\\ S_n&=a_n+a_{n-1}+\cdots+a_1 .\end{aligned}$$

Each pair $a_k+a_{n+1-k}=2a_1+(n-1)d=a_1+a_n$ has the same sum, and there are $n$ pairs, so

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k=\frac{n\,(a_1+a_n)}{2}. \tag{6.1}$$

The sum is the number of terms times the average of the first and last. Section 6 uses (6.1) for loans that repay equal principal each month, whose payments fall arithmetically.

### Summing a geometric sequence

Let $S_n=a+ax+ax^2+\cdots+ax^{n-1}$. Multiplying by $x$ shifts every term one place, $xS_n=ax+ax^2+\cdots+ax^{n}$, and subtracting cancels all but two terms: $S_n-xS_n=a-ax^n$.

> [!result] Geometric sums
> For $x\ne1$,
> $$\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a\,x^k=a\,\frac{1-x^n}{1-x}, \tag{6.2}$$
> and for $x=1$ the sum is $na$. If $|x|<1$, the infinite series converges:
> $$\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a\,x^k=\frac{a}{1-x}. \tag{6.3}$$
> If $|x|\ge1$ and $a\ne0$, the infinite series diverges.

Convergence follows from the gap between the full sum and a partial sum,

$$\frac{a}{1-x}-S_n=\frac{a\,x^n}{1-x}.$$

For $|x|<1$, $|x|^n=e^{n\ln|x|}\to0$ because $\ln|x|<0$ ([[LM1]]), so the gap closes. For $|x|\ge1$ the terms $ax^k$ do not shrink to zero, and a series whose terms do not vanish cannot settle on a finite sum.

The gap formula is useful in its own right. The fraction of an infinite geometric sum that lies beyond its first $n$ terms is exactly $x^n$. With $x=v$, that is the share of a perpetuity's value paid after year $n$.

> [!example] How much of a perpetuity is paid in the first 30 years? {#tail}
> A security pays NT\$1 at the end of every year forever. At discount rates of 2%, 5% and 8%, compute its value and the share of it paid within 10, 30 and 50 years.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The payments form a geometric series with first term $v$ and ratio $v$. By (6.3) the value is $v/(1-v)=1/i$: NT\$50.00 at 2%, NT\$20.00 at 5% and NT\$12.50 at 8%. By the gap formula the share paid after year $n$ is $v^n$, so the share paid within $n$ years is $1-v^n$:
> >
> > | Rate | Value | Within 10 years | Within 30 years | Within 50 years |
> > |--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
> > | 2% | 50.00 | 18.0% | 44.8% | 62.8% |
> > | 5% | 20.00 | 38.6% | 76.9% | 91.3% |
> > | 8% | 12.50 | 53.7% | 90.1% | 97.9% |
> >
> > At 8%, 90% of the value arrives within 30 years; at 2%, less than half does. Low discount rates make distant cash flows matter. Valuations at low rates therefore hinge on assumptions about the far future (Section 4).

### Every time-value problem is a recurrence

An account that earns $i$ per period and receives a net cash flow $c_t$ at the end of period $t$ evolves as

$$B_t=(1+i)\,B_{t-1}+c_t .$$

Deposits have $c_t>0$; withdrawals and loan payments have $c_t<0$. Unrolling the recursion from $B_0$ gives

$$B_n=(1+i)^nB_0+\sum_{t=1}^{n}(1+i)^{\,n-t}c_t, \tag{6.4}$$

as substituting (6.4) for $B_{n-1}$ into the recursion confirms. When the $c_t$ are level, the sum in (6.4) is geometric with ratio $1+i$. When they grow at rate $g$, it is geometric with ratio $(1+g)/(1+i)$ after factoring. Savings plans, pension drawdowns and loan balances are all instances of (6.4).

> [!exhibit] Arithmetic and geometric sequences side by side {#seqtable}
> | | Arithmetic | Geometric |
> |:--|:--|:--|
> | Defining rule | $a_{k+1}=a_k+d$ | $a_{k+1}=x\,a_k$ |
> | $k$-th term | $a_1+(k-1)d$ | $a_1x^{k-1}$ |
> | Sum of $n$ terms | $n(a_1+a_n)/2$ | $a_1(1-x^n)/(1-x)$ |
> | Infinite sum | diverges unless every term is 0 | $a_1/(1-x)$ if $\lvert x\rvert<1$ |
> | In finance | simple interest; equal-principal loan payments | compound growth; discount factors; principal in a level-payment loan |

> [!kc]
> In the 52-week savings challenge (52週存錢法) a saver deposits NT\$100 in week 1, NT\$200 in week 2 and so on, up to NT\$5,200 in week 52. How much is deposited in total? A variant deposits NT\$100 in week 1 and then, each week, 95% of the previous week's amount, forever. What is its total?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > The first plan is arithmetic: by (6.1), $52\times(100+5{,}200)/2=$ NT\$137,800. The second is geometric with ratio 0.95: by (6.3), $100/(1-0.95)=$ NT\$2,000. A ratio below 1 caps an infinite sum; a constant increment does not.

## Present Value, Future Value and Annuities {lo=2}

Interest makes money at different dates into different goods. One period at rate $i$ turns $\mathrm{PV}$ into $(1+i)\mathrm{PV}$, and $n$ periods compound to

$$\mathrm{FV}_n=\mathrm{PV}\,(1+i)^n, \qquad \mathrm{PV}=\mathrm{FV}_n\,(1+i)^{-n}=\mathrm{FV}_n\,v^n .$$

The rate must match the period: a monthly cash flow needs a monthly rate. Converting quoted annual rates into periodic and effective rates is the subject of [[LM1]].

Discounting multiplies each cash flow by a number, so the present value of a stream is the sum of the present values of its parts:

$$\mathrm{PV}=\sum_{t}\mathrm{CF}_t\,v^{t}.$$

This **cash-flow additivity** lets us cut a complicated stream into simple pieces, value each piece and add. Section 7 turns the same principle into no-arbitrage pricing.

### Ordinary annuities

An **ordinary annuity** pays $A$ at the end of each of $n$ periods, at $t=1,\dots,n$. Its present value is a geometric series with first term $Av$ and ratio $v$, so by (6.2)

$$\mathrm{PV}=A\sum_{t=1}^{n}v^t=Av\,\frac{1-v^n}{1-v}.$$

Since $1-v=i/(1+i)=iv$, the factor $v/(1-v)$ equals $1/i$, and

$$\mathrm{PV}_{\text{ord}}=A\,\frac{1-(1+i)^{-n}}{i}. \tag{6.5}$$

Compounding (6.5) forward $n$ periods gives the value on the date of the last payment:

$$\mathrm{FV}_{\text{ord}}=A\,\frac{(1+i)^n-1}{i}. \tag{6.6}$$

We write $a_{n,i}$ and $s_{n,i}$ for the two fractions, the **annuity factors**.

### Annuities due

An **annuity due** pays at the start of each period, at $t=0,\dots,n-1$ ([[ex:timeline]]). Every payment arrives one period earlier than in the ordinary annuity, so every value is multiplied by $1+i$:

$$\mathrm{PV}_{\text{due}}=(1+i)\,\mathrm{PV}_{\text{ord}}, \qquad \mathrm{FV}_{\text{due}}=(1+i)\,\mathrm{FV}_{\text{ord}}. \tag{6.7}$$

Rent, insurance premiums and lease payments are normally paid in advance and form annuities due. Loan payments and bond coupons are paid in arrears and form ordinary annuities.

> [!exhibit] Ordinary annuity and annuity due on a timeline {#timeline}
> | Time $t$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | ⋯ | $n-1$ | $n$ |
> |:--|--:|--:|--:|:-:|--:|--:|
> | Ordinary annuity | | $A$ | $A$ | ⋯ | $A$ | $A$ |
> | Annuity due | $A$ | $A$ | $A$ | ⋯ | $A$ | |
> | Discount factor | 1 | $v$ | $v^2$ | ⋯ | $v^{n-1}$ | $v^n$ |
>
> Note: the annuity due is the ordinary annuity shifted one period earlier, hence the factor $1+i=1/v$ in (6.7).

### Solving for the payment, the term or the rate

Each of (6.5)–(6.7) links a value, the payment $A$, the rate $i$, the term $n$ and the timing convention. Given all but one, the last follows. The payment enters linearly and solves directly. The number of periods solves with logarithms: from (6.5), $v^n=1-\mathrm{PV}\,i/A$, so

$$n=-\frac{\ln\!\left(1-\mathrm{PV}\,i/A\right)}{\ln(1+i)} ,$$

which requires $A>\mathrm{PV}\,i$. A payment no larger than the interest $\mathrm{PV}\,i$ never reduces the balance, so the payments can continue forever (Section 4). The rate has no closed form in general; Section 5 finds it numerically.

> [!example] A down payment and a retirement pot {#savings}
> (a) A couple wants NT\$3,000,000 for a down payment in 8 years. Their account pays 2.4% a year, credited monthly at 0.2% (illustrative). What deposit at the end of each month reaches the target?
>
> (b) A retiree holds NT\$10,000,000 in an account paying 0.25% a month (3% a year, illustrative) and withdraws NT\$60,000 at the end of each month. How long does the money last?
>
> > [!solution]
> > (a) Solve (6.6) for $A$ with $i=0.002$ and $n=96$: $A=3{,}000{,}000\times0.002/(1.002^{96}-1)=$ NT\$28,377.08. The couple deposits NT\$2,724,200 in total, and interest supplies the remaining NT\$275,800. Depositing at the start of each month instead, an annuity due, divides the deposit by $1.002$, to NT\$28,320.44.
> >
> > (b) Here $\mathrm{PV}\,i/A=10{,}000{,}000\times0.0025/60{,}000=0.41667$, so $n=-\ln(0.58333)/\ln(1.0025)=215.87$ months, about 18.0 years. Without interest the money would last $10{,}000{,}000/60{,}000=166.7$ months (13.9 years). A withdrawal of NT\$25,000, equal to the monthly interest, would last forever.

> [!example] Paying rent in advance {#rent}
> A landlord asks for NT\$30,000 at the start of each month, or NT\$350,000 once at the start of the year. The tenant can earn 2% a year on deposits, credited monthly (illustrative). Which offer is cheaper, and what return does paying the lump sum earn?
>
> > [!solution]
> > The monthly rent is a 12-payment annuity due at $i=0.02/12=0.16667\%$. By (6.5) and (6.7) its present value is $30{,}000\times a_{12,i}\times(1+i)=$ NT\$356,724, against NT\$356,130 if the same payments were made at month-end; the ratio is exactly $1+i$. The lump sum is NT\$6,724 cheaper in present-value terms.
> >
> > The return on prepaying is the monthly rate at which the annuity due is worth exactly NT\$350,000. No formula isolates $i$; the bisection method of Section 5 gives 0.5164% a month, a nominal 6.20% a year or 6.38% effective. Prepaying earns more than three times the deposit rate, provided the tenant is sure to stay the full year.

> [!pitfall] Timing and period mismatches
> Two errors account for most wrong annuity answers. The first is timing: $a_{n,i}$ assumes the first payment one period from now, so payments in advance need the extra factor $1+i$. The second is the period: monthly payments need a monthly rate and a count of months. Under the nominal-rate convention, 2.4% a year over eight years means $i=0.2\%$ and $n=96$, not $i=2.4\%$ and $n=8$.

> [!zh] 一條等比級數，走遍所有現金流
> 折現就是每過一期乘上 $v=1/(1+i)$，所以一串固定金額的現金流，現值必然是等比級數：首項 $Av$、公比 $v$，套用 (6.2) 就得到年金公式。期初年金（房租、保費）只是把整串現金流往前移一期，所以剛好乘上 $1+i$。級數的「尾巴」$x^n$ 告訴你第 $n$ 期之後還藏著多少價值：折現率 8% 時，永續年金九成的價值在 30 年內就收完；折現率 2% 時，30 年只收到 45%。利率越低，越遙遠的未來越重要。

> [!kc]
> NT\$1,000 is paid each year for 10 years and money earns 3%. Compute the present value and the future value as an ordinary annuity and as an annuity due. By what factor do the pairs differ?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > Ordinary: $\mathrm{PV}=1{,}000\times(1-1.03^{-10})/0.03=8{,}530.20$ and $\mathrm{FV}=1{,}000\times(1.03^{10}-1)/0.03=11{,}463.88$. Due: $8{,}786.11$ and $11{,}807.80$. Each due value is the ordinary value times $1.03$, by (6.7).

## Perpetuities, Growth and the Gordon Model {lo=3}

Let the number of payments grow without bound, or let the payments themselves grow. Both limits are geometric series, and together they value preferred shares, common stocks and the terminal stage of any long-lived cash flow.

### Perpetuities

As $n\to\infty$ in (6.5), $(1+i)^{-n}\to0$ and the value of a **perpetuity** appears:

$$\mathrm{PV}_{\text{perp}}=\frac{A}{i}. \tag{6.8}$$

A perpetual preferred share paying NT\$2.40 a year is worth NT\$60.00 at a required return of 4%. If the first payment comes at $t=m+1$ instead of $t=1$, the stream is worth $A/i$ at $t=m$ and $v^mA/i$ today: NT\$49.32 when the first dividend arrives in six years ($m=5$). A perpetuity due, which also pays today, is worth $(1+i)A/i=$ NT\$62.40.

### Growing annuities and perpetuities

Let the payment at $t$ be $A_1(1+g)^{t-1}$, growing at rate $g$ per period. Factoring out $A_1/(1+i)$,

$$\mathrm{PV}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{A_1(1+g)^{t-1}}{(1+i)^t}=\frac{A_1}{1+i}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\Big(\frac{1+g}{1+i}\Big)^{t},$$

a geometric series with ratio $x=(1+g)/(1+i)$. Since $1-x=(i-g)/(1+i)$, (6.2) gives

$$\mathrm{PV}_{\text{grow}}=\frac{A_1}{i-g}\left[1-\Big(\frac{1+g}{1+i}\Big)^{n}\right], \qquad g\ne i. \tag{6.9}$$

When $g=i$ every term equals $A_1/(1+i)$ and the sum is $nA_1/(1+i)$. As $n\to\infty$, (6.3) applies if and only if $x<1$, that is $g<i$:

$$\mathrm{PV}_{\text{grow perp}}=\frac{A_1}{i-g}, \qquad g<i. \tag{6.10}$$

If $g\ge i$ the series diverges: payments that grow at least as fast as the discount rate have no finite value. As a check, $A_1=1$, $i=5\%$, $g=3\%$ and $n=20$ give 15.9648, both by (6.9) and by summing the 20 terms. With $g=i$ the value is $20/1.05=19.0476$.

### The Gordon growth model

A share entitles its holder to dividends $D_1,D_2,\dots$. If they grow at a constant rate $g$ forever and investors require a return $k>g$, (6.10) prices the share:

$$P_0=\frac{D_1}{k-g}=\frac{D_0(1+g)}{k-g}. \tag{6.11}$$

This is the **Gordon growth model** (Gordon 1959). Read backwards, a market price implies a required return or a growth rate. Solving (6.11) for $k$ gives the **implied required return**, dividend yield plus growth:

$$k=\frac{D_1}{P_0}+g. \tag{6.12}$$

Solving instead for $g$, $kP_0-gP_0=D_0+gD_0$ gives the **implied growth rate**

$$g=\frac{kP_0-D_0}{P_0+D_0}. \tag{6.13}$$

Dividing (6.11) by next year's earnings $E_1$ gives the **justified forward P/E**, $P_0/E_1=(D_1/E_1)/(k-g)$: the payout ratio divided by $k-g$. A 40% payout with $k=9\%$ and $g=4\%$ justifies a forward P/E of 8.0.

### Changing growth

Growth rarely stays constant: a company may grow fast for some years and then mature. A **two-stage model** values the first $N$ dividends with (6.9) and the rest with (6.11) applied at $t=N$:

$$P_0=\sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{D_t}{(1+k)^t}+\frac{1}{(1+k)^N}\cdot\frac{D_{N+1}}{k-g_2}.$$

> [!example] A two-stage dividend model {#twostage}
> A company has just paid a dividend of NT\$10. Analysts expect 12% growth for five years and 4% a year thereafter; the required return is 9% (illustrative). Value the share.
>
> > [!solution]
> > Stage 1: $D_1,\dots,D_5=11.20,\ 12.544,\ 14.0493,\ 15.7352,\ 17.6234$. Their present value at 9% is 54.2831, by discounting each one or by (6.9) with $A_1=11.20$, $i=9\%$, $g=12\%$ and $n=5$. The formula works with $g>i$ because the stream is finite.
> >
> > Stage 2: $D_6=17.6234\times1.04=18.3284$, so $P_5=18.3284/(0.09-0.04)=366.5671$, worth $366.5671/1.09^5=238.2434$ today.
> >
> > Hence $P_0=54.2831+238.2434=$ NT\$292.53, of which the terminal value is 81.4%. A single-stage model at 4% would give NT\$208.00; at 12% it would give no answer, because $g>k$.

## Bond Pricing and Yield to Maturity {lo=4}

A bond is a promise to pay a schedule of cash flows, and its price is their present value. Bond pricing is therefore annuity pricing; the only new problem is solving for the rate.

### Three kinds of bond

A **zero-coupon bond** pays only its face value $F$ at maturity, so its price is $P=F(1+y)^{-n}$: it is a discount instrument. A **coupon bond** pays $C=cF$ each period and $F$ at maturity, an ordinary annuity plus a single sum:

$$P=C\,\frac{1-(1+y)^{-n}}{y}+F\,(1+y)^{-n}. \tag{6.14}$$

An **amortizing bond** pays a level amount $A$ that covers interest and repays principal over its life. It is worth $A\,a_{n,y}$: the loan of Section 6 seen from the lender's side.

Rearranging (6.14) shows when a bond trades above par. Because $yF\,a_{n,y}+F(1+y)^{-n}=F$, a bond whose coupon equals its yield is worth exactly par, and subtracting gives

$$P-F=(C-yF)\,a_{n,y}. \tag{6.15}$$

Price exceeds par exactly when the coupon rate exceeds the yield, by the present value of the coupon surplus.

A coupon bond is also a portfolio of zero-coupon bonds, one per cash flow. By cash-flow additivity each cash flow must be discounted at its own **spot rate** $z_t$:

$$P=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{\mathrm{CF}_t}{(1+z_t)^t}.$$

The **yield to maturity** $y$ is the single rate that reproduces this price in (6.14). It summarizes the spot curve, weighted by the bond's cash flows. Section 7 shows how spot rates also imply forward rates.

Its premium is smaller than in (b) because principal comes back early, so the 3% coupon is earned on a shrinking balance.

### Yield to maturity as an implied return

Given a market price, the yield to maturity solves $P(y)=P_{\text{mkt}}$, a polynomial equation of degree $n$ in $v=1/(1+y)$. For $n\ge5$ no general formula in radicals exists, but the equation is well behaved. Every term of $P(y)$ falls as $y$ rises, so $P$ is continuous and strictly decreasing. It falls from the sum of the cash flows at $y=0$ towards 0 as $y\to\infty$, so any price in between has exactly one positive yield.

**Bisection** exploits this monotonicity. Start with a bracket $[a,b]$ on which $P(y)-P_{\text{mkt}}$ changes sign, and repeatedly keep the half whose ends still differ in sign. After $k$ halvings the midpoint is within $(b-a)/2^{k}$ of the yield, so $10^{-8}$ from a 5-point bracket takes $\lceil\log_2(0.05/10^{-8})\rceil=23$ steps ([[ex:bisect]]). Newton's method, which also uses the slope $P'(y)$, needs far fewer ([[LM4]]).

> [!deepdive]- When is the IRR unique? Descartes' rule of signs
> Write the NPV as a polynomial in $v=1/(1+y)$: $\mathrm{NPV}(v)=\sum_{t=0}^{n}\mathrm{CF}_t\,v^t$. Descartes' rule of signs, stated here without proof (Wang 2004), counts the positive roots of a polynomial. Their number, counted with multiplicity, equals the number of sign changes among the non-zero coefficients or falls short of it by an even number.
>
> A conventional investment, outflows first and inflows afterwards, has exactly one sign change, hence one positive root $v$ and one IRR above −100%. The policy of [[eg:insurance]] has coefficients $(-,-,-,-,-,-,0,0,0,0,+)$: one change, one IRR.
>
> Unconventional streams can have several. The flows $-100$, $+230$ and $-132$ at $t=0,1,2$ give $-100+230v-132v^2=0$, with roots $v=1/1.1$ and $v=1/1.2$: the IRR is both 10% and 20%. When the cash flows change sign more than once, report the NPV at a stated discount rate instead of an IRR.

## Loans and Amortization {lo=5}

A level-payment loan is an ordinary annuity seen from the borrower's side. The lender hands over $L$ today in exchange for $n$ payments of $A$, so $L=A\,a_{n,i}$ and

$$A=\frac{L\,i}{1-(1+i)^{-n}}. \tag{6.16}$$

In Taiwan this level-payment method is called 本息平均攤還; repaying equal principal each month is called 本金平均攤還.

### Balance, interest and principal

Recurrence (6.4) with $B_0=L$ and $c_t=-A$ gives the balance after $k$ payments in two equivalent forms:

$$B_k=L(1+i)^k-A\,s_{k,i}=A\,a_{n-k,i}. \tag{6.17}$$

The first, retrospective, form accumulates the loan and subtracts the accumulated payments. The second, prospective, form says the balance is the present value of the payments still due. Substituting $L=A\,a_{n,i}$ into the first form shows they agree:

$$A\,\frac{(1-v^n)(1+i)^k-\big((1+i)^k-1\big)}{i}=A\,\frac{1-v^{\,n-k}}{i}=A\,a_{n-k,i}.$$

Each payment splits into interest on the outstanding balance, $I_k=iB_{k-1}$, and principal $P_k=A-I_k$. The prospective form gives $iB_{k-1}=A(1-v^{\,n-k+1})$, so

$$P_k=A\,v^{\,n-k+1}. \tag{6.18}$$

The principal portions form a geometric sequence with ratio $1+i$, from $Av^n$ in the first payment to $Av$ in the last. Interest portions fall as principal portions rise, and each pair sums to $A$.

When does principal overtake interest? $P_k>I_k$ exactly when $v^{\,n-k+1}>\tfrac12$, that is, when the number of payments remaining, $n-k+1$, is shorter than the doubling time $\ln2/\ln(1+i)$. At 2.2% a year, $i=0.18333\%$ a month, money doubles in 378.4 months, longer than a 30-year term, so principal exceeds interest from the first payment. At 5% the doubling time is 166.7 months, and principal overtakes interest only at payment 195, after 16 years.

### Equal principal and grace periods

Under **equal-principal** repayment the borrower repays $L/n$ each period plus interest on the remaining balance. The balance before payment $k$ is $L\big(1-(k-1)/n\big)$, so the payments fall by the constant amount $iL/n$: an arithmetic sequence. Total interest follows from (6.1):

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n}iL\Big(1-\frac{k-1}{n}\Big)=\frac{iL}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}j=\frac{iL\,(n+1)}{2}. \tag{6.19}$$

A **grace period** (寬限期) of $m$ periods charges interest only, $iL$ per period, and then amortizes $L$ over the remaining $n-m$ periods with (6.16).

## Cash-Flow Additivity and No-Arbitrage {lo=6}

Cash-flow additivity says the value of a stream equals the sum of the values of its parts. The **law of one price** adds that two portfolios with identical future cash flows must cost the same today. Otherwise, buying the cheaper portfolio and selling the dearer one would lock in a riskless profit, an **arbitrage**. Together the two principles price anything that can be replicated; this section applies them three times.

### Implied forward rates

An investor with a two-year horizon can buy a two-year zero at the spot rate $z_2$. Alternatively, she can buy a one-year zero at $z_1$ and agree today to reinvest for the second year at a **forward rate** $f_{1,1}$. Both strategies are riskless and pay off on the same date, so they must grow money equally: $(1+z_2)^2=(1+z_1)(1+f_{1,1})$. In general, the forward rate for $B-A$ years starting in $A$ years satisfies

$$(1+z_B)^B=(1+z_A)^A\,(1+f_{A,B-A})^{B-A}. \tag{6.20}$$

With continuous compounding the relation is additive, $f=(Bz_B-Az_A)/(B-A)$. Taking logs of (6.20) and using $\ln(1+x)\approx x$, which drops terms of order $x^2$ ([[LM3]]), gives the same formula as an approximation for annual rates.

### Forward exchange rates

Quote the exchange rate $S$ as TWD per USD. To hold TWD at date $T$, invest TWD 1 at $i_{\text{TWD}}$; alternatively, convert it into USD $1/S$, invest at $i_{\text{USD}}$ and sell forward at $F$. Both routes are riskless, so with simple money-market rates

$$F=S\,\frac{1+i_{\text{TWD}}\,T}{1+i_{\text{USD}}\,T}, \tag{6.21}$$

**covered interest parity**; with continuous rates, $F=Se^{(r_{\text{TWD}}-r_{\text{USD}})T}$. The currency with the lower interest rate trades at a forward premium. When TWD rates are below USD rates, a forward buys fewer TWD per USD than spot. The extra interest a USD deposit earns is given back through the forward.

### Index futures and the cost of carry

To replicate a futures position on a stock index, borrow $Se^{-q\tau}$ at the continuously compounded rate $r$ and buy $e^{-q\tau}$ units of the index basket. Reinvest the dividends, which arrive at the rate $q$, in the basket. At expiry the position has grown to exactly one unit, worth $S_T$, and the loan to $Se^{-q\tau}e^{r\tau}$. A futures contract bought at $F$ pays $S_T-F$, the same payoff, so the delivery price must be

$$F=S\,e^{(r-q)\tau}. \tag{6.22}$$

If $F$ were higher, an arbitrageur would sell futures and buy the basket (**cash-and-carry**); if lower, buy futures and sell the basket short (**reverse cash-and-carry**). Futures are marked to market daily, but with a deterministic interest rate their fair value equals that of a forward (Hull 2022). With discrete dividends $D_j$ paid at times $t_j<\tau$, the same argument gives

$$F=\Big(S-\sum_jD_je^{-rt_j}\Big)e^{r\tau}=S\,e^{r\tau}-\sum_jD_j\,e^{r(\tau-t_j)}: \tag{6.23}$$

the futures price is the index carried forward at $r$, minus the dividends carried forward to expiry.

The basis $F-S=S\big(e^{(r-q)\tau}-1\big)\approx S(r-q)\tau$, with a relative error of about $(r-q)\tau/2$, is negative whenever $q>r$: dividends outweigh interest. With $S=20{,}000$, $r=2\%$, $q=3\%$ and $\tau=0.25$, $F=19{,}950.06$ and the basis is −49.94 points, against −50.00 to first order. At expiry the basis must vanish, the futures version of pull to par. TX is cash-settled against the average of TAIEX over the last 30 minutes of the expiry day (TAIFEX).

### Reading the fair value backwards

Observed $F$ and $S$ pin down $r-q$, not $r$ and $q$ separately. Fixing one gives the other:

$$q\tau=r\tau-\ln\frac{F}{S}, \qquad r_{\text{impl}}=q+\frac{1}{\tau}\ln\frac{F}{S}. \tag{6.24}$$

The first is the **implied dividend yield**, here measured over the contract's life as $q\tau$. The second is the **implied financing rate**, often called the implied repo rate. Two futures with expiries $\tau_1<\tau_2$ give, by the logic of (6.20), an implied forward dividend yield between the expiries: $q_{12}(\tau_2-\tau_1)=r(\tau_2-\tau_1)-\ln(F_2/F_1)$. It does not involve $S$ at all.

Frictions turn (6.22) into a band: cash-and-carry borrows cash at a rate $r_b$. Reverse cash-and-carry must borrow every share in the basket, pay lending fees and usually earn less than $r$ on the short-sale proceeds. Its effective rate is some $r_\ell<r_b$. Arbitrage then holds the futures price only within

$$S\,e^{(r_\ell-q)\tau}\le F\le S\,e^{(r_b-q)\tau},$$

The lower bound is the weak one, because shorting a whole index basket is harder than buying it.

> [!zh] 無套利：同樣的現金流，同樣的價格
> 遠期利率、遠期匯率、期貨合理價，背後都是同一個論證：兩個未來現金流完全相同的組合，今天的價格必須相同，否則就能低買高賣、無風險套利。指數期貨的合理價 $F=Se^{(r-q)\tau}$ 來自「借錢買現股、領股利」的複製：期貨比現貨多了利息 $r$、少了股利 $q$。台指期每年夏天的逆價差，第一個原因就是除息旺季的 $q$ 大於 $r$。但套利的兩個方向難易不同：期貨太貴時，買現股、賣期貨很容易；期貨太便宜時，要放空整籃股票，成本高、限制多，所以逆價差可以偏離合理價很久。

## The TX Basis Through the 2025 Dividend Season {lo=7}

Equation (6.24) leaves one degree of freedom: with $F$ and $S$ observed, choose $r$ to learn $q$, or choose $q$ to learn $r$. To decide which reading fits, we need the dividends themselves, measured independently of the futures market. TWSE effectively publishes them every day.

### Measuring the dividends

Besides the price index, TWSE computes a total-return TAIEX (報酬指數) that adjusts for cash dividends (TWSE 2023). On an ex-dividend date the price index falls by the dividends of the stocks going ex, while the total-return index does not. If dividends worth a fraction $\delta$ of the index go ex, the ratio $TR/S$ rises by the factor $1/(1-\delta)$. Over the life of a contract, therefore,

$$q\tau_{\text{real}}=\ln\frac{TR_T}{TR_t}-\ln\frac{S_T}{S_t}=-\sum_{\text{ex-dates}}\ln(1-\delta_j),$$

which is the $q\tau$ of (6.22) under proportional dividends. A cash-and-carry that reinvests each dividend ends with $\prod_j(1-\delta_j)^{-1}$ units for each unit bought.

An independent check confirms the measure. On 11 December 2025 the total-return index beat the price index by 14.21 bp, or 40.40 index points. The day before, the press estimated the points removed by that day's ex-dividend stocks at 40.34, almost all from TSMC's NT\$5 dividend (money.udn.com).

[[ex:divs]] accumulates the measure over 2025. TAIEX constituents paid 2.93% of the index during the year, and 2.44% — 83% of the total — between June and September. July alone contributed 1.08%.

### Carry, dividends and a residual

With the dividends measured, (6.22) becomes an accounting identity. Let $F^*=Se^{(r-q_{\text{real}})\tau}$ be the fair value given the dividends actually paid, and $\varepsilon=F-F^*$ the residual. Then

$$F-S=\underbrace{S\big(e^{r\tau}-1\big)}_{\text{carry}}-\underbrace{S\,e^{r\tau}\big(1-e^{-q_{\text{real}}\tau}\big)}_{\text{dividends, valued at expiry}}+\underbrace{\varepsilon}_{\text{residual}}. \tag{6.25}$$

Carry is the interest on the index value at the 2% proxy rate. The dividend term is what the dividends paid before expiry are worth at expiry. The residual is everything a frictionless, perfect-foresight model leaves unexplained; a negative residual means the futures were cheap.

### Why the residual can persist

The no-arbitrage band of Section 7 is asymmetric: when TX is rich, cash-and-carry needs only a stock purchase and a futures sale. When TX is cheap, the arbitrage must short every stock in the index, pay lending fees and accept less than $r$ on the proceeds. Its effective rate $r_\ell$ can sit far below 2%. Yet even at $r=0$, the December residual on 27 June was −461.8 points ([[eg:txdec]]).

The 2025 pattern is consistent with hedging pressure against that weak lower bound, a hypothesis that fits the data rather than a measured cause. The residual was most negative after the April tariff shock, when holders of Taiwan stocks had reason to sell futures rather than shares. It turned positive in a rising October market. MacKinlay and Ramaswamy (1988) found that S&P 500 futures mispricing grows, on average, with time to maturity, as in [[ex:decomp]].

For a basis trader the residual, not the raw basis, is the opportunity, and it is not riskless. Dividends must be forecast in real time rather than measured afterwards. The stock leg must track TAIEX closely, whereas 0050 tracks the Taiwan 50 index, whose TSMC weight is much higher ([[LM3]]). And the residual can widen before expiry forces convergence, which is why [[LM25]] models the basis as a mean-reverting spread.

## Time Value of Money in Python {lo=8}

Three habits keep TVM code honest. Write one function per formula, with its equation number in the docstring. Check the bracket before any numerical search. Use Polars expressions, not Python loops, for schedules and market data.

```python
def annuity_pv(A, i, n, due=False):
    """PV of n level payments A at periodic rate i: eq. (6.5); annuity due: eq. (6.7)."""
    pv = A * n if i == 0 else A * (1 - (1 + i) ** -n) / i
    return pv * (1 + i) if due else pv

def bond_price(y, c, n, F=100.0):
    """Annual-coupon bond, eq. (6.14)."""
    return annuity_pv(c * F, y, n) + F * (1 + y) ** -n

def bisect(f, a, b, tol=1e-8):
    """Root of f on [a, b]; f(a) and f(b) must have opposite signs."""
    fa = f(a)
    if fa * f(b) > 0:
        raise ValueError("root not bracketed")
    while (b - a) / 2 > tol:
        m = (a + b) / 2
        fm = f(m)
        if fa * fm <= 0:
            b = m
        else:
            a, fa = m, fm
    return (a + b) / 2

print(f"{bisect(lambda y: bond_price(y, 0.015, 10) - 97.20, 0.0, 0.05):.6%}")   # 1.808601%  (Exhibit 6)
print(f"{annuity_pv(30_000, 0.02 / 12, 12, due=True):,.0f}")                       # 356,724   (Example 3)
```

A level-payment schedule is a few vectorized columns, because (6.18) gives each principal portion in closed form:

```python
import polars as pl

def schedule(L, apr, years):
    i, n = apr / 12, years * 12
    A = L * i / (1 - (1 + i) ** -n)                                    # eq. (6.16)
    return (pl.DataFrame({"k": pl.int_range(1, n + 1, eager=True)})
              .with_columns(principal=A * pl.lit(1 + i).pow(pl.col("k") - n - 1))   # eq. (6.18)
              .with_columns(interest=A - pl.col("principal"))
              .with_columns(balance=L - pl.col("principal").cum_sum()))

s = schedule(10_000_000, 0.022, 30)
print(f"{s['principal'][0]:,.2f}  {s['balance'][59]:,.2f}")   # 19,636.75  8,755,754.89  (payment 1; after 60)
print(f"{s['interest'].sum():,.0f}")                          # 3,669,230  (total interest)
```

The decomposition (6.25) for any day and contract takes one join between the futures file and the two index series:

```python
import numpy as np

def decompose(F, S, tau, TR_t, TR_T, S_T, r=0.02):
    """Basis = carry - dividends + residual, eq. (6.25); dividends from the total-return index."""
    q_real = np.log(TR_T / TR_t) - np.log(S_T / S)
    carry = S * np.expm1(r * tau)
    dividends = S * np.exp(r * tau) * -np.expm1(-q_real)
    return {"basis": F - S, "carry": carry, "dividends": dividends,
            "residual": (F - S) - carry + dividends, "qtau_implied": r * tau - np.log(F / S)}

# December contract on 2025-06-27 (TR and S on 2025-06-27 and 2025-12-17)
print(decompose(21_700, 22_580.08, 173 / 365, 50_427.06, 62_630.84, 27_525.17))
# basis -880.08, carry 215.06, dividends 422.24, residual -672.90, qtau_implied 0.0492
```

## Summary {-}

- An arithmetic sequence adds a constant; a geometric sequence multiplies by one. The geometric sum (6.2) tends to $a/(1-x)$ as $n\to\infty$ only when $|x|<1$. The share of the infinite sum beyond term $n$ is exactly $x^n$.
- Discounting makes every level stream a geometric series with ratio $v=1/(1+i)$, which gives the annuity factors (6.5)–(6.6). An annuity due is worth $1+i$ times the ordinary annuity, (6.7). At 2%, a perpetuity collects only 45% of its value in 30 years.
- An unbounded term gives the perpetuity $A/i$; growing payments give (6.9) and (6.10), which require $g<i$. The Gordon model $P_0=D_1/(k-g)$ reads a price as an implied return or growth rate. For 0050 on 8 October 2026 it implies $k=9.94\%$ at 8.43% dividend growth, with two thirds of the value beyond year 30.
- Bond prices are annuities plus single sums, (6.14); a bond trades above par exactly when its coupon exceeds its yield, (6.15). The yield to maturity is unique for conventional cash flows and is found numerically; bisection halves the error at each step.
- A loan balance is the present value of the remaining payments, (6.17); principal portions grow geometrically at $1+i$, (6.18). On the illustrative NT\$10 million mortgage, equal-principal repayment saves NT\$360,064 of interest and a 3-year grace period costs NT\$261,984.
- No-arbitrage gives forward rates (6.20), covered interest parity (6.21) and the futures fair value $F=Se^{(r-q)\tau}$, (6.22). Observed prices imply $r-q$; fixing one of the two gives the implied dividend yield or the implied financing rate, (6.24).
- TAIEX constituents paid 2.93% of the index in 2025, 83% of it from June to September, the summer shape of the TX discount. On 27 June 2025 the December contract's 880-point discount split into 215 points of carry, 422 of dividends and a residual of −673. The residual put futures below even a zero-rate fair value, consistent with hedging pressure against a weak reverse-arbitrage bound.

## Glossary {-}

| Term | 中文 | Meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Arithmetic sequence | 等差數列 | Sequence with a constant difference between terms. |
| Geometric sequence | 等比數列 | Sequence with a constant ratio between terms. |
| Geometric series | 等比級數 | Sum of a geometric sequence, (6.2)–(6.3). |
| Present value | 現值 | Value today of future cash flows, discounted at the appropriate rate. |
| Future value | 終值 | Value at a future date of cash flows compounded forward. |
| Discount factor | 折現因子 | $v^t=(1+i)^{-t}$, the present value of 1 paid at $t$. |
| Cash-flow additivity | 現金流可加性 | The value of a stream equals the sum of the values of its parts. |
| Ordinary annuity | 普通年金（期末年金） | Level payments at the end of each period, (6.5). |
| Annuity due | 期初年金 | Level payments at the start of each period, (6.7). |
| Perpetuity | 永續年金 | Level payments forever, worth $A/i$. |
| Growing perpetuity | 成長型永續年金 | Payments growing at $g<i$ forever, worth $A_1/(i-g)$. |
| Gordon growth model | 高登成長模型 | $P_0=D_1/(k-g)$, (6.11). |
| Implied required return | 隱含要求報酬率 | $k=D_1/P_0+g$, the return a price implies, (6.12). |
| Justified forward P/E | 合理預估本益比 | Payout ratio divided by $k-g$. |
| Zero-coupon bond | 零息債券 | Bond paying only its face value at maturity. |
| Yield to maturity | 到期殖利率 | Single discount rate equating the promised cash flows to the price. |
| Internal rate of return | 內部報酬率 | Discount rate that sets the net present value to zero. |
| Bisection | 二分法 | Root finding by repeatedly halving a bracket. |
| Amortization schedule | 攤還表 | Payment-by-payment split of a loan into interest and principal. |
| Level-payment loan | 本息平均攤還 | Loan repaid by equal payments, (6.16). |
| Equal-principal loan | 本金平均攤還 | Loan repaying equal principal each period plus interest. |
| Grace period | 寬限期 | Initial periods with interest-only payments. |
| Spot rate | 即期利率 | Yield of a zero-coupon bond of a given maturity. |
| Implied forward rate | 隱含遠期利率 | Rate for a future period implied by spot rates, (6.20). |
| Covered interest parity | 拋補利率平價 | No-arbitrage link between spot, forward and two interest rates, (6.21). |
| Forward points | 換匯點 | Forward exchange rate minus spot. |
| Cash-and-carry arbitrage | 現貨持有套利 | Buying the underlying and selling the futures (reverse: the opposite). |
| Basis | 基差 | Futures price minus spot index, $F-S$. |
| Discount / premium (逆價差／正價差) | 逆價差／正價差 | Futures below / above the spot index. |
| Implied dividend yield | 隱含股利率 | $q$ solving $F=Se^{(r-q)\tau}$ at a given $r$, (6.24). |
| Implied financing rate | 隱含資金成本 | $r$ solving $F=Se^{(r-q)\tau}$ at a given $q$; also called the implied repo rate. |
| Total-return index | 報酬指數 | Index that reinvests cash dividends. |
