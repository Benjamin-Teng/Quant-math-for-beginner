## Introduction

A payment has a date as well as an amount: NT\$1 next year is worth less than NT\$1 today, because today's money earns interest. Discounting at a constant rate multiplies each payment by the same factor per period, so level or steadily growing payments form a **geometric series**. The sum of that series prices deposits, mortgages, bonds and dividend streams. Add one more idea, no-arbitrage, and futures can be priced too.

> [!case] Why does TX trade below TAIEX every summer?
> At the close on Friday 27 June 2025, TAIEX stood at 22,580.08. Every TX futures contract settled below it: July by 458 points, December by 880 and June 2026 by 1,087 ([[ex:curve0627]]). For the December contract this was the deepest discount of the 146 sessions from April to October.
>
> Over June the near-month contract averaged 192 points below the index, against 65 points in April. By September its discount (逆價差) had vanished on average (+0.5 points), and in October TX traded above TAIEX on average (+67 points).
>
> Interest says the opposite. A futures buyer keeps in the bank the cash a stock buyer must spend. With the central bank's discount rate at 2%, futures should sit *above* the index unless dividends going ex before expiry outweigh the interest.
>
> The cost-of-carry formula $F=Se^{(r_f-q)\tau}$ turns each price into the dividend yield $q$ the market must expect. At $r_f=2\%$ the December contract implied dividends of $q\tau=4.92\%$ before its expiry on 17 December. The dividends that went ex in the whole of calendar 2025 came to 2.93%.
>
> Is the discount a dividend forecast, a bearish bet or a mispricing? Section 8 splits each contract's discount into carry, dividends and a residual, measured with TWSE's total-return index. It checks how much rests on one extreme day.

## Sequences and Geometric Series {lo=1}

A deposit earning simple interest grows by the same amount each year; one earning compound interest grows by the same factor. The first is an arithmetic sequence, the second a geometric one.

> [!definition] Arithmetic and geometric sequences
> A sequence $a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots$ is **arithmetic** with common difference $d$ if $a_{j+1}=a_j+d$, so that $a_j=a_1+(j-1)d$. It is **geometric** with common ratio $x$ if $a_{j+1}=x\,a_j$, so that $a_j=a_1x^{j-1}$. A **series** is the sum of the terms of a sequence.

NT\$100 at 2% simple interest is worth 100, 102, 104, … after 0, 1, 2, … years: an arithmetic sequence with $d=2$. At 2% compound interest it is worth 100, 102, 104.04, …: a geometric sequence with $x=1.02$. The discount factors $v,v^2,v^3,\dots$ that convert future money into present money are geometric with ratio $v=1/(1+i)<1$.

### Summing an arithmetic sequence

Write the sum forwards and backwards and add the two lines term by term:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal S_n&=a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n,\\ \mathcal S_n&=a_n+a_{n-1}+\cdots+a_1 .\end{aligned}$$

Each pair $a_j+a_{n+1-j}=2a_1+(n-1)d=a_1+a_n$ has the same sum, and there are $n$ pairs, so

$$\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_j=\frac{n\,(a_1+a_n)}{2}. \tag{6.1}$$

Section 6 uses (6.1) for equal-principal loans, whose payments fall arithmetically.

### Summing a geometric sequence

Let $\mathcal S_n=a_1+a_1x+\cdots+a_1x^{n-1}$. Multiplying by $x$ shifts every term one place, $x\mathcal S_n=a_1x+\cdots+a_1x^{n}$, and subtracting cancels all but two terms: $\mathcal S_n-x\mathcal S_n=a_1-a_1x^n$.

> [!result] Geometric sums
> For $x\ne1$,
> $$\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_1x^j=a_1\,\frac{1-x^n}{1-x}, \tag{6.2}$$
> and for $x=1$ the sum is $na_1$. If $|x|<1$, the infinite series converges:
> $$\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_1x^j=\frac{a_1}{1-x}. \tag{6.3}$$
> If $|x|\ge1$ and $a_1\ne0$, the infinite series diverges.

Convergence follows from the gap between the full sum and a partial sum,

$$\frac{a_1}{1-x}-\mathcal S_n=\frac{a_1x^n}{1-x}.$$

For $|x|<1$, $|x|^n=e^{n\ln|x|}\to0$ because $\ln|x|<0$ ([[LM1]]), so the gap closes. For $|x|\ge1$ the terms $a_1x^j$ do not shrink to zero. A series whose terms do not vanish cannot settle on a finite sum (Stewart, Clegg and Watson 2021).

By the gap formula, the fraction of an infinite geometric sum beyond its first $n$ terms is exactly $x^n$. For $x=v$ this is the share of a perpetuity's value paid after year $n$.

> [!example] How much of a perpetuity is paid in the first 30 years? {#tail}
> A security pays NT\$1 at the end of every year forever. At discount rates of 2%, 5% and 8%, compute its value and the share of it paid within 10, 30 and 50 years.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The payments form a geometric series with first term $v$ and ratio $v$. By (6.3) the value is $v/(1-v)=1/i$: NT\$50.00 at 2%, NT\$20.00 at 5% and NT\$12.50 at 8%. By the gap formula the share paid after year $n$ is $v^n$, so the share paid within $n$ years is $1-v^n$:
> >
> > | Rate | Value | Within 10 years | Within 30 years | Within 50 years |
> > |--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
> > | 2% | 50.00 | 18.0% | 44.8% | 62.8% |
> > | 5% | 20.00 | 38.6% | 76.9% | 91.3% |
> > | 8% | 12.50 | 53.7% | 90.1% | 97.9% |
> >
> > At 8%, 90% of the value arrives within 30 years; at 2%, less than half does. Low discount rates make distant cash flows matter, so valuations at low rates hinge on assumptions about the far future (Section 4).

### Every time-value problem is a recurrence

An account that earns $i$ per period and receives a net cash flow $\mathrm{CF}_t$ at the end of period $t$ evolves as $B_t=(1+i)\,B_{t-1}+\mathrm{CF}_t$. Deposits have $\mathrm{CF}_t>0$; withdrawals and loan payments have $\mathrm{CF}_t<0$. Unrolling the recursion from $B_0$ gives

$$B_n=(1+i)^nB_0+\sum_{t=1}^{n}(1+i)^{\,n-t}\mathrm{CF}_t, \tag{6.4}$$

as substituting (6.4) for $B_{n-1}$ into the recursion confirms. Level cash flows make the sum geometric with ratio $1+i$; flows growing at rate $\gamma$ make it geometric with ratio $(1+\gamma)/(1+i)$ after factoring.

> [!kc]
> In the 52-week savings challenge (52週存錢法) a saver deposits NT\$100 in week 1, NT\$200 in week 2, …, NT\$5,200 in week 52. How much is deposited in total? A variant deposits NT\$100 in week 1 and then, each week, 95% of the previous week's amount, forever. What is its total?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > The first plan is arithmetic: by (6.1), $52\times(100+5{,}200)/2=$ NT\$137,800. The second is geometric with ratio 0.95: by (6.3), $100/(1-0.95)=$ NT\$2,000. A ratio below 1 caps an infinite sum; a constant increment does not.

## Present Value, Future Value and Annuities {lo=2}

One period at rate $i$ turns $\mathrm{PV}$ into $(1+i)\mathrm{PV}$, and $n$ periods compound to

$$\mathrm{FV}_n=\mathrm{PV}\,(1+i)^n, \qquad \mathrm{PV}=\mathrm{FV}_n\,(1+i)^{-n}=\mathrm{FV}_n\,v^n .$$

The rate must match the period; [[LM1]] converts quoted annual rates into periodic and effective rates.

Discounting multiplies each cash flow by a number, so a stream's present value is the sum of its parts' present values, $\mathrm{PV}=\sum_{t}\mathrm{CF}_t\,v^{t}$. This **cash-flow additivity** lets us cut a complicated stream into simple pieces, value each piece and add. Section 7 turns the same principle into no-arbitrage pricing.

### Ordinary annuities

An **ordinary annuity** pays $\mathrm{PMT}$ at the end of each of $n$ periods, at $t=1,\dots,n$. Its present value is a geometric series with first term $\mathrm{PMT}\,v$ and ratio $v$, so by (6.2)

$$\mathrm{PV}=\mathrm{PMT}\sum_{t=1}^{n}v^t=\mathrm{PMT}\,v\,\frac{1-v^n}{1-v}.$$

Since $1-v=i/(1+i)=iv$, the factor $v/(1-v)$ equals $1/i$, and

$$\mathrm{PV}_{\text{ord}}=\mathrm{PMT}\,\frac{1-(1+i)^{-n}}{i}. \tag{6.5}$$

Compounding (6.5) forward $n$ periods gives the value on the date of the last payment:

$$\mathrm{FV}_{\text{ord}}=\mathrm{PMT}\,\frac{(1+i)^n-1}{i}. \tag{6.6}$$

We write $a_{n,i}$ and $s_{n,i}$ for the two fractions, the **annuity factors**.

### Annuities due

An **annuity due** pays at the start of each period, at $t=0,\dots,n-1$ ([[ex:timeline]]). Every payment arrives one period earlier than in the ordinary annuity, so every value is multiplied by $1+i$:

$$\mathrm{PV}_{\text{due}}=(1+i)\,\mathrm{PV}_{\text{ord}}, \qquad \mathrm{FV}_{\text{due}}=(1+i)\,\mathrm{FV}_{\text{ord}}. \tag{6.7}$$

Rent, insurance premiums and leases are usually paid in advance (annuities due); loan payments and bond coupons are paid in arrears (ordinary annuities).

> [!exhibit] Ordinary annuity and annuity due on a timeline {#timeline}
> | Time $t$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | ⋯ | $n-1$ | $n$ |
> |:--|--:|--:|--:|:-:|--:|--:|
> | Ordinary annuity | | $\mathrm{PMT}$ | $\mathrm{PMT}$ | ⋯ | $\mathrm{PMT}$ | $\mathrm{PMT}$ |
> | Annuity due | $\mathrm{PMT}$ | $\mathrm{PMT}$ | $\mathrm{PMT}$ | ⋯ | $\mathrm{PMT}$ | |
> | Discount factor | 1 | $v$ | $v^2$ | ⋯ | $v^{n-1}$ | $v^n$ |
>
> Note: the annuity due is the ordinary annuity shifted one period earlier.

### Solving for the payment, the term or the rate

Each of (6.5)–(6.7) links a value, the payment $\mathrm{PMT}$, the rate $i$, the term $n$ and the timing convention. Given all but one, the last follows. The payment enters linearly. The term solves with logarithms: from (6.5), $v^n=1-\mathrm{PV}\,i/\mathrm{PMT}$, so

$$n=-\frac{\ln\!\left(1-\mathrm{PV}\,i/\mathrm{PMT}\right)}{\ln(1+i)} ,$$

which requires $\mathrm{PMT}>\mathrm{PV}\,i$.

A payment no larger than the interest $\mathrm{PV}\,i$ never reduces the balance, so the payments can continue forever (Section 4). The rate has no closed form in general; Section 5 finds it numerically.

> [!example] A down payment and a retirement pot {#savings}
> (a) A couple wants NT\$3,000,000 for a down payment in 8 years. Their account pays 2.4% a year, credited monthly at 0.2% (illustrative). What deposit at the end of each month reaches the target?
>
> (b) A retiree holds NT\$10,000,000 in an account paying 0.25% a month (3% a year, illustrative), withdrawing NT\$60,000 at the end of each month. How long does the money last?
>
> > [!solution]
> > (a) Solve (6.6) for $\mathrm{PMT}$ with $i=0.002$ and $n=96$: $\mathrm{PMT}=3{,}000{,}000\times0.002/(1.002^{96}-1)=$ NT\$28,377.08. The couple deposits NT\$2,724,200 in total, and interest supplies the remaining NT\$275,800. Depositing at the start of each month instead, an annuity due, divides the deposit by $1.002$, to NT\$28,320.44.
> >
> > (b) Here $\mathrm{PV}\,i/\mathrm{PMT}=10{,}000{,}000\times0.0025/60{,}000=0.41667$, so $n=-\ln(0.58333)/\ln(1.0025)=215.87$ months, about 18.0 years. Without interest the money would last $10{,}000{,}000/60{,}000=166.7$ months (13.9 years). A withdrawal of NT\$25,000, equal to the monthly interest, would last forever.

> [!pitfall] Timing and period mismatches
> Two errors account for most wrong annuity answers. The first is timing: $a_{n,i}$ assumes the first payment one period from now, so payments in advance need the extra factor $1+i$. The second is the period: monthly payments need a monthly rate and a count of months. Under the nominal-rate convention, 2.4% a year over eight years means $i=0.2\%$ and $n=96$, not $i=2.4\%$ and $n=8$.

## Perpetuities, Growth and the Gordon Model {lo=3}

Let the number of payments grow without bound, or let the payments themselves grow. Both limits are geometric series, and together they value preferred shares, common stocks and the terminal stage of any long-lived cash flow.

### Perpetuities

As $n\to\infty$ in (6.5), $(1+i)^{-n}\to0$ and the value of a **perpetuity** appears:

$$\mathrm{PV}_{\text{perp}}=\frac{\mathrm{PMT}}{i}. \tag{6.8}$$

A perpetual preferred share paying NT\$2.40 a year is worth NT\$60.00 at a required return of 4%. If the first payment comes at $t=m+1$ instead of $t=1$, the stream is worth $\mathrm{PMT}/i$ at $t=m$ and $v^m\,\mathrm{PMT}/i$ today. A first dividend in six years ($m=5$) gives NT\$49.32. A perpetuity due, which also pays today, is worth $(1+i)\,\mathrm{PMT}/i=$ NT\$62.40.

### Growing annuities and perpetuities

Let the payment at $t$ be $\mathrm{PMT}_1(1+\gamma)^{t-1}$, growing at rate $\gamma$ per period. Factoring out $\mathrm{PMT}_1/(1+i)$,

$$\mathrm{PV}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{\mathrm{PMT}_1(1+\gamma)^{t-1}}{(1+i)^t}=\frac{\mathrm{PMT}_1}{1+i}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\Big(\frac{1+\gamma}{1+i}\Big)^{t},$$

a geometric series with ratio $x=(1+\gamma)/(1+i)$. Since $1-x=(i-\gamma)/(1+i)$, (6.2) gives

$$\mathrm{PV}_{\text{grow}}=\frac{\mathrm{PMT}_1}{i-\gamma}\left[1-\Big(\frac{1+\gamma}{1+i}\Big)^{n}\right], \quad \gamma\ne i. \tag{6.9}$$

When $\gamma=i$ every term equals $\mathrm{PMT}_1/(1+i)$ and the sum is $n\,\mathrm{PMT}_1/(1+i)$. As $n\to\infty$, (6.3) applies if and only if $x<1$, that is $\gamma<i$:

$$\mathrm{PV}_{\text{grow perp}}=\frac{\mathrm{PMT}_1}{i-\gamma}, \quad \gamma<i. \tag{6.10}$$

If $\gamma\ge i$ the series diverges: payments that grow at least as fast as the discount rate have no finite value. As a check, $\mathrm{PMT}_1=1$, $i=5\%$, $\gamma=3\%$ and $n=20$ give 15.9648, both by (6.9) and by summing the 20 terms. With $\gamma=i$ the value is $20/1.05=19.0476$.

### The Gordon growth model

A share entitles its holder to dividends $D_1,D_2,\dots$. If they grow at a constant rate $\gamma$ forever and investors require a return $k>\gamma$, (6.10) prices the share:

$$P_0=\frac{D_1}{k-\gamma}=\frac{D_0(1+\gamma)}{k-\gamma}. \tag{6.11}$$

This is the **Gordon growth model** (Gordon 1959). Read backwards, a market price implies a required return or a growth rate. Solving (6.11) for $k$ gives the **implied required return**, dividend yield plus growth:

$$k=\frac{D_1}{P_0}+\gamma. \tag{6.12}$$

Solving instead for $\gamma$, $kP_0-\gamma P_0=D_0+\gamma D_0$ gives the **implied growth rate**

$$\gamma=\frac{kP_0-D_0}{P_0+D_0}. \tag{6.13}$$

Dividing (6.11) by next year's earnings $E_1$ gives the **justified forward P/E**, $P_0/E_1=(D_1/E_1)/(k-\gamma)$: the payout ratio divided by $k-\gamma$. A 40% payout with $k=9\%$ and $\gamma=4\%$ justifies a forward P/E of 8.0.

### Changing growth

A company may grow fast for some years and then mature. A **two-stage model** values the first $N$ dividends with (6.9) and the rest, growing at $\gamma_2$, with (6.11) applied at $t=N$:

$$P_0=\sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{D_t}{(1+k)^t}+\frac{1}{(1+k)^N}\cdot\frac{D_{N+1}}{k-\gamma_2}.$$

> [!example] A two-stage dividend model {#twostage}
> A company has just paid a dividend of NT\$10. Analysts expect 12% growth for five years and 4% a year thereafter; the required return is 9% (illustrative). Value the share.
>
> > [!solution]
> > Stage 1: $D_1,\dots,D_5=11.20,\ 12.544,\ 14.0493,\ 15.7352,\ 17.6234$. Their present value at 9% is 54.2831, by discounting each one or by (6.9) with $\mathrm{PMT}_1=11.20$, $i=9\%$, $\gamma=12\%$ and $n=5$. The formula works with $\gamma>i$ because the stream is finite.
> >
> > Stage 2: $D_6=D_5\times1.04=18.32835$ (unrounded $D_5=17.623417$), so $P_5=18.32835/(0.09-0.04)=366.567$, worth $366.567/1.09^5=238.2434$ today.
> >
> > Hence $P_0=54.2831+238.2434=$ NT\$292.53, of which the terminal value is 81.4%. A single-stage model at 4% would give NT\$208.00; at 12% it would give no answer, because $\gamma>k$.

> > (b) By (6.13), $\gamma=(0.08\times114.95-1.60)/(114.95+1.60)=7.596/116.55=6.52\%$.
> >
> > (c) The share after year $n$ is $x^n$ with $x=(1+\gamma)/(1+k)$: $(1.0843/1.0994)^{30}=0.66$. Two thirds of the value lies beyond year 30, against 24% for a stock with $k=9\%$ and $\gamma=4\%$.
> >
> > The model is fragile here. Holding $D_1$ fixed, lowering $k-\gamma$ by half a percentage point raises the model price from NT\$114.95 to NT\$171.90. Raising it by half a point lowers it to NT\$86.34.

Between 2016 and 2026 the yield fell from 3.98% to 1.39%. At a constant 10% required return the implied growth rose from 5.79% to 8.49% ([[ex:gordon0050]]). Either investors came to expect faster dividend growth or they accepted a lower return: the model identifies only $k-\gamma=D_1/P_0$, never its two parts.

> [!pitfall] Gordon near its edge
> Formula (6.11) sums a geometric series with ratio $(1+\gamma)/(1+k)$, so as $\gamma$ approaches $k$ the price becomes hypersensitive to both inputs. Report a range of $(k,\gamma)$ pairs, not a point. Never use price growth as $\gamma$: 0050's price grew 20.43% a year over the decade, and any $\gamma\ge k$ leaves the sum infinite. Put $D_1=D_0(1+\gamma)$, not $D_0$, in the numerator, or the price is understated by the factor $1+\gamma$.

> [!zh] 股利殖利率就是 $k-\gamma$
> 高登模型 $P_0=D_1/(k-\gamma)$ 說：預期股利殖利率 $D_1/P_0$ 剛好等於「要求報酬率減成長率」。0050 在 2026/10/8 的近 12 個月股利殖利率 $D_0/P_0$ 只有 1.39%，換成下一年的股利，$D_1/P_0=k-\gamma$ 也只有 1.51%，代表市場定價隱含的 $k$ 與 $\gamma$ 非常接近：若股利維持過去十年 8.43% 的成長，隱含要求報酬率約 9.94%。但 $k-\gamma$ 很小時，公比 $(1+\gamma)/(1+k)$ 接近 1，級數收斂極慢，30 年後的股利占了價值的三分之二；$k-\gamma$ 只要變動 0.5 個百分點，模型價格就從 114.95 跳到 171.90，或掉到 86.34。模型只能告訴你兩者的「差」，分不出是成長預期變高，還是要求報酬變低。

## Bond Pricing and Yield to Maturity {lo=4}

A bond's price is the present value of its promised cash flows, so bond pricing is annuity pricing. The only new problem is solving for the rate.

### Three kinds of bond

A **zero-coupon bond** pays only its face value $M$ at maturity, so its price is $P=M(1+y)^{-n}$: it is a discount instrument. A **coupon bond** pays a coupon $C$ each period (coupon rate $C/M$) and $M$ at maturity: an ordinary annuity plus a single sum,

$$P=C\,\frac{1-(1+y)^{-n}}{y}+M\,(1+y)^{-n}. \tag{6.14}$$

An **amortizing bond** pays a level amount $\mathrm{PMT}$ that covers interest and repays principal over its life. It is worth $\mathrm{PMT}\,a_{n,y}$: the loan of Section 6 seen from the lender's side.

Rearranging (6.14) shows when a bond trades above par. Because $yM\,a_{n,y}+M(1+y)^{-n}=M$, a bond whose coupon rate equals its yield is worth exactly par, and subtracting gives

$$P-M=(C-yM)\,a_{n,y}. \tag{6.15}$$

Price exceeds par exactly when the coupon rate exceeds the yield, by the present value of the coupon surplus.

A coupon bond is also a portfolio of zero-coupon bonds, one per cash flow. By cash-flow additivity each cash flow must be discounted at its own **spot rate** $z_t$:

$$P=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{\mathrm{CF}_t}{(1+z_t)^t}.$$

The **yield to maturity** $y$ is the single rate that reproduces this price in (6.14). It summarizes the spot curve, weighted by the bond's cash flows.

Its premium is smaller than in (b) because principal comes back early, so the 3% coupon is earned on a shrinking balance.

### Yield to maturity as an implied return

Given a market price, the yield to maturity solves $P(y)=P_{\text{mkt}}$, a polynomial equation of degree $n$ in $1/(1+y)$. It has no closed-form solution in general but is well behaved. Every term of $P(y)$ falls as $y$ rises, so $P$ is continuous and strictly decreasing. It falls from $\sum_t\mathrm{CF}_t$ at $y=0$ towards 0 as $y\to\infty$, so any price in between has exactly one positive yield.

**Bisection** exploits this monotonicity. Start with a bracket $[y_{\text{lo}},y_{\text{hi}}]$ on which $P(y)-P_{\text{mkt}}$ changes sign, and repeatedly keep the half whose ends still differ in sign. Each step halves the bracket and with it the bound on the error.

The error itself need not fall: it rises from step 3 to step 4 in [[ex:bisect]]. After $j$ halvings the midpoint lies within $(y_{\text{hi}}-y_{\text{lo}})/2^{j+1}$ of the yield. From a bracket 5 percentage points wide, $10^{-8}$ takes 22 halvings and 23 midpoints, since $2^{23}\ge0.05/10^{-8}>2^{22}$. Newton's method, which also uses the slope $P'(y)$, needs far fewer ([[LM4]]).

> > At $y=0$ the NPV is $+100{,}000$; at 10% it is negative. Bisection on $[0\%,10\%]$ tries 5.000% (NPV −103,208), 2.500% (−17,744), 1.250% (+36,443), 1.875% (+8,268) and 2.1875% (−4,998). It reaches $y=2.0683\%$, to within $10^{-8}$, at the 24th midpoint.

Is an IRR always unique? Write the NPV as a polynomial in $v=1/(1+y)$. Descartes' rule of signs, stated here without proof (Wang 2004), counts its positive roots. There are as many, counted with multiplicity, as the sign changes among its non-zero coefficients, or fewer by an even number.

Outflows followed by inflows, as in [[eg:insurance]], change sign once: one IRR. The flows $-100$, $+230$ and $-132$ at $t=0,1,2$ change sign twice, and the IRR is both 10% and 20%. For such streams, report the NPV at a stated rate instead.

> [!pitfall] Yield to maturity is a quote, not a forecast
> The yield to maturity is the IRR of the promised cash flows at today's price. ## Loans and Amortization {lo=5}

A level-payment loan is an ordinary annuity seen from the borrower's side. The lender hands over $B_0$ today in exchange for $n$ payments of $\mathrm{PMT}$, so $B_0=\mathrm{PMT}\,a_{n,i}$ and

$$\mathrm{PMT}=\frac{B_0\,i}{1-(1+i)^{-n}}. \tag{6.16}$$

### Balance, interest and principal

Recurrence (6.4) with $\mathrm{CF}_t=-\mathrm{PMT}$ gives the balance after $t$ payments in two equivalent forms:

$$B_t=B_0(1+i)^t-\mathrm{PMT}\,s_{t,i}=\mathrm{PMT}\,a_{n-t,i}. \tag{6.17}$$

The first, retrospective, form accumulates the loan and subtracts the accumulated payments. The second, prospective, form says the balance is the present value of the payments still due. Substituting $B_0=\mathrm{PMT}\,a_{n,i}$ into the first form shows they agree:

$$\mathrm{PMT}\,\frac{(1-v^n)(1+i)^t-\big((1+i)^t-1\big)}{i}=\mathrm{PMT}\,\frac{1-v^{\,n-t}}{i}=\mathrm{PMT}\,a_{n-t,i}.$$

Each payment splits into interest on the outstanding balance, $I_t=iB_{t-1}$, and principal $Q_t=\mathrm{PMT}-I_t$. The prospective form gives $iB_{t-1}=\mathrm{PMT}\,(1-v^{\,n-t+1})$, so

$$Q_t=\mathrm{PMT}\,v^{\,n-t+1}. \tag{6.18}$$

The principal portions form a geometric sequence with ratio $1+i$, from $\mathrm{PMT}\,v^n$ in the first payment to $\mathrm{PMT}\,v$ in the last. Interest portions fall as principal portions rise, and each pair sums to $\mathrm{PMT}$.

Principal exceeds interest, $Q_t>I_t$, exactly when $v^{\,n-t+1}>\tfrac12$: when the $n-t+1$ payments remaining span less than the doubling time $\ln2/\ln(1+i)$. At 2.2% a year money doubles in 378.4 months, longer than a 30-year term, so principal exceeds interest from the first payment. At 5% the doubling time is 166.7 months, and principal overtakes interest only at payment 195.

### Equal principal and grace periods

Under **equal-principal** repayment the borrower repays $B_0/n$ each period plus interest on the remaining balance. The balance before payment $t$ is $B_0\big(1-(t-1)/n\big)$, so the payments fall by the constant amount $iB_0/n$: an arithmetic sequence. Total interest follows from (6.1):

$$\sum_{t=1}^{n}iB_0\Big(1-\frac{t-1}{n}\Big)=\frac{iB_0}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}j=\frac{iB_0\,(n+1)}{2}. \tag{6.19}$$

A **grace period** (寬限期) of $m$ periods charges interest only, $iB_0$ per period, and then amortizes $B_0$ over the remaining $n-m$ periods with (6.16).

## Cash-Flow Additivity and No-Arbitrage {lo=6}

The **law of one price** says that two portfolios with identical future cash flows must cost the same today. Otherwise buying the cheaper and selling the dearer locks in a riskless profit, an **arbitrage**. With cash-flow additivity it prices anything that can be replicated; this section applies the pair three times.

### Implied forward rates

An investor with a two-year horizon can buy a two-year zero at the spot rate $z_2$. Alternatively, she can buy a one-year zero at $z_1$ and agree today to reinvest for the second year at a **forward rate** $y_{1,2}$. Both strategies are riskless and pay off on the same date, so they must grow money equally: $(1+z_2)^2=(1+z_1)(1+y_{1,2})$. In general, the forward rate $y_{t_1,t_2}$ from year $t_1$ to year $t_2$ satisfies

$$(1+z_{t_2})^{t_2}=(1+z_{t_1})^{t_1}\,(1+y_{t_1,t_2})^{t_2-t_1}. \tag{6.20}$$

With continuous compounding the relation is additive, $y_{t_1,t_2}=(t_2z_{t_2}-t_1z_{t_1})/(t_2-t_1)$. Taking logs of (6.20) and using $\ln(1+z_t)\approx z_t$ and $\ln(1+y_{t_1,t_2})\approx y_{t_1,t_2}$, which drop second-order terms ([[LM3]]), gives the same formula, approximately, for annual rates.

### Forward exchange rates

Quote the exchange rate $S$ as TWD per USD. To hold TWD in $\tau$ years, invest TWD 1 at $i_{\text{TWD}}$. Alternatively, convert it into USD $1/S$, invest at $i_{\text{USD}}$ and sell forward at $F$. Both routes are riskless, so with simple annual money-market rates

$$F=S\,\frac{1+i_{\text{TWD}}\,\tau}{1+i_{\text{USD}}\,\tau}, \tag{6.21}$$

**covered interest parity**; with continuous rates, $F=Se^{(r_{\text{TWD}}-r_{\text{USD}})\tau}$. We call $F-S$ the **forward points** and quote them in TWD per USD. The lower-rate currency trades at a forward premium: when TWD rates are below USD rates, a forward buys fewer TWD per USD than spot.

Against a 29.50 quote, borrow TWD 30.00 at 1.6%, buy USD 1, deposit it at 4.0% and sell the USD 1.04 forward at 29.50. ### Index futures and the cost of carry

To replicate an index futures position, borrow $Se^{-q\tau}$ at the continuously compounded rate $r_f$ and buy $e^{-q\tau}$ units of the index basket. Reinvest the dividends, which arrive at the rate $q$, in the basket. At expiry the position has grown to exactly one unit, worth $S_T$, and the loan to $Se^{-q\tau}e^{r_f\tau}$. A futures contract bought at $F$ pays $S_T-F$, the same payoff, so the delivery price must be

$$F=S\,e^{(r_f-q)\tau}. \tag{6.22}$$

If $F$ were higher, an arbitrageur would sell futures and buy the basket (**cash-and-carry**); if lower, buy futures and short the basket (**reverse cash-and-carry**). Futures are marked to market daily, but with a deterministic interest rate their fair value equals that of a forward (Hull 2022). With discrete dividends $D_j$ going ex $t_j$ years after the pricing date ($0<t_j<\tau$), and treated as received then, the same argument gives

$$F=\Big(S-\sum_jD_je^{-r_ft_j}\Big)e^{r_f\tau}=S\,e^{r_f\tau}-\sum_jD_j\,e^{r_f(\tau-t_j)}. \tag{6.23}$$

The futures price is the index carried forward at $r_f$, minus the dividends carried forward to expiry.

The basis $F-S=S\big(e^{(r_f-q)\tau}-1\big)\approx S(r_f-q)\tau$, with a relative error of about $(r_f-q)\tau/2$, is negative whenever $q>r_f$: dividends outweigh interest. With illustrative values $S=20{,}000$, $r_f=2\%$, $q=3\%$ and $\tau=0.25$, $F=19{,}950.06$ and the basis is −49.94 points, against −50.00 to first order. At expiry TX is cash-settled against the average of the TAIEX values published in the last 30 minutes of trading (TAIFEX). $F$ converges to that average, not to the 13:30 close: the futures version of pull to par.

### Reading the fair value backwards

Observed $F$ and $S$ pin down $r_f-q$, not $r_f$ and $q$ separately. Fixing one gives the other:

$$q\tau=r_f\tau-\ln\frac{F}{S}, \qquad r_{\text{impl}}=q+\frac{1}{\tau}\ln\frac{F}{S}. \tag{6.24}$$

The first is the **implied dividend yield**, here measured over the contract's life as $q\tau$. The second is the **implied financing rate**, often called the implied repo rate. Two futures with expiries $\tau_1<\tau_2$ give, by the logic of (6.20), an implied forward dividend yield between the expiries: $q_{12}(\tau_2-\tau_1)=r_f(\tau_2-\tau_1)-\ln(F_2/F_1)$. It does not involve $S$ at all.

Frictions turn (6.22) into a band. Cash-and-carry borrows cash at a rate $r_b$. Reverse cash-and-carry must borrow every share in the basket, pay lending fees and usually earn less than $r_f$ on the short-sale proceeds. Its effective rate is some $r_\ell<r_b$.

Arbitrage then holds the futures price only within

$$S\,e^{(r_\ell-q)\tau}\le F\le S\,e^{(r_b-q)\tau}.$$

The lower bound is the weak one, because shorting a whole index basket is harder than buying it.

## The TX Basis Through the 2025 Dividend Season {lo=7}

Equation (6.24) leaves one degree of freedom: choose $r_f$ to learn $q$, or $q$ to learn $r_f$. Deciding which reading fits needs the dividends themselves, measured outside the futures market, and TWSE effectively publishes them every day.

### Measuring the dividends

Besides the price index $S$, TWSE computes a total-return TAIEX, $\mathrm{TR}$ (報酬指數), that reinvests cash dividends (TWSE 2023). On an ex-dividend date the price index falls by the dividends of the stocks going ex; the total-return index does not. If dividends worth a fraction $\delta$ of the previous close go ex, the ratio $\mathrm{TR}/S$ therefore rises by the factor $1/(1-\delta)$. Over the life of a contract,

$$q_{\text{real}}\tau=\ln\frac{\mathrm{TR}_T}{\mathrm{TR}_t}-\ln\frac{S_T}{S_t}=-\sum_{\text{ex-dates}}\ln(1-\delta_j),$$

which is the $q\tau$ of (6.22) under proportional dividends, counted on their ex-dates.

TSMC's NT\$5 dividend went ex on 11 December 2025, inside the December contract's life, but was paid on 8 January 2026 (TSMC 2025). On the ex-date the ratio $\mathrm{TR}/S$ rose by 14.21 bp in log terms, so $\delta=1-e^{-0.00142149}=14.205$ bp of the previous close, 28,400.73: 40.34 index points. The day before, the press had estimated the points removed by that day's ex-dividend stocks at the same 40.34, almost all from TSMC (money.udn.com).

[[ex:divs]] accumulates the measure over 2025. Dividends going ex during the year came to $q\tau=2.93\%$ of the index, 2.44% of it (83%) between June and September. July alone contributed 1.08%.

### Carry, dividends and a residual

With the dividends measured, (6.22) becomes an accounting identity. Let $F_{\text{fair}}=Se^{(r_f-q_{\text{real}})\tau}$ be the fair value given the dividends that actually went ex, and $\varepsilon=F-F_{\text{fair}}$ the residual. Then

$$F-S=\underbrace{S\big(e^{r_f\tau}-1\big)}_{\text{carry}}-\underbrace{S\,e^{r_f\tau}\big(1-e^{-q_{\text{real}}\tau}\big)}_{\text{dividends}}+\underbrace{\varepsilon}_{\text{residual}}. \tag{6.25}$$

Carry is the interest on the index value at the 2% proxy rate. The dividend term applies the realized dividend yield to the index on the pricing date and carries it to expiry. The residual is everything a frictionless, perfect-foresight model leaves unexplained; a negative residual means the futures were cheap.

### Why the residual can persist

The no-arbitrage band of Section 7 is asymmetric. A rich TX is arbitraged with a stock purchase and a futures sale. A cheap one needs a short sale of every stock in the index, at an effective rate $r_\ell$ possibly far below 2%. Yet even at $r_f=0$ the December contract sat below fair value on 43 of the 44 sessions in June and July ([[eg:txdec]]).

The 2025 pattern is consistent with hedging pressure against that weak lower bound, a hypothesis rather than a measured cause. The near-month residual was deepest just after the April tariff shock (−298 points on 8 April). Holders of Taiwan stocks then had reason to sell futures rather than shares. The longer contracts' residuals were deepest in May and June.

Near-month and December residuals both turned positive in a rising October market. MacKinlay and Ramaswamy (1988) found that S&P 500 futures mispricing grows, on average, with time to maturity.

For a basis trader the residual is the opportunity, but not a riskless one. Dividends must be forecast and the stock leg must track TAIEX. The residual can widen before expiry forces convergence ([[LM25]] models it as a mean-reverting spread).

> [!pitfall] Annualizing a noisy basis
> TAIEX closes on a 13:25–13:30 call auction. The TX daily settlement is the volume-weighted average price of the last minute before the 13:45 close (TAIFEX). The daily basis therefore mixes two prices fifteen minutes apart.
>
> On 30 June TAIEX fell 324 points (1.44%) to its low of the day, while the July contract settled 22 points higher. Its basis jumped from −458 to −112. ## Time Value of Money in Python {lo=8}

Three habits keep TVM code honest. Write one function per formula, with its equation number in the docstring. Check the bracket before any numerical search. Use Polars expressions, not Python loops, for market data.

```python
def annuity_pv(pmt, i, n, due=False):
    """PV of n level payments PMT at periodic rate i, eq. (6.5); due: (6.7)."""
    pv = pmt * n if i == 0 else pmt * (1 - (1 + i) ** -n) / i
    return pv * (1 + i) if due else pv

def bond_price(y, c, n, M=100.0):
    """Annual-coupon bond with coupon rate c and face value M, eq. (6.14)."""
    return annuity_pv(c * M, y, n) + M * (1 + y) ** -n

def bisect(fn, lo, hi, tol=1e-8):
    """Root of fn on a sign-changing [lo, hi]; each pass halves the bracket."""
    f_lo = fn(lo)
    if f_lo * fn(hi) > 0:
        raise ValueError("root not bracketed")
    while (hi - lo) / 2 > tol:
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        f_mid = fn(mid)
        if f_lo * f_mid <= 0:
            hi = mid
        else:
            lo, f_lo = mid, f_mid
    return (lo + hi) / 2

ytm = bisect(lambda y: bond_price(y, 0.015, 10) - 97.20, 0.0, 0.05)
print(f"{ytm:.6%}")                                     # 1.808601%
print(f"{annuity_pv(1_000, 0.03, 10, due=True):,.2f}")  # 8,786.11
```

## Summary {-}

- An arithmetic sequence adds a constant; a geometric sequence multiplies by a constant ratio. The geometric sum (6.2) tends to $a_1/(1-x)$ only when $|x|<1$, and the share of the infinite sum beyond term $n$ is exactly $x^n$.
- Discounting makes every level stream a geometric series with ratio $v=1/(1+i)$, giving the annuity factors (6.5)–(6.6). An annuity due is worth $1+i$ times as much, (6.7).
- Growing payments give (6.9) and (6.10), which require $\gamma<i$. The Gordon model $P_0=D_1/(k-\gamma)$ reads a price as an implied return. For 0050 on 8 October 2026 it gives $k=9.94\%$ at 8.43% dividend growth, with two thirds of the value beyond year 30.
- A bond trades above par exactly when its coupon rate exceeds its yield, (6.15). The yield to maturity is unique for conventional cash flows. Bisection finds it by halving the bracket, and so the error bound, at each step.
- A loan balance is the present value of the remaining payments, (6.17), and principal portions grow at the rate $i$, (6.18). On the illustrative NT\$10 million mortgage, equal-principal repayment pays NT\$360,064 less interest (undiscounted) and a 3-year grace period NT\$261,984 more. Yet at the loan rate every schedule is worth exactly the principal.
- No-arbitrage gives forward rates (6.20), covered interest parity (6.21) and the futures fair value $F=Se^{(r_f-q)\tau}$, (6.22); observed prices imply $r_f-q$, (6.24).
- Dividends going ex in 2025 came to $q\tau=2.93\%$ of TAIEX, 83% of it from June to September: the summer shape of the TX discount. On 27 June, the extreme session, the December contract's 880-point discount split into 215 points of carry, 422 of dividends and a −673 residual. The residual averaged −461 over 23 June–3 July. Even at a zero financing rate the contract sat below fair value on 43 of 44 June–July sessions.

## Glossary {-}

| Term | 中文 | Meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Ordinary annuity | 普通年金（期末年金） | Level payments at the end of each period, (6.5). |
| Annuity due | 期初年金 | Level payments at the start of each period, (6.7). |
| Perpetuity; growing perpetuity | 永續年金；成長型永續年金 | Level payments forever, worth $\mathrm{PMT}/i$; payments growing at $\gamma<i$, worth $\mathrm{PMT}_1/(i-\gamma)$. |
| Gordon growth model | 高登成長模型 | $P_0=D_1/(k-\gamma)$, (6.11); read backwards, $k=D_1/P_0+\gamma$. |
| Yield to maturity | 到期殖利率 | Single discount rate equating the promised cash flows to the price. |
| Internal rate of return | 內部報酬率 | Rate that sets the NPV to zero. |
| Level-payment / equal-principal loan | 本息平均攤還／本金平均攤還 | Equal payments, (6.16) / equal principal plus interest, (6.19). |
| Spot rate; implied forward rate | 即期利率；隱含遠期利率 | Zero-coupon yield; future-period rate implied by spot rates, (6.20). |
| Covered interest parity | 拋補利率平價 | No-arbitrage link between spot, forward and two interest rates, (6.21). |
| Cash-and-carry arbitrage | 現貨持有套利 | Buying the underlying and selling the futures (reverse: the opposite). |
| Basis; discount / premium | 基差；逆價差／正價差 | $F-S$; futures below / above the index. |
| Implied dividend yield | 隱含股利率 | $q$ solving $F=Se^{(r_f-q)\tau}$ at a given $r_f$, (6.24). |
| Implied financing rate | 隱含資金成本 | $r_f$ solving the same equation at a given $q$; the implied repo rate. |
| Total-return index | 報酬指數 | Index that reinvests cash dividends on their ex-dates. |
| Final settlement price | 最後結算價 | Cash-settlement price of an expiring TX contract: the average index over the last 30 minutes of the expiry day. |
