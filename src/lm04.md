## Introduction

Optimization chooses the best value of a decision variable: how many lots to buy, which leverage to hold, what yield prices a bond. In one variable the tools are elementary: a zero derivative at the top, the second derivative to tell tops from bottoms, and the endpoints. A sound answer also settles three questions: is the critical point the global top, does a constraint bind, and how much does the exact location matter? This module settles all three for the sizing problem every leveraged trader faces.

> [!case] Five point four times 0050
> Take 0050's 2,902 daily total returns from 3 November 2014 to 8 October 2026. Suppose you had held a constant leverage $f$: $f$ units of 0050 for each unit of equity, rebalanced every day and financed at zero cost. Which $f$ makes wealth grow fastest?
>
> Wealth after $T$ days is $\prod_t(1+fR_t)$, so the question is which $f$ maximizes the sample mean of $\ln(1+fR_t)$. The answer is **5.42**. At that leverage one unit of equity would have grown to about 1,122, against 10.0 for 0050 itself.
>
> Before acting on it, read the rest of [[ex:case]]. The same path fell 93.3% from peak to trough, and on 7 April 2025 alone it lost 54.2%. Half the leverage would have kept about three-quarters of the growth rate.
>
> The textbook rule $f\approx\mu/\sigma^2$ gives 5.68: close, but not equal. And the mean return is estimated with enough error that its 95% confidence band maps into anything from 2.7× to 8.6×.
>
> Which of these numbers, if any, should guide a decision? By Section 7 you will be able to derive each of them. You will see why the optimum is unique, and why its value is robust while its location is not. And you will see why the in-sample 5.42× describes the past rather than recommends a position.

## First- and Second-Order Conditions {lo=1}

Optimization starts with a function of one decision variable, such as growth against leverage, and asks where it is largest. Calculus answers in two steps. The first-order condition locates the candidates; the second-order condition sorts them.

### Local and global extrema

> [!definition] Local and global maximum
> A point $c$ of the domain $D\subseteq\R$ of a function $h$ is a **global maximum** of $h$ on $D$ if $h(c)\ge h(x)$ for every $x\in D$. It is a **local maximum** if $h(c)\ge h(x)$ for every $x\in D$ in some open interval around $c$. A maximum is **strict** if the inequality is strict for $x\ne c$. Minima reverse the inequalities; maxima and minima together are **extrema**.

Every global maximum is a local one, but not conversely. A trader cares about the global maximum, while calculus sees only local information: the slope and curvature at a point. Section 3 shows when local information is enough.

### The first-order condition

> [!theorem] Fermat's theorem (first-order condition)
> If $h$ has a local extremum at an interior point $c$ of its domain and $h$ is differentiable at $c$, then $h'(c)=0$.

*Proof.* Let $c$ be a local maximum. For small $\Delta>0$, $h(c+\Delta)-h(c)\le0$, so the right difference quotient $[h(c+\Delta)-h(c)]/\Delta$ is $\le0$ and its limit gives $h'(c)\le0$. For small $\Delta<0$ the numerator is still $\le0$ but the denominator is negative, so the left quotient is $\ge0$ and $h'(c)\ge0$. Hence $h'(c)=0$; a minimum reverses the signs. $\blacksquare$

> [!definition] Critical point
> An interior point $c$ of the domain at which $h'(c)=0$, or at which $h'(c)$ does not exist, is a **critical point** of $h$.

Three warnings follow from the proof. The condition is necessary, not sufficient: $h(x)=x^3$ has $h'(0)=0$ and no extremum. It applies only at interior points; at an endpoint the one-sided argument yields an inequality instead (Section 4). And a kink, such as $\lvert x\rvert$ at 0, is a candidate even though no derivative exists there.

### The second-order condition

> [!theorem] Second-derivative test
> Let $h'(c)=0$ and let $h''$ be continuous near $c$. If $h''(c)<0$, then $c$ is a strict local maximum; if $h''(c)>0$, a strict local minimum. If $h''(c)=0$, the test is inconclusive.

*Proof.* Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder ([[LM3]], eq. (3.5)) with $n=1$ gives $h(c+\Delta)=h(c)+h'(c)\Delta+\tfrac12h''(\xi)\Delta^2$ for some $\xi$ between $c$ and $c+\Delta$. The linear term vanishes by the first-order condition. If $h''(c)<0$, continuity keeps $h''<0$ on a small interval around $c$, so $h(c+\Delta)<h(c)$ for every small $\Delta\ne0$. The case $h''(c)>0$ is symmetric. $\blacksquare$

When $h''(c)=0$, look at higher derivatives. If the first non-zero derivative at $c$ has order $k$, a Taylor expansion to order $k$ fixes the sign of $h(c+\Delta)-h(c)$. For even $k$ the point is an extremum, a maximum if $h^{(k)}(c)<0$; for odd $k$ the graph crosses its tangent and there is no extremum. [[ex:classify]] collects the cases.

### Growth as a function of leverage

Now the decision this module is about. A position of $f$ units of an asset per unit of equity turns a return $R$ into $fR$, and one period multiplies wealth by $1+fR$. Wealth compounds, so the natural objective is the expected log growth per period,

$$g(f):=\E\big[\ln(1+fR)\big]. \tag{4.1}$$

Section 5 shows why maximizing $g$ maximizes long-run wealth; here only its shape matters. Expand $\ln(1+x)\approx x-x^2/2$ ([[LM3]], eq. (3.3)) with $x=fR$, take expectations, and use $\E[R^2]=\sigma^2+\mu^2$ (the population form of [[LM3]]'s identity (3.7)):

$$g(f)\approx f\mu-\frac{f^2}{2}\big(\sigma^2+\mu^2\big). \tag{4.2}$$

The neglected terms are of third order in $fR$. This quadratic model is a downward parabola. Its first-order condition $\mu-f(\sigma^2+\mu^2)=0$ gives

$$f_q=\frac{\mu}{\sigma^2+\mu^2}\approx\frac{\mu}{\sigma^2}, \tag{4.3}$$

and $g''=-(\sigma^2+\mu^2)<0$ confirms a strict maximum. The second step drops $\mu^2$, which is smaller than $\sigma^2$ by the factor $(\mu/\sigma)^2$, the squared Sharpe ratio per period: 0.0049 for 0050's daily returns. At the optimum $g(f_q)=\mu^2/\big(2(\sigma^2+\mu^2)\big)\approx\tfrac12(\mu/\sigma)^2$, so maximal growth is about half the squared Sharpe ratio per period. [[LM42]] derives the continuous-time versions, $f^*=(\mu-r_f)/\sigma^2$ and $g^*=r_f+\mathrm{SR}^2/2$.

The predicted maximal growth is $\hat\mu^2/(2m_2)=0.2462\%$ a day, or 59.85% a year over 243.1 trading days. The exact in-sample optimum (Section 6) is 5.42, with 58.84% a year. The rule's leverage is 4% too high, while the growth it predicts is only 1.0 percentage point too high.
> >
> > (2) Here $\mu=0.5\%$ and $\E[R^2]=\tfrac12(0.02^2+0.01^2)=0.00025$, so $f_q=20$ with predicted growth $\mu^2/(2\E[R^2])=5.00\%$ per trade, against the exact 25× and 5.889%. The rule fails because at $f=20$ the levered outcomes are +40% and −20%. That is far outside the range $\lvert x\rvert\le0.1$ where $x-x^2/2$ is accurate (see the pitfall on large returns in [[LM3]]).
> >
> > For 0050 at $f\approx5.4$, a one-standard-deviation day moves equity by 6.7%. But 284 of the 2,902 days, about one in ten, move it by more than 10%, which is why the exact optimum differs from the rule.

> [!example] Order size under linear price impact {#impact}
> A trader expects a stock priced at NT\$100 to outperform by 0.60% before the position is closed: NT\$600 of expected alpha per lot of 1,000 shares. Buying $x$ lots raises the average purchase price by NT\$0.01 per share for every lot bought. This is an illustrative linear impact model of the kind analysed by Almgren and Chriss (2000). How many lots maximize expected profit?
>
> > [!solution]
> > The impact premium is $0.01x$ per share on $1{,}000x$ shares, so the impact cost is $10x^2$ (in NT\$) and expected profit is $\pi(x)=600x-10x^2$. The first-order condition $600-20x=0$ gives $x^*=30$ lots, and $\pi''(x)=-20<0$ confirms a strict maximum. Expected profit is NT\$9,000 on gross alpha of NT\$18,000: impact consumes exactly half.
> >
> > The half is no coincidence. For any objective $ax-\tfrac b2x^2$ the optimum is $x^*=a/b$, and the cost there is $\tfrac b2(a/b)^2=\tfrac12ax^*$. The Kelly model (4.2) has the same form with $a=\mu$ and $b=m_2$, so at its optimum volatility drag consumes half the levered arithmetic return. On 0050 at the exact optimum, a levered arithmetic mean of 114.83% a year becomes log growth of 58.84%: 51% is kept.

> [!pitfall] A zero derivative is not a maximum
> Setting $h'(x)=0$ finds every critical point: maxima, minima and inflection points alike. The first-order condition of a minimum looks exactly like that of a maximum. Only the second-order condition, or concavity on the whole domain (Section 3), tells them apart. Check one of them before calling a critical point optimal.

> [!zh] 先找平的地方，再看往哪邊彎
> 在區間內部的極值點，切線一定是水平的（一階條件 $h'(c)=0$），但切線水平不保證是山頂：可能是谷底，也可能像 $x^3$ 在 0 那樣只是反曲點。二階條件看彎曲方向，$h''<0$ 向下彎才是局部最大。凱利的二次近似 $f\mu-\tfrac{f^2}{2}(\sigma^2+\mu^2)$ 是開口向下的拋物線，所以一階條件給出的 $f\approx\mu/\sigma^2$ 就是最大值。但它只在 $fR$ 不大時準確：+2%/−1% 的賭局開 20 倍，結果變成 +40%/−20%，二次近似就偏離真正的 25 倍。

>

## Concavity and Global Optima {lo=2}

The second-derivative test is local: it compares $c$ only with its neighbours. A sizing decision needs a global answer — no leverage anywhere does better. Concavity converts local information into that global guarantee.

> [!definition] Concave and convex functions
> A function $h$ is **concave** on an interval $I$ if, for all $x,y\in I$ and $\lambda\in[0,1]$,
> $$h\big(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\big)\ge\lambda h(x)+(1-\lambda)h(y),$$
> that is, every chord lies on or below the graph. It is **strictly concave** if the inequality is strict whenever $x\ne y$ and $0<\lambda<1$. A function $h$ is **convex** (strictly convex) if $-h$ is concave (strictly concave).

For twice-differentiable functions there is a working test, stated here without proof (Simon and Blume 1994, chapter 21). On an open interval, $h$ is concave exactly when $h''\le0$ throughout, and $h''<0$ implies strict concavity. The converse of the last statement fails: $-x^4$ is strictly concave although its second derivative vanishes at 0.

> [!theorem] For a concave function, a critical point is a global maximum
> Let $h$ be differentiable and concave on an interval $I$, and let $c$ be an interior point of $I$ with $h'(c)=0$. Then $c$ is a global maximum of $h$ on $I$. If $h$ is strictly concave, $c$ is the only maximizer.

*Proof (when $h''\le0$ on $I$).* For any $x\in I$, Taylor's theorem (3.5) with $n=1$ gives $h(x)=h(c)+h'(c)(x-c)+\tfrac12h''(\xi)(x-c)^2\le h(c)$, because $h'(c)=0$ and $h''(\xi)\le0$. If $h''<0$ on $I$, the inequality is strict for $x\ne c$. $\blacksquare$ The Deep Dive proves the theorem without second derivatives.

One corollary is used constantly. A strictly concave function has at most one critical point, and if it has one, that point is the global maximizer. Finding the optimum then becomes a pure root-finding problem, the subject of Section 6.

> [!deepdive]- A concave function lies below its tangent lines
> Let $h$ be concave and differentiable on $I$, with $c,x\in I$. For $t\in(0,1]$, concavity with weight $t$ on $x$ gives $h\big(c+t(x-c)\big)\ge(1-t)\,h(c)+t\,h(x)$. Subtract $h(c)$ and divide by $t>0$:
> $$\frac{h\big(c+t(x-c)\big)-h(c)}{t}\ge h(x)-h(c).$$
>
> As $t\to0^+$ the left side tends to $h'(c)(x-c)$. Hence $h(x)\le h(c)+h'(c)(x-c)$ for every $x\in I$: the graph lies below each of its tangent lines. If $h'(c)=0$, this reads $h(x)\le h(c)$, which proves the theorem with no assumption on $h''$. $\blacksquare$

### The log-growth function is strictly concave

Apply this to the sample version of (4.1). For returns $R_1,\dots,R_T$, the **empirical growth function** is

$$\bar g(f):=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T\ln(1+fR_t), \tag{4.4}$$

defined on the set $D$ of leverages with $1+fR_t>0$ for every $t$. If the sample contains both gains and losses, $D$ is the open interval $\bigl(-1/R_{\max},\ 1/\lvert R_{\min}\rvert\bigr)$. Differentiating term by term,

$$\bar g'(f)=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T\frac{R_t}{1+fR_t}, \tag{4.5}$$

$$\bar g''(f)=-\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T\frac{R_t^2}{(1+fR_t)^2}<0. \tag{4.6}$$

> [!result] Existence and uniqueness of the empirical Kelly leverage
> Suppose a sample contains at least one gain and one loss. Then $\bar g$ is strictly concave on $D=\bigl(-1/R_{\max},\,1/\lvert R_{\min}\rvert\bigr)$ and has exactly one maximizer $f^*\in D$, the root of $\bar g'(f)=0$.

Uniqueness follows from (4.6), since $\bar g'$ is continuous and strictly decreasing on $D$. For existence, let $f$ rise towards $1/\lvert R_{\min}\rvert$: the worst day's term $R_{\min}/(1+fR_{\min})$ tends to $-\infty$, so $\bar g'\to-\infty$. As $f$ falls towards $-1/R_{\max}$, the best day's term tends to $+\infty$. By the intermediate value theorem $\bar g'$ crosses zero exactly once.

The same edges show that $\bar g(f)\to-\infty$: near $1/\lvert R_{\min}\rvert$ the worst day all but wipes out the account.

For 0050 the largest daily loss and gain were both exactly 10%: −10.00% on 7 April 2025 and +10.00% on 31 July 2026. Ten per cent is Taiwan's daily price limit, in force since 1 June 2015 (it was 7% before). So $D=(-10,10)$, and at 10× a single limit-down day ends the experiment. > [!example] Where the parabola misleads {#zerogrowth}
> A familiar rule says growth returns to zero at about twice the Kelly leverage. Check it on 0050, and explain the gap using the worst day in the sample.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The quadratic model's zero is $2f_q=2\hat\mu/m_2=11.30$, outside $D$. The exact zero-growth leverage, the root of $\bar g(f)=0$ above $f^*$, is 9.74, or 1.80 times $f^*$. It has no closed form; Section 6 shows how to find it.
> >

> >
> > At $f^*$ the same day costs −0.78 log points out of a total of 7.02. The logarithm punishes a large loss far more than it rewards an equal gain: $\ln(1-0.54)=-0.78$, but $\ln(1+0.54)=+0.43$. Fat tails therefore pull the growth curve down faster than any parabola as leverage approaches the ruin point.

> [!pitfall] Local optima of a non-concave objective
> "Local equals global" is a property of the objective, not of the search. A backtest's Sharpe ratio is usually bumpy in its parameters; a grid search returns the highest bump, which is largely noise ([[LM39]]). Kelly sizing is well posed because the logarithm of an affine function of $f$ is concave. Treat the optimum of a bumpy objective as a hypothesis.

> [!zh] 凹函數：局部最大就是全域最大
> 凹函數（concave，圖形向下彎、弦在圖形下方）有個關鍵性質：只要找到切線水平的點，它就是全域最大；嚴格凹時還是唯一的。平均對數成長 $\bar g(f)$ 是許多 $\ln(1+fR_t)$ 的平均，每一項都嚴格凹，所以只要樣本有漲有跌，凱利最適槓桿一定存在，而且只有一個。0050 單日最大漲跌都是 10%（台股漲跌幅限制），槓桿只能落在 −10 到 10 倍之間；越靠近 10 倍，一根跌停就讓帳戶歸零，曲線掉得比拋物線快得多。用語提醒：本書 concave＝凹函數、convex＝凸函數；有些微積分課本說的「凹口向上」其實是 convex。

## Constraints and Corner Solutions {lo=3}

Real positions face limits. A cash account cannot borrow, many mandates cannot sell short, and brokers cap margin. Each limit confines $f$ to an interval, and the optimum may then sit on the boundary, where the derivative need not be zero.

> [!theorem] Extreme value theorem
> A continuous function on a closed, bounded interval $[a,b]$ attains a maximum and a minimum on it.

The theorem is stated without proof (Stewart, chapter on applications of differentiation). It turns optimization on $[a,b]$ into a finite search, the **closed-interval method**. Evaluate $h$ at its critical points in $(a,b)$ and at both endpoints, and take the largest value; the method needs continuity only, not concavity.

### First-order conditions at a corner

At an endpoint the argument of Fermat's theorem runs on one side only, so it delivers an inequality. If a differentiable $h$ attains its maximum on $[a,b]$ at $b$, every left difference quotient $[h(b-\Delta)-h(b)]/(-\Delta)$ is $\ge0$, so $h'(b)\ge0$. Likewise a maximum at $a$ requires $h'(a)\le0$. Collecting the three cases,

$$\text{maximum at } c\in[a,b]\ \Longrightarrow\ \begin{cases} h'(a)\le0 & \text{if } c=a,\\ h'(c)=0 & \text{if } a<c<b,\\ h'(b)\ge0 & \text{if } c=b.\end{cases}$$

These are the one-variable **Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions**, which [[LM7]] extends with Lagrange multipliers. For a concave $h$ they are also sufficient, by the tangent-line inequality of the Deep Dive in Section 3.

The derivative at a binding corner has an economic reading. If the optimum is $b$ and $h'(b)>0$, relaxing the limit to $b+\Delta$ raises the objective by about $h'(b)\,\Delta$. That derivative is the **shadow price** of the constraint: what one more unit of permission is worth. In [[LM7]] it becomes the Lagrange multiplier.

> [!proposition] Clipping
> Let $h$ be concave on an open interval containing $[a,b]$, with an unconstrained maximizer $x^*$. Then the maximizer of $h$ on $[a,b]$ is $\min\big(\max(x^*,a),\,b\big)$: the unconstrained optimum, clipped to the interval.

*Proof.* If $x^*\in[a,b]$ there is nothing to show. If $x^*>b$, then $h'$ is non-increasing with $h'(x^*)=0$, so $h'\ge0$ on $[a,b]$ and $h$ is non-decreasing there; the maximum is at $b$. The case $x^*<a$ is symmetric. $\blacksquare$

## The Binary Kelly Bet {lo=4}

The simplest sizing problem has one source of risk and two outcomes. Its solution is exact. It exposes the confusion between position size and risk size, and it shows how sharply the optimum depends on the win probability. [[LM41]] extends it to general discrete bets.

### Why maximize expected log growth

A trade gains a fraction $G$ of the position with probability $p$ and loses a fraction $L$ with probability $q=1-p$, independently of other trades. With a position of $f$ times equity, one trade multiplies wealth by $1+fG$ or $1-fL$. After $n$ trades with $k$ wins,

$$\frac{W_n}{W_0}=(1+fG)^k(1-fL)^{n-k},\qquad \frac1n\ln\frac{W_n}{W_0}=\frac kn\ln(1+fG)+\Big(1-\frac kn\Big)\ln(1-fL).$$

By the law of large numbers ([[LM17]]), $k/n\to p$, so the per-trade log growth converges to

$$g(f)=p\ln(1+fG)+q\ln(1-fL),\qquad -\frac1G<f<\frac1L. \tag{4.7}$$

If $g(f_1)>g(f_2)$, the ratio of the two wealth paths behaves like $e^{n[g(f_1)-g(f_2)]}$ and grows without bound. The fraction with the highest $g$ therefore ends up with more wealth than any other fixed fraction, with probability one (Breiman 1961; Thorp 2006). That is Kelly's (1956) criterion: maximize the expected log growth.

### Deriving the Kelly position

From (4.7),

$$g'(f)=\frac{pG}{1+fG}-\frac{qL}{1-fL},\qquad g''(f)=-\frac{pG^2}{(1+fG)^2}-\frac{qL^2}{(1-fL)^2}<0.$$

The growth function is strictly concave (the Knowledge Check of Section 3), so the root of $g'$ is the unique global maximum. Setting $g'(f)=0$ and cross-multiplying gives $pG(1-fL)=qL(1+fG)$, so $pG-qL=fGL(p+q)=fGL$, and

$$f^*=\frac{pG-qL}{GL}=\frac pL-\frac qG. \tag{4.8}$$

No approximation was made: (4.8) is exact. Substituting it back gives $1+f^*G=p(G+L)/L$ and $1-f^*L=q(G+L)/G$, so the maximal growth rate is

$$g^*=p\ln\frac{p\,(G+L)}{L}+q\ln\frac{q\,(G+L)}{G}. \tag{4.9}$$

A rewriting makes (4.8) easier to read. Let $\pi:=L/(G+L)$ be the **break-even probability**, at which the expected return $pG-qL$ is zero. Then

$$f^*=\Big(\frac1L+\frac1G\Big)(p-\pi). \tag{4.10}$$

The Kelly position is proportional to the probability edge $p-\pi$, with slope $1/L+1/G$. It is positive exactly when the expected return is positive. A bet with no edge has, under a long-only rule, the corner solution $f=0$.

> [!result] The binary Kelly bet
> For a bet that gains $G$ with probability $p$ and loses $L$ with probability $q=1-p$, the growth-maximizing position is exactly $f^*=p/L-q/G=(1/L+1/G)(p-\pi)$ times equity, where $\pi=L/(G+L)$. The growth function is strictly concave, so $f^*$ is unique, and the maximal growth (4.9) is positive whenever $p>\pi$.

### Position size versus risk size

On a losing trade the account loses $fL$ of equity; call $\rho:=fL$ the **risk fraction**. Multiplying (4.8) by $L$ and writing $b:=G/L$ for the payoff (reward-to-risk) ratio,

$$\rho^*=f^*L=p-\frac qb. \tag{4.11}$$

This is the classic Kelly formula for a bet paying $b$ to 1, which Thorp (2006) writes as $(bp-q)/b$. When the whole stake can be lost, $L=1$ and position equals risk. A trade with a stop-loss loses only $L$ of the position, so the two numbers differ by the factor $1/L$: 100 for a 1% stop.

Equation (4.11) carries a second message. Scaling $G$ and $L$ by the same factor leaves $b$, $\rho^*$, $1+f^*G$ and $1-f^*L$ unchanged, so by (4.9) it leaves $g^*$ unchanged too. Only the payoff ratio and the win probability determine the attainable growth. The stop distance sets the position size that delivers it.

> [!example] The +2% / −1% bet {#coin}
> A trade gains 2% or loses 1% of the position with equal probability. Find the Kelly position, the risk per trade and the maximal growth. Then evaluate a trader who reads "Kelly = 25%" as "put 25% of capital into the trade".
>
> > [!solution]
> > By (4.8), $f^*=0.5/0.01-0.5/0.02=50-25=25$: a position of 25 times equity. A loss costs $25\times1\%=25\%$ of equity, the risk fraction of (4.11): with $b=2$, $\rho^*=0.5-0.5/2=0.25$. Growth at the optimum is $g^*=0.5\ln1.5+0.5\ln0.75=5.889\%$ per trade, from a levered arithmetic mean of $25\times0.5\%=12.5\%$.
> >
> > The trader who holds 0.25 times equity risks only 0.25% per trade. Growth is $0.5\ln1.005+0.5\ln0.9975=0.1242\%$ per trade, 2.1% of the maximum. "25× equity" and "25% at risk" are the same decision in two units; mixing the units costs a factor of 100.
> >
> > The same curve has two more landmarks. Its domain is $(-50,100)$, so one loss at 100× ends the account. And growth returns to zero at exactly $50=2f^*$, because $(1+50\times0.02)(1-50\times0.01)=2\times0.5=1$.

> [!deepdive]- Kelly's information interpretation: maximal growth is a relative entropy
> Write (4.9) with $\pi=L/(G+L)$ and $1-\pi=G/(G+L)$:
> $$g^*=p\ln\frac p\pi+q\ln\frac q{1-\pi}=:D(p\,\Vert\,\pi).$$
> $D$ is the **relative entropy**, or Kullback–Leibler divergence, between the true probability $p$ and the break-even probability $\pi$. Using $\ln x\le x-1$ (Practice Problem 5 of [[LM3]]),
> $$-D(p\Vert\pi)=p\ln\frac\pi p+q\ln\frac{1-\pi}q\le p\Big(\frac\pi p-1\Big)+q\Big(\frac{1-\pi}q-1\Big)=0,$$
> with equality only if $p=\pi$.
>
> Allowing $f<0$ (betting against the trade), maximal growth is positive whenever the true probability differs from the break-even one. It measures that difference in information units. This is the "new interpretation of information rate" of Kelly (1956); Cover and Thomas (2006, chapter 6) develop it for horse races. $\blacksquare$

> [!pitfall] "Kelly says 25%" without a unit
> The same decision can be stated as a 25× position or as 25% of equity at risk. Read as "25% of capital in the trade", it becomes a position one hundred times too small. Read as "25× leverage" on a trade with a 10% stop, it puts 250% of equity at risk: one loss is ruin.
>
> State a Kelly number with its unit: a multiple of equity, or the fraction of equity lost if the trade fails. Recompute the position whenever the stop distance changes.

## Root Finding: Bisection and Newton's Method {lo=5}

The binary bet is the exception: most first-order conditions have no closed form. The empirical Kelly condition $\bar g'(f)=0$, the sample version of $\E[R/(1+fR)]=0$, becomes for 2,902 returns a polynomial equation of degree 2,901 once denominators are cleared. A bond's yield and a project's internal rate of return are roots of polynomials too. Numerical root finding solves $\varphi(x)=0$ to any accuracy, and for a smooth concave objective $h$, maximizing $h$ is root finding on $\varphi=h'$.

### Bisection

> [!definition] Bisection method
> Let $\varphi$ be continuous on $[a,b]$, with $\varphi(a)$ and $\varphi(b)$ of opposite signs. Evaluate $\varphi$ at the midpoint $m=(a+b)/2$ and keep the half-interval whose endpoints still have opposite signs. Repeat.

By the intermediate value theorem every interval in the sequence contains a root. After $n$ steps the bracket has width $(b-a)/2^n$, so its midpoint is within $(b-a)/2^{n+1}$ of a root. A bracket of width $\varepsilon$ takes

$$n\ge\log_2\frac{b-a}{\varepsilon}$$

steps: one binary digit per step, or about 3.3 steps per decimal digit. The error bound shrinks by a constant factor each step, which is called **linear convergence**. Bisection needs only continuity and a sign change, and it cannot fail.

### Newton's method

Newton's method replaces $\varphi$ by its tangent at the current point, the first-order Taylor polynomial of [[LM3]], and jumps to the tangent's root:

$$x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{\varphi(x_n)}{\varphi'(x_n)}. \tag{4.12}$$

Applied to $\varphi=h'$ for a maximization, the step is $x_{n+1}=x_n-h'(x_n)/h''(x_n)$: each iteration maximizes the second-order Taylor model of $h$ at $x_n$.

The speed comes from the remainder. Let $x^*$ be a root with $\varphi'(x^*)\ne0$ and write $e_n=x_n-x^*$. Taylor's theorem gives $0=\varphi(x^*)=\varphi(x_n)+\varphi'(x_n)(x^*-x_n)+\tfrac12\varphi''(\xi_n)(x^*-x_n)^2$ for some $\xi_n$ between $x_n$ and $x^*$. Dividing by $\varphi'(x_n)$ and using (4.12),

$$e_{n+1}=\frac{\varphi''(\xi_n)}{2\,\varphi'(x_n)}\,e_n^2. \tag{4.13}$$

The error is squared at each step: **quadratic convergence**. Once $\lvert e_n\rvert$ is small, the number of correct digits roughly doubles per iteration. The constant $C=\lvert\varphi''(x^*)/(2\varphi'(x^*))\rvert$ says how small is small: squaring helps once $C\lvert e_n\rvert<1$.

> [!theorem] Local quadratic convergence of Newton's method
> Let $\varphi$ be twice continuously differentiable near a root $x^*$ with $\varphi'(x^*)\ne0$. Then there is an interval around $x^*$ such that Newton's method started anywhere in it converges to $x^*$. Moreover, $\lvert e_{n+1}\rvert\le K\lvert e_n\rvert^2$ for some constant $K$.

The theorem is stated without its full proof; (4.13) is the core, and Burden, Faires and Burden (2016, chapter 2) supply the details. It is a local result. Far from the root the tangent can point anywhere. Where $\varphi'(x_n)\approx0$ the step is huge, and a step can leave the domain or land near a different root.

**Safeguarded methods** combine the two ideas. They keep a bracket, accept a fast step (Newton or secant) when it lands inside it, and bisect otherwise. Brent's (1973) method, available as `scipy.optimize.brentq`, is the standard version: it never loses the bracket and converges superlinearly near the root.

### The empirical Kelly condition

Newton's method on $\varphi=\bar g'$ uses (4.5) and (4.6). Start from $f_0=0$, where $\bar g'(0)=\hat\mu$ and $\bar g''(0)=-m_2$:

$$f_1=0-\frac{\hat\mu}{-m_2}=\frac{\hat\mu}{m_2}=\frac{\hat\mu}{s^2+\hat\mu^2}.$$

The quadratic rule (4.3) is exactly one Newton step from zero. Each later step refits the quadratic model at the current point. [[ex:iter]] runs both methods on 0050; bisection starts from the bracket $[0,10]$, since $\bar g'(0)=\hat\mu>0$ and $\bar g'\to-\infty$ as $f\to10$.

> > Two terms give the linear equation $m_1-fm_2=0$: the quadratic rule, $f=5.652$. Three terms give a quadratic equation whose root in $D$ is 5.753, further away, because 0050's daily returns have a small positive skewness (0.037) and so $m_3>0$. Four terms give a cubic with one real root, 5.465, against the exact 5.421.
> >
> > The series converges, since $\lvert f^*R_t\rvert\le0.54$ on every day, but slowly and not monotonically. On the ±10% days the terms shrink only like $0.54^k$, and those days make the fourth moment large: excess kurtosis is 8.12. Four Newton steps on the exact condition beat any number of moments. The lesson matches [[LM3]]: when moves are large, compute the exact function.

> [!pitfall] Newton's method without a bracket
> Newton's method trusts the tangent. Cash flows of −100, +230 and −132 (a project with a closing cost) have two internal rates of return, 10% and 20%. Their NPV is flat at 14.8%. Newton started at 14% first jumps to −1.20% and ends at 10%; started at 15%, it jumps to 72.50% and ends at 20%.
>
> On 0050's Kelly condition Newton converged from each of 199 starting points between −9.9 and 9.9 (a grid of step 0.1), in at most eight steps. That is a property of the function, not of the method: use a bracketing method (`brentq`) whenever a sign change is known.

> [!zh] 二分法與牛頓法
> 二分法每一步把區間砍半，穩但慢：每多一位小數大約要 3.3 步，只要函數連續、端點異號就一定收斂。牛頓法用切線逼近，誤差每一步大約平方一次，正確位數接近翻倍；但它需要好的起點，切線太平時會跳得很遠，可能跳出定義域或跑到另一個根。實務上兩者混合（scipy 的 brentq）：用區間保證不跑掉，再用快速的步驟收斂。最妙的是：從 $f=0$ 出發的第一步牛頓法，剛好就是二次近似 $\hat\mu/(s^2+\hat\mu^2)$；0050 只要四步就到機器精度，二分法要 44 步。

> [!kc]

>
> > [!answer]-
> > The width after $n$ steps is $0.10/2^n$, so we need $2^n\ge0.10/10^{-6}=10^5$, i.e. $n\ge\log_2 10^5=16.6$. Seventeen steps suffice.

## Sensitivity of the Optimum: Flat Tops and the Envelope Theorem {lo=6,7}

Once an optimum is found, two questions remain: how much is lost by missing it, and how do the optimum and its value move when the inputs change? The second-order term of a Taylor expansion answers the first, the envelope theorem the second. Together they resolve the case.

### Flat tops

At an interior maximum the first-order term of the Taylor expansion vanishes, so

$$g(f)=g^*+\tfrac12g''(\xi)(f-f^*)^2\approx g^*+\tfrac12g''(f^*)(f-f^*)^2, \tag{4.14}$$

where the approximation drops terms of third order in $f-f^*$. The loss from mis-sizing is of second order in the sizing error. An error twice as large costs four times as much, and a small error costs very little. The top of a smooth maximum is flat.

In the quadratic model the flat top has an exact form. Write (4.2) as $g(f)=f\mu-\tfrac12f^2m_2$, with $f^*=\mu/m_2$ and $g^*=\mu^2/(2m_2)$. Substituting $f=\lambda f^*$,

$$g(\lambda f^*)=\lambda\frac{\mu^2}{m_2}-\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\,\frac{\mu^2}{m_2}=(2\lambda-\lambda^2)\,g^*=\big[1-(1-\lambda)^2\big]\,g^*. \tag{4.15}$$

> [!result] Fractional Kelly in the quadratic model
> A position of $\lambda$ times the Kelly leverage keeps the fraction $1-(1-\lambda)^2$ of the maximal growth rate, with $\lambda$ times the volatility. Half Kelly keeps 75% of the growth at half the volatility; quarter Kelly keeps 43.75% at a quarter. Over-betting mirrors under-betting: $1.5f^*$ also keeps 75%, at three times the volatility of half Kelly. Growth is zero at $\lambda=2$ and negative beyond.

Thorp (2006) states the same result as $c(2-c)$ for a bet of $cf^*$. [[ex:share]] compares (4.15) with the exact curves for 0050 and for the +2%/−1% bet.

> [!exhibit] Growth kept at a fraction λ of the Kelly leverage {#share}
> <div data-chart="lm04-share"></div>
>
> Note: 0050 uses the exact in-sample ratio $\bar g(\lambda f^*)/\bar g(f^*)$ with $f^*=5.42$; the curve ends at $\lambda=1.84$, where 10× leverage meets the −10% day. The bet uses (4.7) with $f^*=25$.

Below full Kelly the three curves nearly coincide: half Kelly keeps 75% in the model, 74.2% for 0050 and 76.1% for the bet. Beyond full Kelly they part: the bet's curve is exactly symmetric about $\lambda=1$ and reaches zero at $\lambda=2$, as [[eg:coin]] found. 0050 falls away: 1.5 times Kelly keeps 69.1% instead of 75%, 1.75 times keeps 17.9%, and growth is zero at 1.80 times. Over-betting a fat-tailed asset is far worse than the parabola suggests; under-betting is not.

> >

The predicted loss at $f^*/2$ is $\tfrac12\times0.0439\times2.711^2=16.1\%$ a year, against an actual loss of $58.84\%-43.63\%=15.2\%$. The quadratic term gets the size of the loss right to within one percentage point, 2.7 units of leverage away from the top.

### The envelope theorem

> [!theorem] Envelope theorem (one variable)
> Let $F(x,\theta)$ be differentiable, and for each $\theta$ let $x^*(\theta)$ be an interior maximizer of $F(\cdot,\theta)$ that depends differentiably on $\theta$. Then the value function $V(\theta):=F\big(x^*(\theta),\theta\big)$ satisfies
> $$V'(\theta)=\frac{\partial F}{\partial\theta}\big(x^*(\theta),\theta\big). \tag{4.16}$$

*Proof.* By the chain rule, $V'(\theta)=F_x\big(x^*(\theta),\theta\big)\,x^{*\prime}(\theta)+F_\theta\big(x^*(\theta),\theta\big)$. The first-order condition sets $F_x=0$ at the maximizer, which leaves $F_\theta$. $\blacksquare$

The theorem is the flat top seen from the side. When a parameter moves the optimizer moves too, but to first order this does not change the value. To learn how the best achievable value responds, differentiate the objective with respect to the parameter at the old optimum; no re-optimization is needed. Simon and Blume (1994, chapter 19) give the multivariable version; three applications follow.

- **The value of edge.** In the quadratic model without the $\mu^2$ term, $g(f)=f\mu-\tfrac12f^2\sigma^2$ and $V(\mu)=\mu^2/(2\sigma^2)$, so $V'(\mu)=\mu/\sigma^2=f^*$. The envelope theorem gets there in one line: $\partial g/\partial\mu=f$, evaluated at $f^*$. The marginal value of edge is the position you hold.
- **The value of win probability.** For the binary bet, $\partial g/\partial p=\ln(1+fG)-\ln(1-fL)$, so $dg^*/dp=\ln\big[(1+f^*G)/(1-f^*L)\big]$. For the +2%/−1% bet this is $\ln(1.5/0.75)=\ln2=0.693$: one more point of win probability adds about 0.69% of growth per trade. The exact change from $p=0.50$ to 0.51 is 0.71%.
- **The cost of financing**, worked out in [[eg:financing]].

> [!example] What a higher financing rate costs a levered 0050 position {#financing}
> Suppose the investor borrows (and lends idle cash) at a rate $r$ per day, so that one day multiplies wealth by $1+r+f(R_t-r)$. Use the envelope theorem to find how maximal in-sample growth responds to $r$ near zero. Check the answer for an illustrative financing rate of 2% a year.
>
> > [!solution]
> > With $F(f,r)=\frac1T\sum_t\ln\big(1+r+f(R_t-r)\big)$, the partial derivative is $\partial F/\partial r=(1-f)\,\frac1T\sum_t1/\big(1+r+f(R_t-r)\big)$. At $r=0$ and $f=f^*$ the first-order condition does the work:
> > $$\frac1T\sum_t\frac1{1+f^*R_t}=\frac1T\sum_t\frac{(1+f^*R_t)-f^*R_t}{1+f^*R_t}=1-f^*\,\bar g'(f^*)=1.$$
> > So $V'(0)=1-f^*=-4.42$ exactly: each unit of financing rate costs 4.42 units of maximal growth. In annual terms, a rate one percentage point higher costs about 4.42 percentage points a year of growth. The investor is short the financing rate on the $f^*-1=4.42$ borrowed units.
> >
> > At 2% a year the first-order estimate is $-4.42\times2\%=-8.84\%$. Re-optimizing exactly gives $f^*=4.96$ and growth of 50.46% a year, a fall of 8.38%. Keeping the old leverage of 5.42 still earns 49.99%: re-optimizing is worth only 0.47% a year, which is precisely the envelope theorem's point.

### How well the data locate the optimum

The flat top has a second face. If a wide range of leverages earns nearly the same growth, the data can barely tell those leverages apart. The value of the optimum is robust; its location is fragile.

For 0050 the quadratic rule $f^*\approx\mu/\sigma^2$ is proportional to the mean return, so relative errors in $\hat\mu$ pass one for one into $f^*$. For independent returns, the standard error of a sample mean is $s/\sqrt T$; it is stated here without proof and derived in [[LM20]]. For 0050 it is 0.0230% a day against $\hat\mu=0.0871\%$, a relative error of 26%.

A 95% interval, $\hat\mu\pm1.96$ standard errors, runs from 0.042% to 0.132% a day. Through $\mu/s^2$, that is a Kelly leverage anywhere from 2.7 to 8.6.

The data's own history agrees. Over trailing three-year windows ([[ex:rolling]]) the in-sample optimum wandered from 0.94 to 8.87, a range of more than nine to one. The low came in the window ending 17 January 2024, the high in the window ending 13 August 2018. The latest window says 6.63.

>
> Note: each point maximizes the mean of $\ln(1+fR_t)$ over the preceding 729 trading days (about three years); the first window ends on 23 October 2017. The dashed line is the quadratic rule $\hat\mu/s^2$ on the same window, and the horizontal line marks the full-sample optimum, 5.42. Source: as [[ex:case]].

The cost of the error is asymmetric. If the true Kelly leverage were 2.7, holding 5.42 would be $\lambda=1.98$, which keeps only 4.5% of the attainable growth by (4.15). With 0050's fat tails, whose exact curve reaches zero at $\lambda=1.80$, it would probably lose money. If the true value were 8.6, holding 5.42 would be $\lambda=0.63$ and keep 86%.

Under-betting costs a little; over-betting can cost everything. That asymmetry, not timidity, is the case for fractional Kelly, a quarter to a half of $f^*$. [[LM24]] and [[LM44]] formalize it, and [[LM45]] adds parameter uncertainty. MacLean, Thorp and Ziemba (2010) summarize the trade-off: maximal long-run growth, but large positions and a real chance of losing most of the wealth.

> [!pitfall] "The backtest Sharpe ratio is 3, so use full Kelly"
> In the quadratic model $f^*\approx\mu/\sigma^2=\mathrm{SR}/\sigma$ and $g^*\approx\mathrm{SR}^2/2$, with SR and σ annualized ([[LM42]]). A backtest Sharpe ratio of 3 at 15% volatility suggests $f=3/0.15=20$ and log growth of 4.5 a year. That is a 90-fold gain every year, which should itself raise suspicion.
>
> If the true Sharpe ratio is half the backtest's, 20× is twice Kelly and growth is zero. If it is 1, 20× is three times Kelly, and by (4.15) growth is $(2\times3-3^2)\times1^2/2=-1.5$ a year. Wealth then falls 78% a year despite a genuine edge. Backtests overstate Sharpe ratios ([[LM38]], [[LM39]]): size from a haircut estimate, then take a fraction of Kelly.

### Resolving the case

Each number in the Motivating Case now has its place.

- **5.42** is the unique global maximizer of in-sample growth, because $\bar g$ is strictly concave (Section 3). Newton's method finds it in four steps from zero (Section 6).
- **5.68** is the quadratic rule $\hat\mu/s^2$, and 5.65 its version with $\hat\mu^2$ kept; the latter is one Newton step from zero. Both overshoot because the ±10% days fatten 0050's tails (Sections 2 and 6).
- **74.2%** is the flat top: half Kelly kept almost three-quarters of the growth rate at half the volatility (this section).
- **9.74** is the zero-growth leverage, 1.80 times $f^*$ rather than 2, pulled in by the largest losses, above all the limit-down day of 7 April 2025 (Section 3).
- **2.7 to 8.6** is the range of Kelly leverages consistent with the sampling error in $\hat\mu$, and the rolling windows wandered from 0.94 to 8.87 (this section).

The in-sample 5.42× is therefore a description of 2014–2026, not a recommendation. It treats the sample mean as the truth, assumes free daily rebalancing and ignores financing, which costs 8.38% a year of growth at a 2% rate. Even with perfect hindsight, it came with a 93.3% drawdown. This module supplies the calculus; [[LM20]] and [[LM45]] supply the statistics that must come before any leverage decision.

> [!zh] 最適點的位置脆弱，價值卻很穩
> 最大值附近曲線很平（一階項為零，只剩二階項）：槓桿抓錯一點，成長率幾乎不變。在二次模型裡，λ 倍凱利保留 $1-(1-\lambda)^2$ 的成長率：半凱利保留 75%、波動減半，0050 實際是 74.2%。但「平」的另一面是資料很難分辨最適點在哪：0050 平均報酬的估計誤差約 26%，凱利槓桿的 95% 區間約 2.7 到 8.6 倍，滾動三年的樣本內最適值在 0.94 到 8.87 倍之間跳動。而且押太多比押太少危險得多，0050 超過 1.8 倍凱利，長期成長率就轉負。包絡定理則說：參數變動對「最大成長率」的一階影響，只要對參數偏微分、在原最適點取值即可，不必重新最佳化；例如融資利率每高 1 個百分點，最大成長率約少 $f^*-1=4.42$ 個百分點。

## Optimization in Python {lo=7}

SciPy's `optimize` module contains every method of this module. Three habits keep results honest: pass the domain explicitly, give a bracket whenever one is known, and compare at least two methods.

```python
import numpy as np
import polars as pl
from scipy.optimize import brentq, minimize_scalar, newton

R = pl.read_parquet("etf_daily_returns.parquet")["R_0050"].to_numpy()

def gbar(f):                                  # mean log growth, eq. (4.4)
    return np.log1p(f * R).mean()

def dg(f):                                    # Kelly condition, eq. (4.5)
    return (R / (1 + f * R)).mean()

def d2g(f):                                   # curvature, eq. (4.6)
    return -((R / (1 + f * R)) ** 2).mean()

lo, hi = -1 / R.max(), 1 / -R.min()           # domain: 1 + f R_t > 0 for every t
f_brent = brentq(dg, 0.0, 0.999 * hi, xtol=1e-14)                  # bracketed root
f_newton = newton(dg, x0=0.0, fprime=d2g, tol=1e-14)               # Newton from f = 0
f_bound = minimize_scalar(lambda f: -gbar(f), bounds=(0.0, 0.999 * hi), method="bounded").x
print(f"domain ({lo:.2f}, {hi:.2f})  brentq {f_brent:.6f}  newton {f_newton:.6f}  bounded {f_bound:.4f}")
# domain (-10.00, 10.00)  brentq 5.421488  newton 5.421488  bounded 5.4215
```

`minimize_scalar` with `method="bounded"` maximizes $\bar g$ directly by Brent's derivative-free search. It stops at its default tolerance of about $10^{-5}$, which is why it is printed with fewer digits. The fractional-Kelly comparison of [[ex:share]] takes a few lines of Polars:

```python
A = len(R) / 11.937                           # 243.1 trading days a year in this sample
g_star = gbar(f_brent)
frac = pl.DataFrame({"lam": [0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.25, 1.5]})
frac = frac.with_columns(
    (pl.col("lam") * f_brent).alias("f"),
    pl.Series("growth", [gbar(l * f_brent) * A for l in frac["lam"]]),
).with_columns(
    (pl.col("growth") / (g_star * A)).alias("kept"),          # exact share of g*
    (1 - (1 - pl.col("lam")) ** 2).alias("quadratic"),        # eq. (4.15)
)
print(frac)   # kept: 0.429, 0.742, 0.934, 1.000, 0.928, 0.691
```

The same tools solve [[eg:ytm]] in one line once the price function is vectorized:

```python
def price(y, c=1.5, n=5, face=100.0):
    t = np.arange(1, n + 1)
    return (c / (1 + y) ** t).sum() + face / (1 + y) ** n

ytm = brentq(lambda y: price(y) - 97.80, 0.0, 0.10, xtol=1e-14)
print(f"{ytm:.6%}")                           # 1.966292%
```

## Summary {-}

- At an interior maximum of a differentiable function the derivative is zero (Fermat's theorem). A negative second derivative confirms a strict local maximum; a zero second derivative decides nothing, and higher derivatives must be examined.
- Concavity makes a critical point the global maximum, and strict concavity makes it unique. The empirical growth function $\bar g(f)$ is strictly concave, so the empirical Kelly leverage exists and is unique whenever the sample contains gains and losses.
- On a closed interval, compare critical points with endpoints. At a binding corner the derivative is the constraint's shadow price; for a concave objective the constrained optimum is the unconstrained one clipped to the interval.
- The binary Kelly bet has the exact solution $f^*=p/L-q/G=(1/L+1/G)(p-\pi)$. It is a position size; the risk size is $f^*L=p-q/b$. The position moves by $1/L+1/G$ per unit of win probability, and attainable growth depends only on $p$ and $b$.
- Bisection gains a binary digit per step; Newton's method squares the error near a simple root but needs a good start. On 0050's Kelly condition, Newton's first step from zero is the quadratic rule and four steps reach machine precision; bisection needs 44 for a bracket of $10^{-12}$.
- Near an optimum, growth is flat: $\lambda f^*$ keeps $1-(1-\lambda)^2$ of the maximum in the quadratic model. The envelope theorem prices parameter changes without re-optimizing: at the empirical Kelly point, each unit of financing rate costs $f^*-1$ units of growth.
- In-sample, 0050's growth-optimal leverage over 2014–2026 is 5.42, with 58.84% a year of log growth and a 93.3% drawdown. Sampling error spans 2.7 to 8.6, and over-betting costs far more than under-betting: the number describes the past and is not a recommendation.

## Glossary {-}

| Term | 中文 | Meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Critical point | 臨界點 | Interior point where $h'=0$ or $h'$ does not exist. |
| First-order condition | 一階條件 | $h'(c)=0$ at an interior extremum (Fermat's theorem). |
| Second-order condition | 二階條件 | Sign of $h''$ at a critical point: negative for a strict local maximum. |
| Local / global maximum | 局部／全域極大值 | Best value in a neighbourhood / on the whole domain. |
| Inflection point | 反曲點 | Point where curvature changes sign; not an extremum. |
| Concave function | 凹函數 | Chords lie on or below the graph ($h''\le0$); a critical point is a global maximum. |
| Convex function | 凸函數 | Chords lie on or above the graph ($h''\ge0$). |
| Extreme value theorem | 極值定理 | A continuous function on $[a,b]$ attains its maximum and minimum. |
| Corner solution | 角解 | Optimum at a boundary of the feasible interval. |
| Shadow price | 影子價格 | Gain in the objective per unit of relaxation of a binding constraint. |
| KKT conditions | KKT 條件 | First-order conditions with inequality constraints; at a corner, a sign condition on $h'$. |
| Kelly criterion | 凱利準則 | Choose the position that maximizes expected log growth. |
| Kelly position (fraction) | 凱利部位（比例） | Growth-optimal position $f^*$, as a multiple of equity. |
| Risk fraction | 風險比例 | Fraction of equity lost if a trade fails, $fL$. |
| Break-even probability | 損益兩平勝率 | $\pi=L/(G+L)$: the win probability with zero expected return. |
| Fractional Kelly | 分數凱利 | A position $\lambda f^*$ with $\lambda<1$, such as half or quarter Kelly. |
| Bisection method | 二分法 | Halve a sign-change bracket at each step; linear convergence. |
| Newton's method | 牛頓法 | $x_{n+1}=x_n-\varphi(x_n)/\varphi'(x_n)$; quadratic convergence near a simple root. |
| Quadratic convergence | 二次收斂 | $e_{n+1}\approx Ce_n^2$: correct digits roughly double per step. |
| Envelope theorem | 包絡定理 | $V'(\theta)=\partial F/\partial\theta$ evaluated at the optimum. |
| Yield to maturity | 到期殖利率 | Discount rate that equates a bond's present value to its price. |
| Internal rate of return | 內部報酬率 | Discount rate at which net present value is zero. |
| Relative entropy | 相對熵 | $D(p\Vert\pi)=\sum_i p_i\ln(p_i/\pi_i)$; equals the binary bet's $g^*$. |
