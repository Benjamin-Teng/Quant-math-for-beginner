## Introduction

Every portfolio decision turns more than one dial: how much 0050, how much bond, how much leverage. Single-variable calculus ([[LM2]], [[LM4]]) turns one dial at a time; this module turns them together, adds constraints and prices the constraints themselves. Applied to two assets, these tools produce the minimum-variance portfolio, the tangency portfolio and the Kelly fractions. They also show why an answer fitted to the past is not the answer you will get.

> [!case] How much bond minimizes risk?
> A TWD investor holds Yuanta Taiwan 50 (0050) and considers adding Yuanta U.S. Treasury 20+ Year Bond ETF (00679B). The fund tracks the ICE US Treasury 20+ Year Bond Index and is quoted in New Taiwan dollars. Its holdings report lists US Treasuries and US-dollar cash but no currency forwards, so its returns combine US long-bond returns with USD/TWD moves.
>
> From the close of 17 January 2017, 00679B's first trading day, to 8 October 2026, 0050 returned +767.3% with dividends reinvested, at 20.1% annualized volatility. 00679B returned −17.3% at 13.5%. Their daily returns were mildly negatively correlated, −0.11.
>
> Suppose the investor had sized the mix at the end of 2021 with the five years then available. Over 2017–2021 the correlation was −0.22. The calculus of Section 6 then says that 42.3% in 0050 and 57.7% in 00679B minimized volatility, at a predicted 9.22% a year.
>
> Then came 2022: 0050 lost 21.3% and 00679B lost 22.1%. Over 2022–2026 the correlation drifted to −0.02, and the same mix realized 12.44%. Was the calculus wrong, was the weight wrong, or did the inputs change? By the end of Section 6 you will split the 3.2-point miss into its parts.

> [!note] Notation for this module
> Bold letters are vectors: $\mathbf w=(w_1,w_2)$ portfolio weights and $\mathbf f=(f_1,f_2)$ exposures per unit of capital (Kelly fractions). For two assets, $\sigma_1,\sigma_2$ are annualized volatilities, $\rho$ the correlation, $\sigma_{12}=\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2$ the covariance, and $e_i=\mu_i-r_f$ asset $i$'s expected excess return. Subscripts on a function are partial derivatives ($f_x=\partial f/\partial x$, $f_{xy}=\partial^2 f/\partial y\,\partial x$); $\nabla f$ is the gradient, $H$ the Hessian, $\lambda$ a Lagrange multiplier and $\nu$ a multiplier on an inequality constraint. Sample moments use divisor $T$, as in [[LM3]]; for the multi-year windows ($T\ge1{,}150$) the divisor $T-1$ would change volatilities by less than 0.05% of their value.

## Functions of Several Variables and Partial Derivatives {lo=1}

A portfolio's volatility depends on two weights, two volatilities and a correlation at once. A TWD investor's return on a US bond depends on the bond and on the currency. Functions of several variables are the norm in finance, and this section extends the derivative of [[LM2]] to them.

### Functions, surfaces and level curves

> [!definition] Function of two variables
> A **function of two variables** assigns to each point $(x,y)$ of a domain $D\subseteq\mathbb R^2$ one number $f(x,y)$. Its **graph** is the surface $z=f(x,y)$. A **level curve** (contour) is the set of points where $f$ takes a fixed value, $\{(x,y): f(x,y)=c\}$.

Level curves are the map view of the surface, like contour lines on a hiking map. Take the variance of a two-asset portfolio as a function of the two weights,

$$V(w_1,w_2)=w_1^2\sigma_1^2+2w_1w_2\sigma_{12}+w_2^2\sigma_2^2 .$$

Its level curves are ellipses centred on the all-cash point $(0,0)$, one for each variance level. The budget rule $w_1+w_2=1$ is a straight line across them. Section 5 shows that the minimum-variance portfolio sits where that line just touches an ellipse, and [[ex:levels]] draws the picture.

### Partial derivatives

> [!definition] Partial derivative
> The **partial derivative** of $f$ with respect to $x$ at $(a,b)$ is the ordinary derivative of the one-variable function $x\mapsto f(x,b)$:
> $$f_x(a,b)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a,b):=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h,b)-f(a,b)}{h}.$$
> $f_y$ is defined in the same way with $x$ held at $a$.

To compute a partial derivative, treat every other variable as a constant and apply the rules of [[LM2]]. For the portfolio variance,

$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial w_1}=2\big(w_1\sigma_1^2+w_2\sigma_{12}\big),\qquad \frac{\partial V}{\partial w_2}=2\big(w_1\sigma_{12}+w_2\sigma_2^2\big).$$

The bracket $w_1\sigma_1^2+w_2\sigma_{12}$ is the covariance of asset 1 with the portfolio, $\operatorname{Cov}(R_1,w_1R_1+w_2R_2)$. So the marginal variance of a position is twice its covariance with the portfolio it joins — a fact [[LM36]] builds into risk budgeting.

**Higher-order partial derivatives.** Differentiating again gives $f_{xx}$, $f_{yy}$ and the mixed partials $f_{xy}=(f_x)_y$ and $f_{yx}=(f_y)_x$. For every function in this book the order does not matter.

> [!theorem] Symmetry of mixed partial derivatives (Schwarz–Clairaut)
> If $f_{xy}$ and $f_{yx}$ exist and are continuous near $(a,b)$, then $f_{xy}(a,b)=f_{yx}(a,b)$. Stated without proof; see Stewart, chapter on partial derivatives.

### The total differential and the linear approximation

Move both inputs at once, by $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$. Going first along $x$ and then along $y$, and using the one-variable linear approximation of [[LM2]] on each leg, gives

$$f(a+\Delta x,\,b+\Delta y)\approx f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)\,\Delta x+f_y(a,b)\,\Delta y, \tag{7.1}$$

with an error of second order in $(\Delta x,\Delta y)$ when $f$ has continuous partial derivatives. The linear part, $df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy$, is the **total differential**. Dividing by a time step gives the multivariable **chain rule**: if $x=x(t)$ and $y=y(t)$, then $\frac{d}{dt}f(x(t),y(t))=f_x\,x'(t)+f_y\,y'(t)$.

> [!example] A US bond in Taiwan dollars: 00679B in 2022 {#fx}
> A TWD investor's return on a US-dollar asset is $f(x,y)=(1+x)(1+y)-1$, where $x$ is the asset's return in USD and $y$ the change in the USD/TWD rate. In 2022, 00679B returned −22.09% in TWD, while USD/TWD rose from 27.690 to 30.708, +10.90%. Back out the USD return implied for the fund, compute the partial derivatives there, and split the TWD return into bond, currency and cross terms.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The implied USD return solves $1+x=(1-0.2209)/1.1090$: $x=-29.75\%$.
> >
> > The partial derivatives are $f_x=1+y=1.109$ and $f_y=1+x=0.7025$, and $f_{xy}=f_{yx}=1$. An extra 1% of bond return in USD adds 1.109% in TWD, because it is converted at a stronger dollar. An extra 1% rise in the dollar adds only 0.70%, because it applies to a bond position already down 30%.
> >
> > Expanding the product gives the exact split $f=x+y+xy=-29.75\%+10.90\%-3.24\%=-22.09\%$. The linear approximation (7.1), $x+y=-18.85\%$, overstates the return by the cross term of 3.24 points: second order, but not small when the moves are 30% and 11%. On the log scale the split is exact and additive, $\ln(1+f)=\ln(1+x)+\ln(1+y)$, which is the aggregation rule of [[LM3]] in a new setting.

The currency term also drives daily returns. 00679B's worst day in the sample, −7.15% on 5 May 2025, came as USD/TWD fell from 31.064 to 30.145. That was one session after the New Taiwan dollar's 3.07% gain of 2 May, its largest one-day rise since records began in 2002.

> With the full-sample estimates for 0050 and 00679B — $\sigma_1^2=0.040425$, $\sigma_2^2=0.018214$, $\sigma_{12}=-0.002866$ (so $\sigma_1=20.11\%$, $\sigma_2=13.50\%$, $\rho=-0.106$) — evaluate the variance, volatility and partial derivatives of $V$ at $\mathbf w=(0.6,0.4)$. >
> > [!solution]
> > $V=0.36(0.040425)+0.16(0.018214)+0.48(-0.002866)=0.016092$, so $\sigma_p=12.69\%$. The partial derivatives are $V_{w_1}=2(0.6\cdot0.040425-0.4\cdot0.002866)=0.0462$ and $V_{w_2}=2(-0.6\cdot0.002866+0.4\cdot0.018214)=0.0111$.
> >
> > Because $\sigma_p=\sqrt V$, the chain rule gives $\partial\sigma_p/\partial w_i=V_{w_i}/(2\sigma_p)$: 0.182 for 0050 and 0.044 for 00679B.

(b) $\Delta\mathbf w=(0.01,-0.01)$: $\Delta\sigma_p\approx(0.182-0.044)\times0.01=0.14$ points (exact: 0.14). The two answers differ because they are different experiments: (a) raises total exposure, while (b) stays on the budget line $w_1+w_2=1$.

> [!pitfall] "Holding everything else fixed" is not always possible
> A partial derivative moves one input and freezes the rest. In a fully invested portfolio the weights cannot move one at a time, because buying 0050 means selling something. So $\partial V/\partial w_1$ describes a trade financed with cash or margin, case (a) above. For a budget-neutral switch, use the derivative along the feasible direction $(1,-1)$, as in case (b), or substitute the constraint (Sections 5 and 6). The same caution applies to "$\partial\sigma_p/\partial\rho$ holding volatilities fixed": in a crisis, volatilities and correlations tend to move together.

> 偏導數就是「其他條件不變」時的敏感度：只動一個變數，其餘全部凍結。台幣投資美債的報酬是 $(1+x)(1+y)-1$，它對匯率的偏導數是 $1+x$：2022 年債券本身已跌三成，美元再升 1%，台幣報酬只多 0.70%。全微分把各方向的敏感度加總，是一階近似；變動很大時（債跌三成、美元升一成），交叉項 $xy$ 就有 3.24 個百分點，不能忽略。在投資組合裡「其他條件不變」常常做不到：權重加總必須是 1，加碼 0050 就得減碼另一檔，要看的是沿著 $(1,-1)$ 方向的變化。

## Gradient, Hessian and the Multivariate Taylor Expansion {lo=2}

The total differential is a first-order model. This section packages first derivatives into the gradient and second derivatives into the Hessian, and builds the second-order model the rest of the module relies on.

### Directional derivatives and the gradient

Pick a direction, given by a unit vector $\mathbf u=(u_1,u_2)$, and walk from $\mathbf a$ along it. The slope of $f$ along the walk is the **directional derivative**

$$D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf a):=\lim_{t\to0}\frac{f(\mathbf a+t\mathbf u)-f(\mathbf a)}{t}=f_x(\mathbf a)\,u_1+f_y(\mathbf a)\,u_2 ,$$

where the second equality is the chain rule applied to $\varphi(t)=f(\mathbf a+t\mathbf u)$.

> [!definition] Gradient
> The **gradient** of $f$ at $\mathbf a$ is the vector of its partial derivatives, $\nabla f(\mathbf a):=\big(f_x(\mathbf a),\,f_y(\mathbf a)\big)$. Every directional derivative is a dot product with it: $D_{\mathbf u}f=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u$.

Two geometric facts follow. First, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u\le\lVert\nabla f\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf u\rVert=\lVert\nabla f\rVert$, with equality only when $\mathbf u$ points along $\nabla f$. So **the gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its length is the steepest slope**.

Second, if $\mathbf r(t)$ runs along a level curve, then $f(\mathbf r(t))=c$, and differentiating gives $\nabla f\cdot\mathbf r'(t)=0$. So **the gradient is perpendicular to the level curves**. Section 5 builds Lagrange's method on this second fact.

For a step $\mathbf h$ that is not a unit vector, $\nabla f\cdot\mathbf h$ is the first-order change in $f$. Case (b) of [[eg:marginal]] used exactly this, with $\mathbf h=(0.01,-0.01)$.

### The Hessian

> [!definition] Hessian
> The **Hessian** of $f$ at $\mathbf a$ is the matrix of its second partial derivatives,
> $$H(\mathbf a):=\begin{pmatrix} f_{xx} & f_{xy}\\ f_{yx} & f_{yy}\end{pmatrix}\!(\mathbf a),$$
> symmetric when the Schwarz–Clairaut theorem applies.

The Hessian measures curvature in every direction at once. Differentiating $\varphi(t)=f(\mathbf a+t\mathbf u)$ twice with the chain rule gives

$$\varphi''(0)=f_{xx}u_1^2+2f_{xy}u_1u_2+f_{yy}u_2^2=\mathbf u^{\top}H\,\mathbf u ,$$

the curvature of $f$ along $\mathbf u$. For the portfolio variance the Hessian is constant, $H=2\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_1^2&\sigma_{12}\\ \sigma_{12}&\sigma_2^2\end{pmatrix}=2\Sigma$: twice the covariance matrix, the object [[LM10]] studies.

### The second-order Taylor expansion

The one-variable Taylor polynomial of [[LM3]], applied to $\varphi(t)=f(\mathbf a+t\mathbf h)$ at $t=1$, gives $\varphi(1)\approx\varphi(0)+\varphi'(0)+\tfrac12\varphi''(0)$. Substituting the two derivatives just computed:

$$f(\mathbf a+\mathbf h)\approx f(\mathbf a)+f_x h_1+f_y h_2+\tfrac12\big(f_{xx}h_1^2+2f_{xy}h_1h_2+f_{yy}h_2^2\big)=f(\mathbf a)+\nabla f\cdot\mathbf h+\tfrac12\,\mathbf h^{\top}H\,\mathbf h, \tag{7.2}$$

with all derivatives evaluated at $\mathbf a$. The Lagrange remainder of [[LM3]] (3.5), applied to $\varphi$, puts the error at $\varphi'''(\xi)/6$ for some $\xi\in(0,1)$: third order in $\mathbf h$. With three variables the pattern is the same, with three first derivatives and a $3\times3$ Hessian. A polynomial of degree two is reproduced exactly by (7.2), and one of degree three once the third-order terms are added.

### Why did the 42/58 mix get riskier? A Taylor attribution

Hold the weights of the case fixed at $w_1=42.3\%$ in 0050 and $w_2=57.7\%$ in 00679B. Their variance is then a function of three inputs,

$$V(\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\rho)=w_1^2\sigma_1^2+w_2^2\sigma_2^2+2w_1w_2\,\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2 .$$

Between the estimation and test windows the inputs moved from $(16.37\%,\,13.55\%,\,-0.221)$ to $(23.40\%,\,13.43\%,\,-0.022)$. The partial derivatives at the starting point are $V_{\sigma_1}=2w_1^2\sigma_1+2w_1w_2\rho\sigma_2=0.0440$, $V_{\sigma_2}=2w_2^2\sigma_2+2w_1w_2\rho\sigma_1=0.0725$ and $V_\rho=2w_1w_2\sigma_1\sigma_2=0.0108$. Multiplying each by its input's change gives the first-order terms of [[ex:attrib]].

The first-order approximation explains 51.70 of the 69.67 %² rise in variance, or 74%. The Hessian terms supply the rest, slightly overshooting. The two largest are $\tfrac12V_{\sigma_1\sigma_1}(\Delta\sigma_1)^2=8.86$, with $V_{\sigma_1\sigma_1}=2w_1^2$, and the cross term $V_{\sigma_1\rho}\,\Delta\sigma_1\Delta\rho=9.28$, with $V_{\sigma_1\rho}=2w_1w_2\sigma_2$. A higher correlation hurts more when 0050 is more volatile, and only a second-order model can see that.

Because $V$ is a polynomial of degree three in its inputs, the single third-order term $2w_1w_2\,\Delta\sigma_1\Delta\sigma_2\Delta\rho$, about −0.08, makes the expansion exact.

### A two-variable Taylor model of portfolio growth

The most important use of (7.2) in this book is the growth rate of a levered portfolio. Hold a fraction $f_1$ of capital in 0050 and $f_2$ in 00679B, and finance the rest at an illustrative risk-free rate $r_f=1.5\%$ a year ($r=r_f/A$ a day). The daily return is $R_p=r+f_1X_1+f_2X_2$, with excess returns $X_i=R_i-r$. The quantity a Kelly investor maximizes ([[LM4]]) is the average log growth, annualized:

$$g(f_1,f_2):=A\cdot\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ln\!\big(1+r+f_1X_{1t}+f_2X_{2t}\big).$$

Differentiate under the sum: $g_{f_i}=A\cdot\frac1T\sum_t X_{it}/(1+R_{pt})$ and $g_{f_if_j}=-A\cdot\frac1T\sum_t X_{it}X_{jt}/(1+R_{pt})^2$. At $\mathbf f=\mathbf 0$ the daily return is $r$, so the gradient is the vector of mean excess returns and the Hessian is minus the matrix of mean products.

On the data, $\nabla g(\mathbf 0)=(0.2275,\,-0.0255)$, which is $(e_1,e_2)$ up to the factor $1/(1+r)$. The Hessian $H(\mathbf 0)$ has entries $(-0.0406,\;0.0029,\;-0.0182)$, against $-\sigma_1^2=-0.0404$, $-\sigma_{12}=0.0029$ and $-\sigma_2^2=-0.0182$. The small gap in the first entry is the squared daily mean, a term of order $\Delta t$ relative to the variance. Dropping it and the factors $1+r$, (7.2) becomes

$$g(f_1,f_2)\approx r_f+f_1e_1+f_2e_2-\tfrac12\big(f_1^2\sigma_1^2+2f_1f_2\sigma_{12}+f_2^2\sigma_2^2\big). \tag{7.3}$$

The same model follows from expanding $\ln(1+R_p)\approx R_p-\tfrac12R_p^2$ day by day and averaging, exactly as in [[LM3]] (3.8). What is neglected is of third order in the daily portfolio return, plus the squared daily mean. In matrix form (7.3) reads $g\approx r_f+\mathbf f^{\top}\mathbf e-\tfrac12\mathbf f^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf f$, the starting point of [[LM43]].

## Unconstrained Optimization in Two Variables {lo=3}

[[LM4]] found optima of one-variable functions with two conditions: a zero derivative and the sign of the second derivative. Both carry over to two variables. The second condition needs more care, because a surface can curve up in one direction and down in another.

### First-order conditions

If $f$ is differentiable and has a local maximum or minimum at an interior point $\mathbf a$, then every one-variable slice through $\mathbf a$ has zero slope there. In particular $f_x(\mathbf a)=f_y(\mathbf a)=0$, that is, $\nabla f(\mathbf a)=\mathbf 0$. Points where the gradient vanishes are **critical** (or stationary) **points**. As in one variable, the condition is necessary, not sufficient.

### Second-order conditions: the 2×2 test

At a critical point the linear term of (7.2) vanishes, so for small steps

$$f(\mathbf a+\mathbf h)-f(\mathbf a)\approx\tfrac12Q(\mathbf h),\qquad Q(\mathbf h)=f_{xx}h_1^2+2f_{xy}h_1h_2+f_{yy}h_2^2 .$$

The sign of the quadratic form $Q$ decides the shape. If $f_{xx}\neq0$, completing the square gives

$$Q(\mathbf h)=f_{xx}\Big(h_1+\frac{f_{xy}}{f_{xx}}h_2\Big)^2+\frac{D}{f_{xx}}\,h_2^2,\qquad D:=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2=\det H. \tag{7.4}$$

If $D>0$, both terms have the sign of $f_{xx}$, and $Q$ is non-zero for every $\mathbf h\neq\mathbf 0$. If $D<0$, the two terms have opposite signs, and $Q$ takes both signs. [[ex:dtest]] collects the cases. In matrix language, $H$ is **positive definite** exactly when $f_{xx}>0$ and $\det H>0$, the $2\times2$ case of a criterion [[LM10]] extends to $N$ assets.

> [!exhibit] The second-derivative test at a critical point of $f(x,y)$ {#dtest}
> | Condition | Quadratic form $Q$ | Shape | Example in this module |
> |:--|:--|:--|:--|
> | $D>0$ and $f_{xx}>0$ | positive definite | local minimum (bowl) | portfolio variance $V(w_1,w_2)$ with $\lvert\rho\rvert<1$ |
> | $D>0$ and $f_{xx}<0$ | negative definite | local maximum (dome) | the growth model (7.3) |
> | $D<0$ | indefinite | saddle point | $x^3-3xy+y^3$ at the origin ([[eg:classify]]) |
> | $D=0$ | semidefinite or degenerate | test inconclusive | $V$ with $\rho=\pm1$: a valley floor |
>
> Note: $D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2$ evaluated at the critical point. The test classifies local behaviour only.

> [!deepdive]- Why the remainder cannot overturn a definite quadratic form
> Suppose $H$ is positive definite at the critical point $\mathbf a$. The function $\mathbf u\mapsto\mathbf u^{\top}H\mathbf u$ is continuous on the unit circle, so it has a minimum $m>0$ there, and $Q(\mathbf h)\ge m\lVert\mathbf h\rVert^2$ for every $\mathbf h$. By (7.2) with its remainder, $f(\mathbf a+\mathbf h)-f(\mathbf a)=\tfrac12Q(\mathbf h)+E(\mathbf h)$ with $\lvert E(\mathbf h)\rvert\le C\lVert\mathbf h\rVert^3$ near $\mathbf a$. Hence $f(\mathbf a+\mathbf h)-f(\mathbf a)\ge\lVert\mathbf h\rVert^2\big(\tfrac m2-C\lVert\mathbf h\rVert\big)>0$ whenever $0<\lVert\mathbf h\rVert<m/(2C)$: a strict local minimum.
>
> If $D<0$, pick directions $\mathbf u$ with $Q(\mathbf u)>0$ and $\mathbf v$ with $Q(\mathbf v)<0$. The same bound shows that, close to $\mathbf a$, $f$ rises along $\mathbf u$ and falls along $\mathbf v$: a saddle. When $D=0$, the cubic remainder decides, and the test is silent. $\blacksquare$

### Global optima and convexity

A local test says nothing about the rest of the surface. One condition does: if the Hessian is positive semidefinite everywhere, $f$ is **convex**, and any critical point is a global minimum. Quadratic functions have a constant Hessian, so for them the local test is global.

Portfolio variance has $H=2\Sigma$ with $D=4\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2(1-\rho^2)\ge0$: it is convex, and strictly convex when $\lvert\rho\rvert<1$. The growth model (7.3) has $H=-\Sigma$ and is concave. That is why both of the portfolio problems in Section 6 have unique, global answers.

> [!example] Fitting a line by calculus: 00679B on 0050 {#ols}
> Choose $\alpha$ and $\beta$ to minimize $S(\alpha,\beta)=\sum_t(y_t-\alpha-\beta x_t)^2$, with $y_t$ the daily return of 00679B and $x_t$ that of 0050 over the full sample ($T=2{,}363$). Solve the first-order conditions and verify the minimum with the $2\times2$ test.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The first-order conditions are $S_\alpha=-2\sum_t(y_t-\alpha-\beta x_t)=0$ and $S_\beta=-2\sum_t x_t(y_t-\alpha-\beta x_t)=0$. The first gives $\alpha=\bar y-\beta\bar x$. > >
> > On the data, $\hat\beta=-0.0709$ and $\hat\alpha=0.0028\%$ a day (0.67% a year). The Hessian has $S_{\alpha\alpha}=2T=4{,}726$, $S_{\alpha\beta}=2\sum_t x_t=4.715$ and $S_{\beta\beta}=2\sum_t x_t^2=0.7907$. So $D=4\big[T\sum_t x_t^2-(\sum_t x_t)^2\big]=3{,}714.8>0$ with $S_{\alpha\alpha}>0$: a minimum, and global because $S$ is quadratic.
> >

> [!example] A saddle and a local minimum {#classify}
> Find and classify the critical points of $f(x,y)=x^3-3xy+y^3$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > $f_x=3x^2-3y=0$ gives $y=x^2$, and $f_y=3y^2-3x=0$ gives $x=y^2$. Hence $x=x^4$, so $x=0$ or $x=1$, and the critical points are $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$. The second derivatives are $f_{xx}=6x$, $f_{yy}=6y$ and $f_{xy}=-3$.
> >
> > At $(0,0)$: $D=0\cdot0-9=-9<0$, a saddle. Indeed $f(t,-t)=3t^2>0$ while $f(t,t)=2t^3-3t^2<0$ for small $t\neq0$.
> >
> > At $(1,1)$: $f_{xx}=6>0$ and $D=36-9=27>0$, a local minimum with $f(1,1)=-1$. It is not global, because $f(x,0)=x^3\to-\infty$ as $x\to-\infty$. Without convexity, a local test cannot certify a global optimum.

> [!pitfall] When $D=0$: perfectly correlated assets
> With $\lvert\rho\rvert=1$ the variance Hessian $2\Sigma$ has $D=0$. The minimum is then a whole line of weights (a valley floor) or a riskless long–short hedge. The growth model (7.3) becomes unbounded if the two Sharpe ratios differ, because a riskless spread with positive return can be levered without limit.
>
> Real assets are never exactly collinear, but correlations near ±1 make $D$ tiny. Optimizers then return huge, unstable long–short weights; [[LM9]] and [[LM11]] treat this ill-conditioning.

> [!kc]
> Classify the critical point $(0,0)$ of $f(x,y)=x^2+3xy+2y^2$. Every coefficient is positive; is the origin a minimum?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > $f_{xx}=2$, $f_{yy}=4$, $f_{xy}=3$, so $D=8-9=-1<0$: a saddle, not a minimum. Along $(1,-1)$, $f(t,-t)=t^2-3t^2+2t^2=0$, and along $(2,-1)$, $f(2t,-t)=4t^2-6t^2+2t^2=0$; between these directions $f<0$, for example $f(3t,-2t)=9t^2-18t^2+8t^2=-t^2$. Positive coefficients do not make a quadratic form positive definite; the cross term must satisfy $f_{xy}^2<f_{xx}f_{yy}$.

## Equality Constraints: Lagrange Multipliers {lo=4}

Most portfolio problems come with constraints: the weights must sum to one, the volatility must hit a target, an order must be completed. When a constraint can be solved for one variable, substitution reduces the problem to [[LM4]]. Lagrange's method works without solving the constraint, and it delivers a bonus: the price of the constraint.

### The tangency condition

Consider optimizing $f(x,y)$ subject to $g(x,y)=c$, and walk along the constraint curve. At a constrained optimum the slope of $f$ along the curve must be zero, so $\nabla f$ is perpendicular to the curve there. The constraint curve is a level curve of $g$, so $\nabla g$ is perpendicular to it too (Section 3).

Two vectors perpendicular to the same curve are parallel: at the optimum, the level curve of $f$ just touches the constraint curve. [[ex:levels]] shows this for the minimum-variance portfolio.

The algebra confirms the picture. Suppose $g_y\neq0$ at the optimum. Near it the constraint defines $y$ as a function of $x$, and differentiating $g(x,y(x))=c$ gives $g_x+g_y\,y'(x)=0$, so $y'=-g_x/g_y$. The one-variable function $F(x)=f(x,y(x))$ has a critical point at the optimum:

$$F'(x)=f_x+f_y\,y'(x)=f_x-f_y\frac{g_x}{g_y}=0 .$$

Writing $\lambda:=f_y/g_y$, this says $f_x=\lambda g_x$, and $f_y=\lambda g_y$ holds by definition of $\lambda$.

> [!theorem] Lagrange multiplier condition
> Let $f$ and $g$ have continuous partial derivatives, and let $(x^*,y^*)$ be a local maximum or minimum of $f$ subject to $g(x,y)=c$, with $\nabla g(x^*,y^*)\neq\mathbf 0$. Then there is a number $\lambda^*$ with
> $$\nabla f(x^*,y^*)=\lambda^*\,\nabla g(x^*,y^*),\qquad g(x^*,y^*)=c. \tag{7.5}$$
> Equivalently, $(x^*,y^*,\lambda^*)$ is a critical point of the **Lagrangian** $\mathcal L(x,y,\lambda):=f(x,y)-\lambda\,\big(g(x,y)-c\big)$.

The three conditions $\mathcal L_x=\mathcal L_y=\mathcal L_\lambda=0$ are three equations in three unknowns. They give candidates; whether a candidate is a maximum, a minimum or neither needs a second-order or a global argument. For the portfolio problems of this module the objective is convex or concave and the constraints are linear, so a candidate is the global answer.

### The multiplier is a shadow price

The multiplier is not a by-product. It measures what the constraint costs.

> [!result] Shadow price (envelope theorem)
> Let $V(c):=f\big(x^*(c),y^*(c)\big)$ be the optimal value when the constraint level is $c$, and suppose the solution moves smoothly with $c$. Then
> $$V'(c)=\lambda^*(c). \tag{7.6}$$

*Proof.* By the chain rule and (7.5), $V'(c)=f_x\,x^{*\prime}+f_y\,y^{*\prime}=\lambda^*\big(g_x\,x^{*\prime}+g_y\,y^{*\prime}\big)$. Differentiating the identity $g(x^*(c),y^*(c))=c$ shows that the bracket equals 1. $\blacksquare$

So $\Delta V\approx\lambda^*\,\Delta c$, with an error of second order in $\Delta c$. The units of $\lambda^*$ are units of objective per unit of constraint: NT\$ per contract, return per unit of risk, variance per unit of capital. Reading those units is half of interpreting an optimization.

> [!example] Splitting a TX order between two sessions {#split}
> A trader must buy $Q=100$ TX contracts, $x$ in the day session and $y$ after hours. Model the temporary-impact cost as $C=k_dx^2+k_ny^2$ with illustrative coefficients $k_d=2$ and $k_n=5$ (NT\$ per contract squared), the night book being thinner. Find the cost-minimizing split and interpret the multiplier.
>
> > [!solution]
> > $\mathcal L=2x^2+5y^2-\lambda(x+y-100)$. The conditions $\mathcal L_x=4x-\lambda=0$, $\mathcal L_y=10y-\lambda=0$ and $x+y=100$ give $4x=10y=\lambda$, so $x^*=71.43$, $y^*=28.57$ and $\lambda^*=285.7$, in NT\$ per contract.
> >
> > The multiplier is the **marginal cost** of the last contract, and the optimum equalizes it across sessions. If one session were cheaper at the margin, moving a contract there would cut the total.
> >
> > Shadow price: the optimal cost is $C^*(Q)=\frac{k_dk_n}{k_d+k_n}Q^2=\tfrac{10}{7}Q^2$, so $C^*(100)=14{,}286$ (NT\$) and $C^{*\prime}(100)=\tfrac{20}{7}\cdot100=285.7=\lambda^*$, as (7.6) requires. The 101st contract adds NT\$287.1 exactly; the difference from $\lambda^*$ is the second-order term $\tfrac{10}{7}\approx1.4$.
> >
> > In whole contracts a 71/29 split costs NT\$14,287, essentially optimal. An equal 50/50 split costs NT\$17,500, 22.5% more. Note that the marginal cost is twice the average cost of NT\$142.9, a property of quadratic impact.

> [!example] A risk budget across two strategies {#budget}
> A desk runs a TX trend strategy (expected excess return $e_1=8\%$, volatility $\sigma_1=16\%$) and a futures–spot basis strategy ($e_2=3\%$, $\sigma_2=4\%$), with correlation $\rho=0.2$ — illustrative figures. >

> > $$e_1=2\lambda\big(\sigma_1^2f_1+\sigma_{12}f_2\big),\qquad e_2=2\lambda\big(\sigma_{12}f_1+\sigma_2^2f_2\big).$$
> > This is a $2\times2$ linear system in $(f_1,f_2)$. Cramer's rule gives $f_1\propto\sigma_2^2e_1-\sigma_{12}e_2$ and $f_2\propto\sigma_1^2e_2-\sigma_{12}e_1$, a ratio of 1 to 7.43. Scaling to 10% volatility gives $\mathbf f=(0.274,\,2.038)$: the low-volatility basis trade is levered about two times.

> >
> > The multiplier on the variance constraint is $\lambda^*=SR^*/(2\sigma_T)=4.15$. By (7.6), the optimal excess return $E^*$ rises by 4.15 per unit of variance budget. In volatility units $dE^*/d\sigma_T=SR^*$, so raising the budget from 10% to 11% adds 0.83 percentage points. **The shadow price of risk is the best attainable Sharpe ratio.**

> [!deepdive]- Second-order conditions: the bordered Hessian
> Along the constraint, $F(x)=f(x,y(x))$. Differentiating twice, and eliminating $y''$ with the twice-differentiated constraint $g_{xx}+2g_{xy}y'+g_{yy}y'^2+g_y\,y''=0$, gives
> $$F''=\mathcal L_{xx}+2\mathcal L_{xy}\,y'+\mathcal L_{yy}\,y'^2,\qquad y'=-g_x/g_y,$$
> where $\mathcal L_{xx}=f_{xx}-\lambda g_{xx}$ and so on. Multiply by $g_y^2$ and compare with the determinant of the **bordered Hessian**
> $$\bar H=\begin{pmatrix}0&g_x&g_y\\ g_x&\mathcal L_{xx}&\mathcal L_{xy}\\ g_y&\mathcal L_{xy}&\mathcal L_{yy}\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det\bar H=-\big(\mathcal L_{xx}g_y^2-2\mathcal L_{xy}g_xg_y+\mathcal L_{yy}g_x^2\big)=-g_y^2\,F''.$$
> So $\det\bar H>0$ means $F''<0$, a constrained local maximum, and $\det\bar H<0$ means a constrained local minimum. In [[eg:split]], $\det\bar H=-(4+10)=-14<0$: a minimum, as expected. $\blacksquare$

> [!pitfall] Signs and units of the multiplier
> Writing the Lagrangian as $f+\lambda(g-c)$ instead of $f-\lambda(g-c)$ flips the sign of $\lambda$, and solvers use their own conventions. The safe check is (7.6) itself: re-solve with the constraint relaxed slightly and compare the change in the optimal value with $\lambda^*\Delta c$. Also check the units. A multiplier of 4.15 on a variance budget and 0.83 on a volatility budget describe the same shadow price.

## Two-Asset Portfolios by Calculus {lo=5,6}

This section applies Sections 2–5 to two assets and answers the case. It derives the minimum-variance weight twice, studies how correlation shapes it and takes the estimate out of sample. It then adds expected returns to obtain the tangency portfolio and the Kelly fractions.

### Minimum variance, by substitution and by Lagrange

**Substitution.** With $w$ in asset 1 and $1-w$ in asset 2, the variance is a one-variable function,

$$V(w)=w^2\sigma_1^2+(1-w)^2\sigma_2^2+2w(1-w)\sigma_{12}.$$

Its derivative is $V'(w)=2w\sigma_1^2-2(1-w)\sigma_2^2+2(1-2w)\sigma_{12}$. Setting it to zero:

$$w^*=\frac{\sigma_2^2-\sigma_{12}}{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2-2\sigma_{12}}. \tag{7.7}$$

The second derivative is $V''(w)=2(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2-2\sigma_{12})=2\operatorname{Var}(R_1-R_2)>0$, so $w^*$ is the global minimum unless the two returns differ by a constant. Substituting $w^*$ back, and writing $D_V:=\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2-2\sigma_{12}$:

$$\sigma_{\min}^2=V(w^*)=\frac{\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2-\sigma_{12}^2}{D_V}=\frac{\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2(1-\rho^2)}{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2-2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2}. \tag{7.8}$$

Because $V$ is a quadratic in $w$ with curvature $D_V$, it can be written exactly around its minimum:

$$V(w)=\sigma_{\min}^2+D_V\,(w-w^*)^2. \tag{7.9}$$

This identity will measure the cost of a wrong weight.

**Lagrange.** Minimize $V(w_1,w_2)$ subject to $w_1+w_2=1$. With $\mathcal L=V-\lambda(w_1+w_2-1)$, the conditions are

$$2\big(w_1\sigma_1^2+w_2\sigma_{12}\big)=\lambda,\qquad 2\big(w_1\sigma_{12}+w_2\sigma_2^2\big)=\lambda .$$

Subtracting gives $w_1(\sigma_1^2-\sigma_{12})=w_2(\sigma_2^2-\sigma_{12})$, which with $w_1+w_2=1$ is (7.7) again. The two conditions say more: at the minimum, **each asset has the same covariance with the portfolio**.

Multiply the first condition by $w_1$, the second by $w_2$ and add: $2V=\lambda(w_1+w_2)=\lambda$, so $\lambda^*=2\sigma_{\min}^2$. That is the shadow price (7.6) of the budget: investing $b$ units in the same proportions gives variance $b^2\sigma_{\min}^2$, whose derivative at $b=1$ is $2\sigma_{\min}^2$.

### Correlation and diversification

Formula (7.8) shows how correlation sets the benefit of diversification.

- **$\rho=+1$.** Then $\sigma_{\min}^2=0$ only through a short position: $w^*=-\sigma_2/(\sigma_1-\sigma_2)$ when $\sigma_1>\sigma_2$. Long-only, volatility is linear in $w$, and there is no diversification.
- **$\rho=0$.** Then $w^*=\sigma_2^2/(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)$: each asset is weighted by its inverse variance.
- **$\rho=-1$.** Then $w^*=\sigma_2/(\sigma_1+\sigma_2)$ and $\sigma_{\min}=0$: a perfect hedge without shorting.

The minimum-variance portfolio shorts the riskier asset when the numerator of (7.7) turns negative, $\sigma_2^2<\sigma_{12}$, that is, when $\rho>\sigma_2/\sigma_1$ (asset 1 being the riskier one). For 0050 and 00679B the ratio is $13.50/20.11=0.67$, far above any correlation in the sample, so the minimum is interior. Section 7 returns to the case where it is not.

How fast does $\sigma_{\min}^2$ rise with $\rho$? The envelope logic behind (7.6) answers without differentiating (7.8). Because $V'(w^*)=0$, the change in the optimal weight has no first-order effect, and

$$\frac{d\sigma_{\min}^2}{d\rho}=\frac{\partial V}{\partial\rho}\Big|_{w^*}=2w_1^*w_2^*\sigma_1\sigma_2 .$$

With the 2017–2021 estimates this is 0.0108. A correlation 0.1 higher would therefore raise $\sigma_{\min}^2$ by about 0.00108, which matches the exact change to that precision, lifting $\sigma_{\min}$ from 9.22% to 9.79%.

### The tangency portfolio

Now add expected returns and a risk-free rate. The portfolio with the highest Sharpe ratio, $(\mu_p-r_f)/\sigma_p$, is the **tangency portfolio**. On a chart of expected return against volatility, it is where a line from the risk-free rate touches the set of risky portfolios ([[LM33]]).

[[eg:budget]] already solved this problem. Maximizing expected excess return for a fixed volatility gave exposures proportional to $\big(\sigma_2^2e_1-\sigma_{12}e_2,\ \sigma_1^2e_2-\sigma_{12}e_1\big)$, whatever the budget. The Sharpe ratio does not change when exposures are scaled by a positive constant, so the same direction maximizes the Sharpe ratio. Normalizing it to sum to one gives the tangency weights

$$w_1^T=\frac{\sigma_2^2e_1-\sigma_{12}e_2}{\sigma_2^2e_1+\sigma_1^2e_2-\sigma_{12}(e_1+e_2)},\qquad w_2^T=1-w_1^T. \tag{7.10}$$

The highest Sharpe ratio follows by evaluating the ratio on that direction. With $\det\Sigma=\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2-\sigma_{12}^2$, it is $SR^{*2}=(\sigma_2^2e_1^2-2\sigma_{12}e_1e_2+\sigma_1^2e_2^2)/\det\Sigma$. Dividing numerator and denominator by $\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2$ and writing $SR_i=e_i/\sigma_i$:

$$SR^{*2}=\frac{SR_1^2+SR_2^2-2\rho\,SR_1SR_2}{1-\rho^2}. \tag{7.11}$$

Subtracting $SR_1^2$ from (7.11) leaves $(SR_2-\rho SR_1)^2/(1-\rho^2)\ge0$. So adding asset 2 raises the best Sharpe ratio unless $SR_2=\rho\,SR_1$, which is exactly when its weight in (7.10) is zero. In [[eg:budget]], $SR^{*2}=(0.25+0.5625-0.15)/0.96=0.690$, so $SR^*=0.831$.

One warning comes from the denominator of (7.10): when it is near zero, the tangency weights explode. The test-window estimates in [[ex:kelly]] produce exactly that.

### The two-asset Kelly fractions

The Kelly investor maximizes the growth rate. With the quadratic model (7.3), the first-order conditions are

$$g_{f_1}=e_1-\big(\sigma_1^2f_1+\sigma_{12}f_2\big)=0,\qquad g_{f_2}=e_2-\big(\sigma_{12}f_1+\sigma_2^2f_2\big)=0,$$

a $2\times2$ linear system. By Cramer's rule,

$$f_1^*=\frac{\sigma_2^2e_1-\sigma_{12}e_2}{\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2-\sigma_{12}^2},\qquad f_2^*=\frac{\sigma_1^2e_2-\sigma_{12}e_1}{\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2-\sigma_{12}^2}. \tag{7.12}$$

The Hessian of (7.3) is $-\Sigma$, with $g_{f_1f_1}=-\sigma_1^2<0$ and $D=\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2(1-\rho^2)>0$ whenever $\lvert\rho\rvert<1$: by [[ex:dtest]], a maximum, and global because (7.3) is concave. Three readings of (7.12) connect it to the rest of the book.

1. **Matrix form.** The inverse of a $2\times2$ matrix is $\Sigma^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det\Sigma}\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_2^2&-\sigma_{12}\\ -\sigma_{12}&\sigma_1^2\end{pmatrix}$ ([[LM9]]). So (7.12) is $\mathbf f^*=\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu-r_f\mathbf 1)$, the multi-asset Kelly rule of [[LM43]].
2. **Kelly is tangency, levered.** The numerators of (7.12) and (7.10) are the same. Hence $\mathbf f^*=L^*\,\mathbf w^T$, with total exposure $L^*=f_1^*+f_2^*$. The Kelly investor holds the tangency portfolio and chooses only how much of it to hold.
3. **Growth at the optimum.** At $\mathbf f^*$ the conditions give $\mathbf f^{*\top}\Sigma\mathbf f^*=\mathbf f^{*\top}\mathbf e=SR^{*2}$, so (7.3) becomes $g^*=r_f+\tfrac12SR^{*2}$. With one asset, $f^*=e/\sigma^2$, the result of [[LM4]].

## Inequality Constraints: The KKT Conditions {lo=7}

Real mandates are full of inequalities: no short sales, at least 60% in equities, gross exposure at most twice capital. [[LM4]] handled one inequality in one variable: a maximum on an interval is either interior, with $f'=0$, or at an endpoint where $f'$ points outward. The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions extend that logic to several variables and several constraints.

### Slack or binding

At the optimum each inequality constraint is either **slack**, and could be deleted without changing the answer, or **binding**, and behaves like an equality with a multiplier. The multiplier's sign must say that the constraint pushes in the right direction. For a maximization with constraints written as $g_i(\mathbf x)\le b_i$, relaxing a constraint can only help, so its shadow price cannot be negative.

> [!theorem] Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions
> Maximize $f(\mathbf x)$ subject to $g_i(\mathbf x)\le b_i$ for $i=1,\dots,m$. If $\mathbf x^*$ is a local maximum and the gradients of the binding constraints are linearly independent, there are multipliers $\nu_1,\dots,\nu_m$ with
> $$\nabla f(\mathbf x^*)=\sum_{i=1}^m\nu_i\nabla g_i(\mathbf x^*),\qquad g_i(\mathbf x^*)\le b_i,\qquad \nu_i\ge0,\qquad \nu_i\big(b_i-g_i(\mathbf x^*)\big)=0. \tag{7.13}$$
> If $f$ is concave and every $g_i$ is convex, these conditions are also sufficient for a global maximum. Stated without proof; see Boyd and Vandenberghe (2004, §5.5). For a minimization, apply (7.13) to $-f$. An equality constraint enters as in Section 5, with a multiplier of either sign.

The four parts are **stationarity**, **feasibility**, **dual feasibility** and **complementary slackness**. Stationarity says that the uphill direction $\nabla f$ is a non-negative combination of the binding constraints' outward normals. Every direction that would raise $f$ therefore leaves the feasible set. Complementary slackness says that, for each constraint, either the constraint binds or its multiplier is zero. As in (7.6), $\nu_i$ is the rate at which the optimal value rises when $b_i$ is relaxed.

The practical method is **enumeration**. Guess which constraints bind, solve stationarity together with those constraints as equalities, and keep the candidate only if it is feasible and every multiplier is non-negative. With two variables and two or three constraints, the list of candidates is short.

> [!example] A minimum-variance mix under an equity floor {#floor}
> An investment policy, illustrative here, requires at least 60% in 0050. With the full-sample estimates of [[eg:marginal]], find the minimum-variance weights, the multiplier on the floor and the cost of the policy.
>
> > [!solution]
> > Minimize $V(w_1,w_2)$ subject to $w_1+w_2=1$ and $w_1\ge0.6$. Without the floor, (7.7) gives $w_1^*=32.7\%$, which violates it. So the floor binds: $\mathbf w=(0.6,0.4)$.
> >
> > Stationarity for the maximization of $-V$, with multiplier $\lambda$ on the budget and $\nu$ on the floor $-w_1\le-0.6$, reads $-V_{w_1}=\lambda-\nu$ and $-V_{w_2}=\lambda$. Hence $\nu=V_{w_1}-V_{w_2}=0.0462-0.0111=0.0351\ge0$: all conditions hold. The multiplier is the derivative of $V$ along the switch direction $(1,-1)$, case (b) of [[eg:marginal]].
> >
> > Shadow price: lowering the floor to 59% would cut the variance by about $0.01\nu=0.000351$ (exactly 0.000344), taking volatility from 12.69% to 12.55%. Relative to the unconstrained minimum of 10.64%, the policy costs 2.05 points of volatility. Whether the equity exposure is worth that is a question about expected returns, which the policy answers implicitly.

> [!example] Kelly with a leverage cap and no short sales {#cap}
> Maximize the growth model (7.3) with the full-sample estimates of [[eg:kellyeg]] subject to $f_1+f_2\le2$ (an illustrative cap on gross exposure), $f_1\ge0$ and $f_2\ge0$. Identify the binding constraints and interpret the multipliers.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The unconstrained optimum $(5.59,\,-0.52)$ violates both the cap and the no-short rule. [[ex:kkt]] enumerates the candidates. Only one passes every test: the cap and $f_2\ge0$ bind, at $\mathbf f=(2,0)$.
> >
> > Write the constraints as $f_1+f_2\le2$, $-f_1\le0$ and $-f_2\le0$, with multipliers $\nu_c$, $\nu_1$ and $\nu_2$. Stationarity reads $g_{f_1}=\nu_c-\nu_1$ and $g_{f_2}=\nu_c-\nu_2$, with $\nu_1=0$ because $f_1\ge0$ is slack at $f_1=2$.
> >
> > At $(2,0)$, $g_{f_1}=e_1-2\sigma_1^2=0.22751-0.08085=0.1467$, so $\nu_c=0.1467$. Next, $g_{f_2}=e_2-2\sigma_{12}=-0.025466+0.005733=-0.0197$, so $\nu_2=0.1467+0.0197=0.1664$. Both multipliers are non-negative.

> > Interpretation: in-sample, one more unit of gross exposure is worth about 14.7 points of model growth a year. That is the figure to compare with a margin lender's spread over $r_f$. Raising the cap to 2.1 adds 1.45 points exactly, against 1.47 from the multiplier. The multiplier $\nu_2$ prices the no-short rule: allowing a 1% short in 00679B would add about 0.17 points.

The same logic explains long-only minimum variance. When $\rho>\sigma_2/\sigma_1$, formula (7.7) gives a negative weight on the riskier asset. With no short sales, the optimum moves to the corner that holds only the safer asset. The multiplier is then the slope of $V$ at that corner. The widget in Section 6 reports it whenever the minimum leaves the shaded band, and Practice Problem 13 works an example with a leveraged fund.

> [!pitfall] Checking only some of the KKT conditions
> Every candidate in [[ex:kkt]] satisfies stationarity, and each rejected one fails a different test. "Cap only" and "$f_2\ge0$ only" are infeasible: one shorts 00679B, the other ignores the cap. "Cap and $f_1\ge0$" is feasible but has negative multipliers, which says that relaxing those constraints would *lower* growth: they are not what holds the solution back. Report a constrained optimum only after all four conditions of (7.13) hold. State which constraints bind, because the binding set is what changes when the mandate changes.

> [!zh] KKT 條件：限制式是緊還是鬆
> 不等式限制（不可放空、最低股票比重、槓桿上限）在最優點只有兩種狀態：沒碰到（鬆弛），就當它不存在，乘數為 0；碰到了（緊），就當成等式處理，乘數必須 ≥ 0，表示這個限制確實擋住了你想去的方向。這就是互補鬆弛性：乘數與鬆弛量至少有一個是 0。實務解法是列舉哪些限制是緊的，對每個候選解檢查四個條件，缺一不可。槓桿上限的乘數就是「多一倍槓桿能多賺多少成長率」，可以直接拿來和融資利差比較；樣本內這個數字是每年 14.7 個百分點，但它和凱利比例一樣，是樣本內的估計值。

## Multivariable Optimization in Python {lo=8}

Closed forms such as (7.7) and (7.12) exist because the problems are quadratic in two variables. The exact growth function is not quadratic, and real problems have more assets and messier constraints. Three numerical tools cover most cases: finite-difference derivatives, Newton's method, and a constrained solver.

### Numerical gradients and Hessians

Central differences carry over from [[LM2]] coordinate by coordinate, with an error of order $h^2$. For a mixed partial derivative, difference in both coordinates:

$$f_{xy}\approx\frac{f(x+h,y+h)-f(x+h,y-h)-f(x-h,y+h)+f(x-h,y-h)}{4h^2}.$$

The code below builds the exact growth function $g(f_1,f_2)$ of Section 3 and checks its derivatives at $\mathbf f=\mathbf 0$ against the moments.

```python
from datetime import date
import numpy as np
import polars as pl

df = pl.read_parquet("lm07_returns.parquet").drop_nulls("R_0050")
R = df.select("R_0050", "R_00679B").to_numpy()               # T x 2 daily total returns
years = (date.fromisoformat(df["date"][-1]) - date(2017, 1, 17)).days / 365.25
A, rf = len(R) / years, 0.015                                  # 243.05 days a year; illustrative r_f
r = rf / A
X = R - r                                                      # daily excess returns

def g(f):
    """Annualized sample log growth of exposures f = (f1, f2), financed at r_f."""
    return A * np.log1p(r + X @ f).mean()

def num_grad(fun, x, h=1e-5):
    e = np.eye(len(x))
    return np.array([(fun(x + h * e[i]) - fun(x - h * e[i])) / (2 * h) for i in range(len(x))])

def num_hess(fun, x, h=1e-4):
    e, n = np.eye(len(x)), len(x)
    return np.array([[(fun(x + h*e[i] + h*e[j]) - fun(x + h*e[i] - h*e[j])
                       - fun(x - h*e[i] + h*e[j]) + fun(x - h*e[i] - h*e[j])) / (4 * h * h)
                      for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)])

print(num_grad(g, np.zeros(2)).round(4))    # [ 0.2275 -0.0255]  ~ (e1, e2)
print(num_hess(g, np.zeros(2)).round(4))    # [[-0.0406  0.0029] [ 0.0029 -0.0182]]  ~ -Sigma
```

### Gradient ascent and Newton's method

**Gradient ascent** repeats $\mathbf f_{k+1}=\mathbf f_k+\alpha\nabla g(\mathbf f_k)$: take a step uphill, re-measure the slope, repeat. Let $c_{\min}$ and $c_{\max}$ be the smallest and largest curvatures of a quadratic with Hessian $-\Sigma$, the eigenvalues of $\Sigma$ ([[LM11]]). The best fixed step is then $\alpha=2/(c_{\min}+c_{\max})$. With it, the distance to the optimum shrinks at least by the factor $(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)$ per step, where $\kappa=c_{\max}/c_{\min}$ is the **condition number**. The bound is stated without proof; see Nocedal and Wright (2006). For the full-sample $\Sigma$ the curvatures are 0.0179 and 0.0408, so $\kappa=2.29$, $\alpha=34.1$ and the factor is 0.39.

Near its optimum the exact growth function is more curved, with curvatures 0.0198 and 0.0493. The same step therefore contracts the distance by $\lvert1-34.1\times0.0493\rvert=0.68$ per step, and the growth shortfall by about 0.46.

**Newton's method** replaces the function by its second-order Taylor model (7.2) at the current point and jumps to the model's maximum. The update is $\mathbf f_{k+1}=\mathbf f_k-H(\mathbf f_k)^{-1}\nabla g(\mathbf f_k)$, the two-variable version of [[LM4]]'s iteration. On a quadratic it lands on the optimum in one step. On the exact growth function, the first step from $\mathbf 0$ lands at $(5.5621,\,-0.5156)$, near the model optimum $(5.59,\,-0.52)$, because at $\mathbf 0$ the Taylor model is essentially (7.3). Two more steps reach $(5.3186,\,-0.2641)$.

```python
def grad_hess(f):
    """Exact gradient and Hessian of g: A*mean(X/(1+R_p)) and -A*mean(X X'/(1+R_p)^2)."""
    w = 1 / (1 + r + X @ f)
    return A * (X * w[:, None]).mean(0), -A * (X.T * w**2) @ X / len(X)

f = np.zeros(2)
for k in range(1, 5):
    gr, H = grad_hess(f)
    f = f - np.linalg.solve(H, gr)                             # Newton step
    print(k, f.round(4), f"{g(f):.4%}")
# 1 [ 5.5621 -0.5156] 63.7851%
# 2 [ 5.3231 -0.2675] 64.0296%
# 3 [ 5.3186 -0.2641] 64.0296%
# 4 [ 5.3186 -0.2641] 64.0296%
```

Newton needs two iterations to bring the shortfall below $10^{-6}$ and three to go below $10^{-10}$; gradient ascent needs 18 and 30 ([[ex:converge]]). The price of Newton is the Hessian: $N^2$ second derivatives and a linear solve per step, cheap for two assets and expensive for two thousand. Gradient methods dominate large problems for that reason, and badly conditioned problems ($\kappa$ in the thousands) are where they struggle.

### Constrained problems with `scipy.optimize.minimize`

For constraints, `scipy.optimize.minimize` with `method="SLSQP"` (sequential least-squares quadratic programming; Kraft 1988) handles bounds, equalities and inequalities. It solves a sequence of quadratic models, each a small KKT problem. Verify a multiplier the way (7.6) defines it: re-solve with the constraint relaxed and divide the change in the optimum by the change in the constraint. Recent SciPy versions also report the multipliers in the result object.

```python
from scipy.optimize import minimize

def kelly_capped(cap):
    """Maximize exact sample growth subject to f1 + f2 <= cap and f >= 0."""
    res = minimize(lambda f: -g(f), x0=[0.5, 0.5], method="SLSQP", bounds=[(0, None), (0, None)],
                   constraints=[{"type": "ineq", "fun": lambda f: cap - f.sum()}])
    return res.x, -res.fun

f2, g2 = kelly_capped(2.0)
print(f2.round(4), f"{g2:.4%}")                                # [2. 0.] 38.8741%
nu_cap = (kelly_capped(2.01)[1] - kelly_capped(1.99)[1]) / 0.02
print(f"shadow price of the cap: {nu_cap:.4f}")               # 0.1461
```

The exact solution agrees with [[eg:cap]]: the cap and the no-short rule bind, and the cap's shadow price is 0.146 against the model's 0.147.

### A symbolic check with SymPy

SymPy solves the Lagrangian conditions of Section 6 symbolically and confirms both (7.7) and $\lambda^*=2\sigma_{\min}^2$:

```python
import sympy as sp

w1, w2, lam = sp.symbols("w1 w2 lambda")
s1, s2, s12 = sp.symbols("sigma1 sigma2 sigma12", positive=True)
V = w1**2 * s1**2 + 2 * w1 * w2 * s12 + w2**2 * s2**2
L = V - lam * (w1 + w2 - 1)
sol = sp.solve([sp.diff(L, w1), sp.diff(L, w2), sp.diff(L, lam)], [w1, w2, lam], dict=True)[0]
print(sp.simplify(sol[w1]))                                    # (-sigma12 + sigma2**2)/(sigma1**2 - 2*sigma12 + sigma2**2)
print(sp.factor(sp.simplify(sol[lam] - 2 * V.subs(sol))))      # 0: the multiplier is twice the minimum variance
```

> Why does Newton's method reach the maximum of the model (7.3) in one step from any starting point, but need three steps on the exact growth function?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > Each Newton step maximizes the second-order Taylor model at the current point. For (7.3) that model *is* the function, because (7.3) is quadratic, so the first step lands on $\mathbf f^*$. The exact growth function has third- and higher-order terms, so each step lands only near the optimum. ## Summary {-}

- A partial derivative is a sensitivity with every other input frozen; the total differential (7.1) adds them up to first order. For a TWD holder of 00679B in 2022, the bond and currency terms gave −18.85% and the cross term a further −3.24%.
- The gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent and is perpendicular to level curves. The Hessian measures curvature, and the second-order expansion (7.2) is exact for quadratics. On the case data, the first-order terms explained 74% of the rise in the 42/58 mix's variance and the second-order terms nearly all the rest.
- The quadratic growth model $g\approx r_f+f_1e_1+f_2e_2-\tfrac12(f_1^2\sigma_1^2+2f_1f_2\sigma_{12}+f_2^2\sigma_2^2)$ is accurate to about 1 bp for unlevered mixes. At full Kelly it overstates growth by 2 points a year, because fat tails dominate.
- Interior optima have $\nabla f=\mathbf 0$. The $2\times2$ test (7.4) classifies them through $f_{xx}$ and $D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2$; convexity turns a local minimum into a global one.
- Lagrange's condition $\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g$ (7.5) locates constrained optima. The multiplier is the shadow price $V'(c)=\lambda$ (7.6): the marginal cost of the last TX contract, or the best Sharpe ratio for a volatility budget.
- The two-asset minimum-variance weight (7.7) depends only on variances and the covariance, and $\lambda^*=2\sigma_{\min}^2$. Estimated on 2017–2021 it gave 42.3% in 0050 and 9.22% predicted volatility; in 2022–2026 the mix realized 12.44%, mainly because 0050's volatility and the correlation rose. By (7.9), the 17-point weight error cost only 0.9 points.
- The tangency weights (7.10) and Kelly fractions (7.12) share the same numerators: Kelly is the tangency portfolio levered by $L^*=f_1^*+f_2^*$, and $g^*=r_f+\tfrac12SR^{*2}$ with $SR^*$ from (7.11). Estimates swing violently between windows; in-sample optima are not recommendations.
- The KKT conditions (7.13) add complementary slackness and non-negative multipliers for inequalities. Enumerating binding sets solves small problems; Newton's method and SLSQP solve larger ones, and bumping a constraint verifies its multiplier.

The estimate is exact because $f$ is linear in $y$ for fixed $x$.

3. **A** is correct. $V_{w_1}=0.08w_1+0.008w_2=0.044$ and $V_{w_2}=0.02w_2+0.008w_1=0.014$. C forgets the factor 2 from differentiating the squares and the cross term.

At $(0.20,-0.10)$: $0.05-0.01125=3.875\%$, against the exact $\sqrt{1.08}-1=3.923\%$, an error of 4.8 bp from third-order terms. The two returns have mean $\tfrac12(x+y)$ and population standard deviation $s=\lvert x-y\rvert/2=15\%$, so the quadratic term $-\tfrac18(x-y)^2=-s^2/2$ is exactly the volatility drag of [[LM3]] (3.9).

5. **C** is correct. $f_{xx}=2$, $f_{yy}=2$, $f_{xy}=4$, so $D=4-16=-12<0$. For instance $f(t,-t)=-2t^2<0<f(t,t)=6t^2$.

6. $f_x=3x^2-3=0$ gives $x=\pm1$; $f_y=3y^2-12=0$ gives $y=\pm2$. With $f_{xx}=6x$, $f_{yy}=6y$ and $f_{xy}=0$, $D=36xy$. At $(1,2)$: $D=72>0$, $f_{xx}=6>0$, a local minimum with $f=2$. At $(-1,-2)$: $D=72>0$, $f_{xx}=-6<0$, a local maximum with $f=38$. At $(1,-2)$ and $(-1,2)$: $D=-72<0$, saddles, with $f=34$ and $f=6$.

8. **A** is correct. By (7.6), $\Delta V\approx\lambda^*\Delta b=0.02\times0.05=0.0010$. Since $\lambda^*=2\sigma_{\min}^2$, $\sigma_{\min}^2=0.01$, and exactly $(1.05^2-1)\times0.01=0.001025$; the difference is second order.

9. $\sigma_{12}=0.3\times0.25\times0.15=0.01125$ and $D_V=0.0625+0.0225-0.0225=0.0625$.
