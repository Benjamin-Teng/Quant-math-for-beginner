## Introduction

Every portfolio decision turns more than one dial: how much 0050, how much bond, how much leverage. Single-variable calculus ([[LM2]], [[LM4]]) turns one dial at a time; this module turns them together, adds constraints and prices the constraints themselves. Applied to two assets, it yields the minimum-variance portfolio, the tangency portfolio and the Kelly fractions, and it shows why an answer fitted to the past is not the answer you will get.

> [!case] How much bond minimizes risk?
> A TWD investor holds Yuanta Taiwan 50 (0050) and considers adding Yuanta U.S. Treasury 20+ Year Bond ETF (00679B). The fund tracks the ICE US Treasury 20+ Year Bond Index and is quoted in New Taiwan dollars. Its holdings report lists US Treasuries and US-dollar cash but no currency forwards, so its returns combine US long-bond returns with USD/TWD moves.
>
> From the close of 17 January 2017, 00679B's first trading day, to 8 October 2026, 0050 returned +767.3% with dividends reinvested, at 20.1% annualized volatility. 00679B returned −17.3% at 13.5%. Their daily returns were mildly negatively correlated, −0.11 ([[ex:case]]).
>
> Suppose the investor had sized the mix at the end of 2021 with the five years then available. Over 2017–2021 the correlation was −0.22, and the calculus of Section 6 says that 42.3% in 0050 and 57.7% in 00679B minimized volatility, at a predicted 9.22% a year.
>
> Then came 2022: 0050 lost 21.3% and 00679B lost 22.1%. Over 2022–2026 the correlation drifted to −0.02, and the same mix realized 12.44%. Was the calculus wrong, was the weight wrong, or did the inputs change? By the end of Section 6 you will split the 3.2-point miss into its parts.

> [!note] Notation for this module
> A generic function $h$ of generic coordinates $x,y$, instantiated per example, has partial derivatives $h_x=\partial h/\partial x$ and $h_{xy}=\partial^2h/\partial y\,\partial x$, gradient $\nabla h$ and Hessian $H$. Constraints read $\psi(\mathbf x)=b$ or $\psi_i(\mathbf x)\le b_i$, with multipliers $\lambda$ (equality) and $\nu$ (inequality) and optimal value $J(b)$. Bold letters are vectors: weights $\mathbf w$, exposures per unit of capital $\mathbf f$ (Kelly fractions), expected returns $\boldsymbol\mu$, excess returns $\mathbf e$ ($e_i=\mu_i-r_f$), $\mathbf 1=(1,1)$, a point $\mathbf a$, a direction $\mathbf u$, a step $\boldsymbol\Delta$. For two assets, $\sigma_1,\sigma_2$ are annualized volatilities, $\sigma_{12}$ the covariance, $\rho$ the correlation, $\sigma_d^2$ the variance of $R_1-R_2$; $V$ is a portfolio variance, $\sigma_b$ a volatility budget, $g$ a log growth rate, $m,M$ the smallest and largest curvatures of $\Sigma$. Counts: $T$ returns, $N$ assets, iteration $k$; $i,j$ index assets or constraints. Moments use divisor $T$, as in [[LM3]]; with 1,150 or more returns, $T-1$ would change volatilities by under 0.05%.

## Functions of Several Variables and Partial Derivatives {lo=1}

A portfolio's volatility depends on two weights, two volatilities and a correlation at once, and a TWD investor's return on a US bond depends on the bond and on the currency.

### Functions, surfaces and level curves

> [!definition] Function of two variables
> A **function of two variables** assigns to each point $(x,y)$ of a domain $\mathcal D\subseteq\mathbb R^2$ one number $h(x,y)$. Its **graph** is the surface $z=h(x,y)$. A **level curve** (contour) is the set of points where $h$ takes a fixed value $b$, $\{(x,y): h(x,y)=b\}$.

Level curves are the map view of the surface. The central example is the variance of a two-asset portfolio. With weights $w_1,w_2$ and daily returns $R_{1t},R_{2t}$, the portfolio return $R_{pt}=w_1R_{1t}+w_2R_{2t}$ deviates from its mean by $w_1(R_{1t}-\bar R_1)+w_2(R_{2t}-\bar R_2)$. Squaring and averaging gives

$$\begin{aligned}\frac1T\sum_t(R_{pt}-\bar R_p)^2&=w_1^2\,\frac1T\sum_t(R_{1t}-\bar R_1)^2+2w_1w_2\,\frac1T\sum_t(R_{1t}-\bar R_1)(R_{2t}-\bar R_2)\\&\quad+w_2^2\,\frac1T\sum_t(R_{2t}-\bar R_2)^2 .\end{aligned}$$

The middle average is the **covariance** of the two returns, positive when they tend to fall on the same side of their means ([[LM16]]). Divided by the two standard deviations it gives the **correlation** $\rho$. It lies between −1 and 1 by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $\big\lvert\sum_tx_ty_t\big\rvert\le\sqrt{\sum_tx_t^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_ty_t^2}$, applied to the deviations (stated without proof; [[LM8]]). Annualizing each average with $A$,

$$V(w_1,w_2)=w_1^2\sigma_1^2+2w_1w_2\sigma_{12}+w_2^2\sigma_2^2,\qquad \sigma_{12}=\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2 .$$

The same expansion gives two facts used later. Averaging $(R_{1t}-\bar R_1)(R_{pt}-\bar R_p)$ instead shows that the covariance of $R_1$ with the portfolio is $w_1\sigma_1^2+w_2\sigma_{12}$, and weights $(1,-1)$ give $\operatorname{Var}(R_1-R_2)=\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2-2\sigma_{12}$.

When $\lvert\rho\rvert<1$, the level curves of $V$ are ellipses centred on the all-cash point $(0,0)$, one for each variance level. The budget rule $w_1+w_2=1$ is a straight line across them. Section 5 shows that the minimum-variance portfolio sits where that line just touches an ellipse, and [[ex:levels]] draws the picture.

### Partial derivatives

> [!definition] Partial derivative
> The **partial derivative** of $h$ with respect to $x$ at $(x_0,y_0)$ is the ordinary derivative of the one-variable function $x\mapsto h(x,y_0)$:
> $$h_x(x_0,y_0)=\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}(x_0,y_0):=\lim_{\delta\to0}\frac{h(x_0+\delta,y_0)-h(x_0,y_0)}{\delta}.$$
> $h_y$ is defined in the same way with $x$ held at $x_0$.

To compute a partial derivative, treat every other variable as a constant and apply the rules of [[LM2]]. For the portfolio variance,

$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial w_1}=2\big(w_1\sigma_1^2+w_2\sigma_{12}\big),\qquad \frac{\partial V}{\partial w_2}=2\big(w_1\sigma_{12}+w_2\sigma_2^2\big).$$

The first bracket is the covariance of asset 1 with the portfolio, found above. So the marginal variance of a position is twice its covariance with the portfolio it joins, a fact [[LM31]] builds into risk budgeting.

**Higher-order partial derivatives.** Differentiating again gives $h_{xx}$, $h_{yy}$ and the mixed partials $h_{xy}=(h_x)_y$ and $h_{yx}=(h_y)_x$. For every function in this book the order does not matter.

> [!theorem] Symmetry of mixed partial derivatives (Schwarz–Clairaut)
> If $h_{xy}$ and $h_{yx}$ exist and are continuous near $(x_0,y_0)$, then $h_{xy}(x_0,y_0)=h_{yx}(x_0,y_0)$. Stated without proof; see Stewart, Clegg and Watson (2021, §14.3).

### The total differential and the linear approximation

Move both inputs at once, by $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$. Going first along $x$ and then along $y$, and using the one-variable linear approximation of [[LM2]] on each leg, gives

$$h(x_0+\Delta x,\,y_0+\Delta y)\approx h(x_0,y_0)+h_x(x_0,y_0)\,\Delta x+h_y(x_0,y_0)\,\Delta y. \tag{7.1}$$

When $h$ has continuous partial derivatives, the error is small relative to the step; when its second partial derivatives are continuous too, the error is of second order in $(\Delta x,\Delta y)$. The linear part, $dh=h_x\,dx+h_y\,dy$, is the **total differential**. Dividing by a time step gives the multivariable **chain rule**: if $x=x(t)$ and $y=y(t)$, then $\frac{d}{dt}h(x(t),y(t))=h_x\,x'(t)+h_y\,y'(t)$.

In 2022, 00679B returned −22.09% in TWD, while USD/TWD rose from 27.690 to 30.708, +10.90%. > [!pitfall] "Holding everything else fixed" is not always possible
> In a fully invested portfolio the weights cannot move one at a time: buying 0050 means selling something. So $\partial V/\partial w_1$ describes a trade financed with cash or margin, case (a) above; a budget-neutral switch needs the derivative along $(1,-1)$, case (b), or substitution of the constraint (Sections 5 and 6). Likewise, "$\partial\sigma_p/\partial\rho$ holding volatilities fixed" ignores that in a crisis volatilities and correlations tend to move together.

## Gradient, Hessian and the Multivariate Taylor Expansion {lo=2}

### Directional derivatives and the gradient

Pick a direction, given by a unit vector $\mathbf u=(u_1,u_2)$, and walk from $\mathbf a$ along it; the height of the surface along the walk is $z(t):=h(\mathbf a+t\mathbf u)$. By the chain rule, its slope at the start, the **directional derivative**, is

$$\partial_{\mathbf u}h(\mathbf a):=z'(0)=h_x(\mathbf a)\,u_1+h_y(\mathbf a)\,u_2 .$$

> [!definition] Gradient
> The **gradient** of $h$ at $\mathbf a$ is the vector of its partial derivatives, $\nabla h(\mathbf a):=\big(h_x(\mathbf a),\,h_y(\mathbf a)\big)$. Every directional derivative is a dot product with it: $\partial_{\mathbf u}h=\nabla h\cdot\mathbf u$.

Two geometric facts follow. First, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $\nabla h\cdot\mathbf u\le\lVert\nabla h\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf u\rVert=\lVert\nabla h\rVert$, with equality only when $\mathbf u$ points along $\nabla h$. So **the gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its length is the steepest slope**.

Second, if a point $\mathbf x(t)$ moves along a level curve, then $h(\mathbf x(t))=b$, and differentiating gives $\nabla h\cdot\mathbf x'(t)=0$. So **the gradient is perpendicular to the level curves**.

For a step $\boldsymbol\Delta$ that is not a unit vector, $\nabla h\cdot\boldsymbol\Delta$ is the first-order change in $h$; case (b) of [[eg:marginal]] used exactly this, with $\boldsymbol\Delta=(0.01,-0.01)$.

### The Hessian

> [!definition] Hessian
> The **Hessian** of $h$ at $\mathbf a$ is the matrix of its second partial derivatives,
> $$H(\mathbf a):=\begin{pmatrix} h_{xx} & h_{xy}\\ h_{yx} & h_{yy}\end{pmatrix}\!(\mathbf a),$$
> symmetric when the Schwarz–Clairaut theorem applies.

The Hessian measures curvature in every direction at once. Differentiating $z(t)=h(\mathbf a+t\mathbf u)$ twice with the chain rule gives

$$z''(0)=h_{xx}u_1^2+2h_{xy}u_1u_2+h_{yy}u_2^2=:\mathbf u^{\top}H\,\mathbf u ,$$

the curvature of $h$ along $\mathbf u$. For the portfolio variance the Hessian is constant, $H=2\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_1^2&\sigma_{12}\\ \sigma_{12}&\sigma_2^2\end{pmatrix}=2\Sigma$: twice the **covariance matrix** $\Sigma$, the object [[LM10]] studies.

### The second-order Taylor expansion

The one-variable Taylor polynomial of [[LM3]], applied to $z(t)=h(\mathbf a+t\boldsymbol\Delta)$ at $t=1$, gives $z(1)\approx z(0)+z'(0)+\tfrac12z''(0)$. Substituting the two derivatives just computed:

$$h(\mathbf a+\boldsymbol\Delta)\approx h(\mathbf a)+h_x\Delta_1+h_y\Delta_2+\tfrac12\big(h_{xx}\Delta_1^2+2h_{xy}\Delta_1\Delta_2+h_{yy}\Delta_2^2\big)=h(\mathbf a)+\nabla h\cdot\boldsymbol\Delta+\tfrac12\,\boldsymbol\Delta^{\top}H\,\boldsymbol\Delta, \tag{7.2}$$

with all derivatives evaluated at $\mathbf a$. If $h$ has continuous third partial derivatives, the Lagrange remainder of [[LM3]] (3.5), applied to $z$, puts the error at $z'''(\xi)/6$ for some $\xi\in(0,1)$: third order in $\boldsymbol\Delta$. With three variables the pattern is the same, with three first derivatives and a $3\times3$ Hessian. A polynomial of degree two is reproduced exactly by (7.2), and one of degree three once the third-order terms are added.

### A Taylor attribution of the 42/58 mix's extra risk

Hold the weights of the case fixed at $w_1=42.317\%$ in 0050 and $w_2=57.683\%$ in 00679B. Their variance is then a function of three inputs,

$$V(\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\rho)=w_1^2\sigma_1^2+w_2^2\sigma_2^2+2w_1w_2\,\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2 .$$

Between the estimation and test windows the inputs moved from $(16.37\%,\,13.55\%,\,-0.221)$ to $(23.40\%,\,13.43\%,\,-0.022)$. The partial derivatives at the starting point, from the unrounded estimates, are $V_{\sigma_1}=2w_1^2\sigma_1+2w_1w_2\rho\sigma_2=0.043994$, $V_{\sigma_2}=2w_2^2\sigma_2+2w_1w_2\rho\sigma_1=0.072465$ and $V_\rho=2w_1w_2\sigma_1\sigma_2=0.010827$. Multiplying each by its input's change gives the first-order terms of [[ex:attrib]].

The first-order approximation explains 51.70 of the 69.67 %² rise in variance, or 74%. The Hessian terms supply the rest, slightly overshooting. The two largest are $\tfrac12V_{\sigma_1\sigma_1}(\Delta\sigma_1)^2=8.86$, with $V_{\sigma_1\sigma_1}=2w_1^2$, and the cross term $V_{\sigma_1\rho}\,\Delta\sigma_1\Delta\rho=9.28$, with $V_{\sigma_1\rho}=2w_1w_2\sigma_2$. A higher correlation hurts more when 0050 is more volatile, and only a second-order model can see that. Because $V$ is a polynomial of degree three in its inputs, the single third-order term $2w_1w_2\,\Delta\sigma_1\Delta\sigma_2\Delta\rho$, about −0.08, makes the expansion exact.

### A two-variable Taylor model of portfolio growth

The most important use of (7.2) in this book is the growth rate of a levered portfolio. Hold a fraction $f_1$ of capital in 0050 and $f_2$ in 00679B, and finance the rest at an illustrative risk-free rate $r_f=1.5\%$ a year, $r_f/A$ a day. The daily return is $R_p=r_f/A+f_1X_1+f_2X_2$, with excess returns $X_i=R_i-r_f/A$. The quantity a Kelly investor maximizes (Kelly 1956; [[LM4]]) is the average log growth, annualized:

$$g(f_1,f_2):=A\cdot\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ln\!\big(1+r_f/A+f_1X_{1t}+f_2X_{2t}\big).$$

Differentiate under the sum: $g_{f_i}=A\cdot\frac1T\sum_tX_{it}/(1+R_{pt})$ and $g_{f_if_j}=-A\cdot\frac1T\sum_tX_{it}X_{jt}/(1+R_{pt})^2$. At $\mathbf f=\mathbf 0$ these are the mean excess returns and minus the mean products, each divided by a power of $1+r_f/A$.

On the data, $\nabla g(\mathbf 0)=(0.2275,\,-0.0255)$, which is $(e_1,e_2)$ up to the factor $1/(1+r_f/A)$. The Hessian $H(\mathbf 0)$ has entries $(-0.0406,\;0.0029,\;-0.0182)$, against $-\sigma_1^2=-0.0404$, $-\sigma_{12}=0.0029$ and $-\sigma_2^2=-0.0182$. The small gap in the first entry is the squared daily mean, smaller than the variance by a factor of order $1/A$. Dropping it and the factors $1+r_f/A$, (7.2) becomes

$$g(f_1,f_2)\approx r_f+f_1e_1+f_2e_2-\tfrac12\big(f_1^2\sigma_1^2+2f_1f_2\sigma_{12}+f_2^2\sigma_2^2\big). \tag{7.3}$$

The same model follows from expanding $\ln(1+R_p)\approx R_p-\tfrac12R_p^2$ day by day and averaging, as in [[LM3]] (3.8). It neglects the squared daily mean, $\tfrac12A\bar R_p^{\,2}$, and the terms of third and higher order in the daily portfolio return. In matrix form (7.3) reads $g\approx r_f+\mathbf f^{\top}\mathbf e-\tfrac12\mathbf f^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf f$, the starting point of [[LM43]].

## Unconstrained Optimization in Two Variables {lo=3}

[[LM4]] found optima of one-variable functions with two conditions: a zero derivative and the sign of the second derivative. Both carry over to two variables, the second with more care, because a surface can curve up in one direction and down in another.

### First-order conditions

If $h$ is differentiable and has a local maximum or minimum at an interior point $\mathbf a$, then every one-variable slice through $\mathbf a$ has zero slope there. In particular $h_x(\mathbf a)=h_y(\mathbf a)=0$, that is, $\nabla h(\mathbf a)=\mathbf 0$. Points where the gradient vanishes are **critical** (or stationary) **points**. As in one variable, the condition is necessary, not sufficient.

### Second-order conditions: the 2×2 test

At a critical point the linear term of (7.2) vanishes, so for small steps $h(\mathbf a+\boldsymbol\Delta)-h(\mathbf a)\approx\tfrac12\boldsymbol\Delta^{\top}H\boldsymbol\Delta$, and the sign of this quadratic form decides the shape. If $h_{xx}\neq0$, completing the square gives

$$\boldsymbol\Delta^{\top}H\boldsymbol\Delta=h_{xx}\Big(\Delta_1+\frac{h_{xy}}{h_{xx}}\Delta_2\Big)^2+\frac{\det H}{h_{xx}}\,\Delta_2^2,\qquad \det H:=h_{xx}h_{yy}-h_{xy}^2. \tag{7.4}$$

If $\det H>0$, both terms have the sign of $h_{xx}$, and the form is non-zero for every $\boldsymbol\Delta\neq\mathbf 0$. If $\det H<0$, the two terms have opposite signs, and the form takes both signs. When $h$ has continuous second partial derivatives, the remainder of (7.2) is small next to $\lVert\boldsymbol\Delta\rVert^2$ near $\mathbf a$ and cannot overturn a definite form (stated without proof; Stewart, Clegg and Watson 2021, §14.7). When $\det H=0$ the test is silent and higher-order terms decide: $x^2+y^4$ has a minimum at the origin and $x^2+y^3$ does not, with the same Hessian there. [[ex:dtest]] collects the cases. In matrix language, $H$ is **positive definite** exactly when $h_{xx}>0$ and $\det H>0$, the $2\times2$ case of a criterion [[LM10]] extends to $N$ assets.

> [!exhibit] The second-derivative test at a critical point of $h(x,y)$ {#dtest}
> | Condition | Quadratic form $\boldsymbol\Delta^{\top}H\boldsymbol\Delta$ | Shape | Example in this module |
> |:--|:--|:--|:--|
> | $\det H>0$ and $h_{xx}>0$ | positive definite | local minimum (bowl) | portfolio variance $V(w_1,w_2)$ with $\lvert\rho\rvert<1$ |
> | $\det H>0$ and $h_{xx}<0$ | negative definite | local maximum (dome) | the growth model (7.3) |
> | $\det H<0$ | indefinite | saddle point | $x^3-3xy+y^3$ at the origin ([[eg:classify]]) |
> | $\det H=0$ | semidefinite or degenerate | test inconclusive | $V$ with $\rho=\pm1$: a valley floor |
>
> Note: $\det H$ at the critical point; the test classifies local behaviour only.

### Global optima and convexity

A local test says nothing about the rest of the surface. One condition does: if $\boldsymbol\Delta^{\top}H\boldsymbol\Delta\ge0$ for every $\boldsymbol\Delta$ at every point (the Hessian is positive semidefinite everywhere), $h$ is **convex**, and any critical point is a global minimum (stated without proof; [[LM29]] develops convexity). Quadratic functions have a constant Hessian, so for them the local test is global.

Portfolio variance has $H=2\Sigma$ with $\det H=4\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2(1-\rho^2)\ge0$: it is convex, and strictly convex when $\lvert\rho\rvert<1$. The growth model (7.3) has $H=-\Sigma$ and is concave.

> [!example] A saddle and a local minimum {#classify}
> Find and classify the critical points of $h(x,y)=x^3-3xy+y^3$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > $h_x=3x^2-3y=0$ gives $y=x^2$, and $h_y=3y^2-3x=0$ gives $x=y^2$. Hence $x=x^4$, so $x=0$ or $x=1$, and the critical points are $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$. The second derivatives are $h_{xx}=6x$, $h_{yy}=6y$ and $h_{xy}=-3$.
> >
> > At $(0,0)$: $\det H=0\cdot0-9=-9<0$, a saddle. Indeed $h(t,-t)=3t^2>0$ while $h(t,t)=2t^3-3t^2<0$ for small $t\neq0$.
> >
> > At $(1,1)$: $h_{xx}=6>0$ and $\det H=36-9=27>0$, a local minimum with $h(1,1)=-1$. It is not global, because $h(x,0)=x^3\to-\infty$ as $x\to-\infty$. Without convexity, a local test cannot certify a global optimum.

> [!pitfall] When $\det H=0$: perfectly correlated assets
> With $\lvert\rho\rvert=1$ the variance Hessian $2\Sigma$ has $\det H=0$. The minimum is then a whole line of weights (a valley floor) or a riskless long–short hedge. The growth model (7.3) is then unbounded unless $\mathrm{SR}_1=\mathrm{SR}_2$ (for $\rho=+1$) or $\mathrm{SR}_1=-\mathrm{SR}_2$ (for $\rho=-1$), where $\mathrm{SR}_i=e_i/\sigma_i$ is asset $i$'s annualized Sharpe ratio. Otherwise a riskless spread with a positive excess return can be levered without limit.
>
> Real assets are never exactly collinear, but correlations near ±1 make $\det H$ tiny. Optimizers then return huge, unstable long–short weights; [[LM9]] and [[LM11]] treat this ill-conditioning.

> [!kc]
> Classify the critical point $(0,0)$ of $h(x,y)=x^2+3xy+2y^2$. Every coefficient is positive; is the origin a minimum?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > $h_{xx}=2$, $h_{yy}=4$, $h_{xy}=3$, so $\det H=8-9=-1<0$: a saddle, not a minimum. For example $h(3t,-2t)=9t^2-18t^2+8t^2=-t^2<0<h(t,0)=t^2$. Positive coefficients do not make a quadratic form positive definite; the cross term must satisfy $h_{xy}^2<h_{xx}h_{yy}$.

## Equality Constraints: Lagrange Multipliers {lo=4}

Most portfolio problems come with constraints: the weights must sum to one, the volatility must hit a target, an order must be completed. When a constraint can be solved for one variable, substitution reduces the problem to [[LM4]]. Lagrange's method works without solving the constraint, and it delivers a bonus: the price of the constraint.

### The tangency condition

Consider optimizing $h(x,y)$ subject to $\psi(x,y)=b$, and walk along the constraint curve. At a constrained optimum the slope of $h$ along the curve must be zero, so $\nabla h$ is perpendicular to the curve there. The constraint curve is a level curve of $\psi$, so $\nabla\psi$ is perpendicular to it too (Section 3). Two vectors perpendicular to the same curve are parallel: at the optimum, the level curve of $h$ just touches the constraint curve. [[ex:levels]] shows this for the minimum-variance portfolio.

Algebraically, suppose $\psi_y\neq0$ at the optimum. Near it the constraint defines $y$ as a function of $x$ (the implicit function theorem, stated without proof), and differentiating $\psi(x,y(x))=b$ gives $\psi_x+\psi_y\,y'(x)=0$, so $y'=-\psi_x/\psi_y$. The one-variable function $F(x)=h(x,y(x))$ has a critical point at the optimum:

$$F'(x)=h_x+h_y\,y'(x)=h_x-h_y\frac{\psi_x}{\psi_y}=0 .$$

Writing $\lambda:=h_y/\psi_y$, this says $h_x=\lambda\psi_x$, and $h_y=\lambda\psi_y$ holds by definition of $\lambda$.

> [!theorem] Lagrange multiplier condition
> Let $h$ and $\psi$ have continuous partial derivatives, and let $(x^*,y^*)$ be a local maximum or minimum of $h$ subject to $\psi(x,y)=b$, with $\nabla\psi(x^*,y^*)\neq\mathbf 0$. Then there is a number $\lambda^*$ with
> $$\nabla h(x^*,y^*)=\lambda^*\,\nabla\psi(x^*,y^*),\qquad \psi(x^*,y^*)=b. \tag{7.5}$$
> Equivalently, $(x^*,y^*,\lambda^*)$ is a critical point of the **Lagrangian** $\mathcal L(x,y,\lambda):=h(x,y)-\lambda\,\big(\psi(x,y)-b\big)$.

The three conditions $\mathcal L_x=\mathcal L_y=\mathcal L_\lambda=0$ give candidates; whether a candidate is a maximum, a minimum or neither needs a second-order or a global argument. A convex objective (for a minimum) or a concave one (for a maximum) with a linear constraint makes a candidate the global answer, as in the minimum-variance problems of Section 6; Section 7 states the general result. A curved constraint can produce several candidates: [[eg:budget]] has two.

### The multiplier is a shadow price

> [!result] Shadow price (envelope theorem)
> Let $J(b):=h\big(x^*(b),y^*(b)\big)$ be the optimal value when the constraint level is $b$, and suppose the solution moves smoothly with $b$. Then
> $$J'(b)=\lambda^*(b). \tag{7.6}$$

*Proof.* By the chain rule and (7.5), $J'(b)=h_x\,x^{*\prime}+h_y\,y^{*\prime}=\lambda^*\big(\psi_x\,x^{*\prime}+\psi_y\,y^{*\prime}\big)$. Differentiating the identity $\psi(x^*(b),y^*(b))=b$ shows that the bracket equals 1. $\blacksquare$

This is the envelope theorem of [[LM4]] (4.16) applied to the Lagrangian, whose partial derivative in $b$ is $\lambda$. So $\Delta J\approx\lambda^*\,\Delta b$, with an error of second order in $\Delta b$. The units of $\lambda^*$ are units of objective per unit of constraint: NT\$ per contract, return per unit of risk, variance per unit of capital.

> [!example] Splitting a TX order between two sessions {#split}
> A trader must buy $Q=100$ TX contracts, $x$ in the day session and $y$ after hours. Model the temporary-impact cost as $C=k_dx^2+k_ny^2$ with illustrative coefficients $k_d=2$ and $k_n=5$ (NT\$ per contract squared), the night book being thinner. Find the cost-minimizing split and interpret the multiplier.
>
> > [!solution]
> > $\mathcal L=2x^2+5y^2-\lambda(x+y-100)$. The conditions $\mathcal L_x=4x-\lambda=0$, $\mathcal L_y=10y-\lambda=0$ and $x+y=100$ give $4x=10y=\lambda$, so $x^*=71.43$, $y^*=28.57$ and $\lambda^*=285.7$, in NT\$ per contract.
> >
> > The multiplier is the **marginal cost** of the last contract, and the optimum equalizes it across sessions: if one session were cheaper at the margin, moving a contract there would cut the total.
> >
> > Shadow price: the optimal cost is $C^*(Q)=\frac{k_dk_n}{k_d+k_n}Q^2=\tfrac{10}{7}Q^2$, so $C^*(100)=14{,}286$ (NT\$) and $C^{*\prime}(100)=\tfrac{20}{7}\cdot100=285.7=\lambda^*$, as (7.6) requires. The 101st contract adds NT\$287.1 exactly; the difference from $\lambda^*$ is the second-order term $\tfrac{10}{7}\approx1.4$.
> >
> > In whole contracts a 71/29 split costs NT\$14,287, essentially optimal; an equal split costs NT\$17,500, 22.5% more.

> [!example] A risk budget across two strategies {#budget}
> A desk runs a TX trend strategy (expected excess return $e_1=8\%$, volatility $\sigma_1=16\%$) and a futures–spot basis strategy ($e_2=3\%$, $\sigma_2=4\%$), with correlation $\rho=0.2$ — illustrative figures. Exposures $f_1,f_2$ per unit of capital can be levered. Maximize expected excess return subject to a volatility budget $\sigma_b=10\%$, and interpret the multiplier.
>
> > [!solution]
> > Maximize $f_1e_1+f_2e_2$ subject to $\psi(\mathbf f):=f_1^2\sigma_1^2+2f_1f_2\sigma_{12}+f_2^2\sigma_2^2=\sigma_b^2$, with $\sigma_{12}=0.2\times0.16\times0.04=0.00128$. The Lagrangian conditions are
> > $$e_1=2\lambda\big(\sigma_1^2f_1+\sigma_{12}f_2\big),\qquad e_2=2\lambda\big(\sigma_{12}f_1+\sigma_2^2f_2\big).$$
> > This is a $2\times2$ linear system in $(f_1,f_2)$; eliminating one unknown (Cramer's rule, [[LM9]]) gives $f_1\propto\sigma_2^2e_1-\sigma_{12}e_2$ and $f_2\propto\sigma_1^2e_2-\sigma_{12}e_1$, a ratio of 1 to 7.43. The constraint is an ellipse, so scaling to 10% volatility gives two candidates, $\pm(0.274,\,2.038)$, with $\lambda=\pm4.15$. The minus sign is the minimum, an excess return of $-8.31\%$. The maximum is $\mathbf f=(0.274,\,2.038)$, with $\lambda>0$: the low-volatility basis trade is levered about two times. Posed as $\psi(\mathbf f)\le\sigma_b^2$, the problem rules out the second candidate by the sign condition of Section 7.
> >
> > The expected excess return is 8.31%, against 5.0% for the trend strategy alone at 10% volatility and 7.5% for the basis strategy alone. It equals $\sigma_b\cdot \mathrm{SR}^*$, where $\mathrm{SR}^*=0.831$ is the best attainable Sharpe ratio, derived in Section 6.
> >
> > The multiplier on the variance budget is $\lambda^*=\mathrm{SR}^*/(2\sigma_b)=4.15$. By (7.6), the optimal excess return $E^*$ rises by 4.15 per unit of variance budget. In volatility units $dE^*/d\sigma_b=\mathrm{SR}^*$, so raising the budget from 10% to 11% adds 0.83 percentage points. **The shadow price of risk is the best attainable Sharpe ratio.**

## Two-Asset Portfolios by Calculus {lo=5,6}

This section applies Sections 2–5 to two assets, in the tradition of Markowitz (1952) and of Merton's (1972) closed forms: the minimum-variance weight, in and out of sample, then the tangency portfolio and the Kelly fractions.

### Minimum variance, by substitution and by Lagrange

**Substitution.** With $w$ in asset 1 and $1-w$ in asset 2, the variance is a one-variable function,

$$V(w)=w^2\sigma_1^2+(1-w)^2\sigma_2^2+2w(1-w)\sigma_{12}.$$

Its derivative is $V'(w)=2w\sigma_1^2-2(1-w)\sigma_2^2+2(1-2w)\sigma_{12}$. Setting it to zero:

$$w^*=\frac{\sigma_2^2-\sigma_{12}}{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2-2\sigma_{12}}. \tag{7.7}$$

The second derivative is $V''(w)=2\sigma_d^2$, where $\sigma_d^2:=\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2-2\sigma_{12}=\operatorname{Var}(R_1-R_2)$ (Section 2). It is positive unless the two returns differ by a constant, so $w^*$ is the global minimum. Substituting $w^*$ back:

$$\sigma_{\min}^2=V(w^*)=\frac{\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2-\sigma_{12}^2}{\sigma_d^2}=\frac{\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2(1-\rho^2)}{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2-2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2}. \tag{7.8}$$

Because $V$ is a quadratic in $w$ with second derivative $2\sigma_d^2$, it can be written exactly around its minimum:

$$V(w)=\sigma_{\min}^2+\sigma_d^2\,(w-w^*)^2. \tag{7.9}$$

**Lagrange.** Minimize $V(w_1,w_2)$ subject to $w_1+w_2=1$. With $\mathcal L=V-\lambda(w_1+w_2-1)$, the conditions are

$$2\big(w_1\sigma_1^2+w_2\sigma_{12}\big)=\lambda,\qquad 2\big(w_1\sigma_{12}+w_2\sigma_2^2\big)=\lambda .$$

Subtracting gives $w_1(\sigma_1^2-\sigma_{12})=w_2(\sigma_2^2-\sigma_{12})$, which with $w_1+w_2=1$ is (7.7) again. The two conditions say more: at the minimum, **each asset has the same covariance with the portfolio**.

Multiply the first condition by $w_1$, the second by $w_2$ and add: $2V=\lambda(w_1+w_2)=\lambda$, so $\lambda^*=2\sigma_{\min}^2$. That is the shadow price (7.6) of the budget: investing $b$ units in the same proportions gives variance $b^2\sigma_{\min}^2$, whose derivative at $b=1$ is $2\sigma_{\min}^2$.

### Correlation and diversification

Formula (7.8) shows how correlation sets the benefit of diversification.

- **$\rho=+1$.** Then $\sigma_{\min}^2=0$ only through a short position: $w^*=-\sigma_2/(\sigma_1-\sigma_2)$ when $\sigma_1>\sigma_2$. Long-only, volatility is linear in $w$, and there is no diversification.
- **$\rho=0$.** Then $w^*=\sigma_2^2/(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)$: each asset is weighted by its inverse variance.
- **$\rho=-1$.** Then $w^*=\sigma_2/(\sigma_1+\sigma_2)$ and $\sigma_{\min}=0$: a perfect hedge without shorting.

The minimum-variance portfolio shorts the riskier asset when the numerator of (7.7) turns negative, $\sigma_2^2<\sigma_{12}$, that is, when $\rho>\sigma_2/\sigma_1$ (asset 1 being the riskier one). For 0050 and 00679B the ratio is $13.50/20.11=0.67$, far above any correlation in the sample, so the minimum is interior.

The envelope theorem of [[LM4]] (4.16) prices a change in $\rho$ without differentiating (7.8): because $V'(w^*)=0$, $d\sigma_{\min}^2/d\rho=\partial V/\partial\rho=2w_1^*w_2^*\sigma_1\sigma_2$ at $w^*$. With the 2017–2021 estimates this is 0.0108, so a correlation 0.1 higher raises $\sigma_{\min}^2$ by 0.00108 (exact to that precision) and $\sigma_{\min}$ from 9.22% to 9.79%.

### The tangency portfolio

The portfolio with the highest Sharpe ratio, $(\mu_p-r_f)/\sigma_p$, is the **tangency portfolio**: on a chart of expected return against volatility, it is where a line from the risk-free rate touches the set of risky portfolios ([[LM28]]).

[[eg:budget]] already solved this problem. Maximizing expected excess return for a fixed volatility gave exposures proportional to $\big(\sigma_2^2e_1-\sigma_{12}e_2,\ \sigma_1^2e_2-\sigma_{12}e_1\big)$, whatever the budget. Scaling exposures by a positive constant leaves the Sharpe ratio unchanged, so the same direction maximizes it. Normalizing it to sum to one gives the tangency weights

$$w_1^{\text{tan}}=\frac{\sigma_2^2e_1-\sigma_{12}e_2}{\sigma_2^2e_1+\sigma_1^2e_2-\sigma_{12}(e_1+e_2)},\qquad w_2^{\text{tan}}=1-w_1^{\text{tan}}. \tag{7.10}$$

The highest Sharpe ratio follows by evaluating the ratio on that direction. With $\det\Sigma=\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2-\sigma_{12}^2$, it is $\mathrm{SR}^{*2}=(\sigma_2^2e_1^2-2\sigma_{12}e_1e_2+\sigma_1^2e_2^2)/\det\Sigma$. Dividing numerator and denominator by $\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2$:

$$\mathrm{SR}^{*2}=\frac{\mathrm{SR}_1^2+\mathrm{SR}_2^2-2\rho\,\mathrm{SR}_1\mathrm{SR}_2}{1-\rho^2}. \tag{7.11}$$

Subtracting $\mathrm{SR}_1^2$ from (7.11) leaves $(\mathrm{SR}_2-\rho \mathrm{SR}_1)^2/(1-\rho^2)\ge0$. So adding asset 2 raises the best Sharpe ratio unless $\mathrm{SR}_2=\rho\,\mathrm{SR}_1$, which is exactly when its weight in (7.10) is zero. In [[eg:budget]], $\mathrm{SR}^{*2}=(0.25+0.5625-0.15)/0.96=0.690$, so $\mathrm{SR}^*=0.831$.

When the denominator of (7.10) is near zero the tangency weights explode, as the test-window estimates in [[ex:kelly]] show.

### The two-asset Kelly fractions

The Kelly investor maximizes the growth rate. With the quadratic model (7.3), the first-order conditions are

$$g_{f_1}=e_1-\big(\sigma_1^2f_1+\sigma_{12}f_2\big)=0,\qquad g_{f_2}=e_2-\big(\sigma_{12}f_1+\sigma_2^2f_2\big)=0,$$

a $2\times2$ linear system. Eliminating one unknown (Cramer's rule, [[LM9]]) gives

$$f_1^*=\frac{\sigma_2^2e_1-\sigma_{12}e_2}{\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2-\sigma_{12}^2},\qquad f_2^*=\frac{\sigma_1^2e_2-\sigma_{12}e_1}{\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2-\sigma_{12}^2}. \tag{7.12}$$

The Hessian of (7.3) is $-\Sigma$, with $g_{f_1f_1}=-\sigma_1^2<0$ and $\det(-\Sigma)=\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2(1-\rho^2)>0$ whenever $\lvert\rho\rvert<1$: by [[ex:dtest]], a maximum, and global because (7.3) is concave. Three readings of (7.12) connect it to the rest of the book.

1. **Matrix form.** A $2\times2$ matrix times a vector is the vector of each row's dot product with it. The matrix $\Sigma^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det\Sigma}\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_2^2&-\sigma_{12}\\ -\sigma_{12}&\sigma_1^2\end{pmatrix}$ undoes multiplication by $\Sigma$ ([[LM9]]), and $\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf e$ is exactly (7.12). So $\mathbf f^*=\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu-r_f\mathbf 1)$, the multi-asset Kelly rule of [[LM43]].
2. **Kelly is tangency, levered.** The numerators of (7.12) and (7.10) are the same. Hence $\mathbf f^*=L^*\,\mathbf w^{\text{tan}}$, with total exposure $L^*=f_1^*+f_2^*$. The Kelly investor holds the tangency portfolio and chooses only how much of it to hold.
3. **Growth at the optimum.** At $\mathbf f^*$ the conditions give $\mathbf f^{*\top}\Sigma\mathbf f^*=\mathbf f^{*\top}\mathbf e=\mathrm{SR}^{*2}$, so (7.3) becomes $g^*=r_f+\tfrac12\mathrm{SR}^{*2}$. With one asset, $f^*=e/\sigma^2=(\mu-r_f)/\sigma^2$: [[LM4]] derived $\mu/\sigma^2$ with no financing cost, and [[LM42]] adds $r_f$.

## Inequality Constraints: The KKT Conditions {lo=7}

Real mandates are full of inequalities: no short sales, at least 60% in equities, exposure at most twice capital. [[LM4]] handled one inequality in one variable: a maximum on an interval is either interior, with $h'=0$, or at an endpoint where $h'$ points outward. The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions, found by Karush (1939) and again by Kuhn and Tucker (1951), extend that logic to several variables and constraints; Kjeldsen (2000) tells the history, and [[LM29]] applies the conditions to larger portfolios.

### Slack or binding

At the optimum each inequality constraint is either **slack**, and could be deleted without changing the answer, or **binding**, and behaves like an equality with a multiplier. The multiplier's sign must say that the constraint pushes in the right direction: for a maximization with constraints $\psi_i(\mathbf x)\le b_i$, relaxing a constraint can only help, so its shadow price cannot be negative.

> [!theorem] Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions
> Let $h$ and every $\psi_i$ have continuous partial derivatives. Maximize $h(\mathbf x)$ subject to $\psi_i(\mathbf x)\le b_i$, one inequality for each $i$. If $\mathbf x^*$ is a local maximum and the gradients of the binding constraints are linearly independent (for two of them: not parallel), there are multipliers $\nu_i$ with
> $$\nabla h(\mathbf x^*)=\sum_i\nu_i\nabla\psi_i(\mathbf x^*),\qquad \psi_i(\mathbf x^*)\le b_i,\qquad \nu_i\ge0,\qquad \nu_i\big(b_i-\psi_i(\mathbf x^*)\big)=0. \tag{7.13}$$
> If $h$ is concave and every $\psi_i$ is convex, these conditions are also sufficient for a global maximum. Stated without proof; see Nocedal and Wright (2006, Theorem 12.1) for necessity and Boyd and Vandenberghe (2004, §5.5.3) for sufficiency. For a minimization, apply (7.13) to $-h$. An equality constraint enters as in Section 5, with a multiplier of either sign, and must be linear for sufficiency.

The four parts are **stationarity**, **feasibility**, **dual feasibility** and **complementary slackness**. Stationarity says that the uphill direction $\nabla h$ is a non-negative combination of the binding constraints' outward normals, so every direction that would raise $h$ leaves the feasible set. Complementary slackness says that each constraint either binds or has a zero multiplier. As in (7.6), $\nu_i$ is the rate at which the optimal value rises when $b_i$ is relaxed.

The practical method is **enumeration**. Guess which constraints bind, solve stationarity together with those constraints as equalities, and keep the candidate only if it is feasible and every multiplier is non-negative. With two variables and three constraints there are at most eight binding sets to try.

> [!example] Kelly with a leverage cap and no short sales {#cap}
> Maximize the growth model (7.3) with the full-sample estimates of [[eg:kellyeg]] subject to $f_1+f_2\le2$, $f_1\ge0$ and $f_2\ge0$. The first constraint, illustrative, caps net (total) exposure at twice capital; it equals gross exposure $\lvert f_1\rvert+\lvert f_2\rvert$ only when both positions are long. Identify the binding constraints and interpret the multipliers.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The unconstrained optimum $(5.59,\,-0.52)$ violates the cap and the no-short rule. Of the eight possible binding sets, the one with all three constraints is impossible ($f_1=f_2=0$ breaks $f_1+f_2=2$), which leaves seven; [[ex:kkt]] solves each. Only one passes every test: the cap and $f_2\ge0$ bind, at $\mathbf f=(2,0)$.
> >
> > Write the constraints as $f_1+f_2\le2$, $-f_1\le0$ and $-f_2\le0$, with multipliers $\nu_c$, $\nu_1$ and $\nu_2$. Stationarity reads $g_{f_1}=\nu_c-\nu_1$ and $g_{f_2}=\nu_c-\nu_2$, with $\nu_1=0$ because $f_1\ge0$ is slack at $f_1=2$.
> >
> > At $(2,0)$, $g_{f_1}=e_1-2\sigma_1^2=0.22751-0.08085=0.1467$, so $\nu_c=0.1467$. Next, $g_{f_2}=e_2-2\sigma_{12}=-0.02547+0.005732=-0.0197$, so $\nu_2=0.1467+0.0197=0.1664$. Both multipliers are non-negative.
> >
> > In-sample, at the margin each unit of exposure is worth 14.7 points of model growth a year, the figure to compare with a margin lender's spread over $r_f$. That is a first-order value: exactly, raising the cap to 2.1 adds 1.45 points, and to 3 adds 12.64.
> >
> > $\nu_2$ needs care. Relaxing $f_2\ge0$ to $f_2\ge-0.01$ lets the investor short 1% of 00679B and, because the cap limits net exposure, hold 2.01 in 0050. Re-solving gives $(2.01,\,-0.01)$ and 0.166 points more growth, as $\nu_2$ predicts. The extra 0050 adds 0.1465 and the short 0.0196: since $g_{f_2}=-0.0197<0$, selling 00679B raises growth. Under a gross cap $\lvert f_1\rvert+\lvert f_2\rvert\le2$ the short would use up cap instead: $(1.99,\,-0.01)$ loses 0.127 points, and $(2,0)$ stays optimal.
> >
> > With the exact growth function instead of (7.3), the optimum is the same and the cap's multiplier is 0.146 (Section 8).

> [!pitfall] Checking only some of the KKT conditions
> Every candidate in [[ex:kkt]] satisfies stationarity. Four are infeasible, and three of those beat the optimum's growth because they ignore a constraint. > [!zh] KKT 條件：限制式是緊還是鬆
> 不等式限制（不可放空、最低股票比重、曝險上限）在最適點只有兩種狀態：沒碰到（鬆弛），就當它不存在，乘數為 0；碰到了（緊），就當成等式處理，乘數必須 ≥ 0，表示這個限制確實擋住了你想去的方向。這就是互補鬆弛性：乘數與鬆弛量至少有一個是 0。實務解法是列舉哪些限制是緊的，對每個候選解檢查四個條件，缺一不可。槓桿上限的乘數是邊際上「多一單位曝險能多賺多少成長率」，樣本內約每年 14.7 個百分點，可以直接拿來和融資利差比較；這是一階估計，上限從 2 放寬到 3 實際多 12.64 個百分點。要注意例子裡的上限管的是淨曝險 $f_1+f_2$，放空 00679B 會騰出額度加碼 0050；若上限管的是總曝險 $\lvert f_1\rvert+\lvert f_2\rvert$，放空反而會占用額度。

## Multivariable Optimization in Python {lo=8}

Closed forms such as (7.7) and (7.12) need quadratic problems in two variables; the exact growth function is not quadratic, and real problems have more assets and constraints. Three numerical tools cover most cases: finite-difference derivatives, Newton's method and a constrained solver.

### Numerical gradients and Hessians

Central differences carry over from [[LM2]] coordinate by coordinate, with an error of order $\delta^2$ for a step $\delta$. For a mixed partial derivative, difference in both coordinates (the lab applies this to the Hessian):

$$h_{xy}\approx\frac{h(x+\delta,y+\delta)-h(x+\delta,y-\delta)-h(x-\delta,y+\delta)+h(x-\delta,y-\delta)}{4\delta^2}.$$

The code below builds the exact growth function $g(f_1,f_2)$ of Section 3 from the lab's daily returns and checks its gradient at $\mathbf f=\mathbf 0$ against the mean excess returns.

```python
import numpy as np

# from Part 1 of the lab: daily total returns R1, R2 (0050, 00679B)
# and A = T/years ≈ 243.0543
rf = 0.015                                    # illustrative r_f
rd = rf / A                                   # daily financing rate r_f/A
X = np.column_stack([R1, R2]) - rd            # daily excess returns, T x 2

def g(f):
    """Annualized sample log growth of exposures f, financed at r_f."""
    return A * np.log1p(rd + X @ f).mean()

def num_grad(fun, x, d=1e-5):
    e = np.eye(len(x))
    return np.array([(fun(x + d * e[i]) - fun(x - d * e[i])) / (2 * d)
                     for i in range(len(x))])

print(num_grad(g, np.zeros(2)).round(4))    # [ 0.2275 -0.0255]  ~ (e1, e2)
```

### Gradient ascent and Newton's method

**Gradient ascent** repeats $\mathbf f_{k+1}=\mathbf f_k+\alpha\nabla g(\mathbf f_k)$: step uphill, re-measure the slope, repeat. For the quadratic model (7.3), whose Hessian is $-\Sigma$, let $m$ and $M$ be the smallest and largest curvatures $\mathbf u^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf u$ over unit directions: the roots of $x^2-(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)\,x+\det\Sigma=0$, the eigenvalues of $\Sigma$ ([[LM11]]). The best fixed step is $\alpha=2/(m+M)$, and it shrinks the distance to the optimum at least by the factor $(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)$ per step, where $\kappa=M/m$ is the **condition number** (stated without proof; see Nocedal and Wright 2006). For the full-sample $\Sigma$, $m=0.01785$ and $M=0.04079$, so $\kappa=2.29$, $\alpha=34.1$ and the factor is 0.39.

Near its optimum the exact growth function is more curved, with curvatures 0.0198 and 0.0493. The same step therefore contracts the distance by $\lvert1-34.1\times0.0493\rvert=0.68$ per step, and the growth shortfall by about $0.68^2=0.46$.

**Newton's method** maximizes the second-order Taylor model (7.2) of $g$ at the current point. Setting the model's gradient with respect to the step $\boldsymbol\Delta$ to zero gives the $2\times2$ linear system $H(\mathbf f_k)\boldsymbol\Delta=-\nabla g(\mathbf f_k)$, solved as in Section 6, and $\mathbf f_{k+1}=\mathbf f_k+\boldsymbol\Delta$: the two-variable version of [[LM4]]'s iteration. On a quadratic it lands on the optimum in one step. On the exact growth function, the first step from $\mathbf 0$ lands at $(5.5621,\,-0.5156)$, near the model optimum $(5.59,\,-0.52)$, because at $\mathbf 0$ the Taylor model is essentially (7.3). Two more steps reach $(5.3186,\,-0.2641)$.

```python
def grad_hess(f):
    """Exact gradient and Hessian of g:
    A*mean(X/(1+R_p)) and -A*mean(X X'/(1+R_p)^2)."""
    w = 1 / (1 + rd + X @ f)
    return A * (X * w[:, None]).mean(0), -A * (X.T * w**2) @ X / len(X)

f = np.zeros(2)
for k in range(1, 5):
    gr, H = grad_hess(f)
    f = f + np.linalg.solve(H, -gr)    # Newton step: H Delta = -grad g
    print(k, f.round(4), f"{g(f):.4%}")
# 1 [ 5.5621 -0.5156] 63.7851%
# 2 [ 5.3231 -0.2675] 64.0296%
# 3 [ 5.3186 -0.2641] 64.0296%
# 4 [ 5.3186 -0.2641] 64.0296%
```

Each series ends at its first shortfall below $10^{-10}$, drawn on the bottom edge: far smaller shortfalls approach the rounding error of the computed growth rate (about $10^{-16}$) and vary from machine to machine.

Newton needs two iterations to bring the shortfall below $10^{-6}$ and three to go below $10^{-10}$; gradient ascent needs 18 and 30 ([[ex:converge]]). Newton's shortfall falls from $2.4\times10^{-3}$ to $7.0\times10^{-7}$ and then below $10^{-10}$: the number of correct digits roughly doubles at each step. The price is the Hessian: $N^2$ second derivatives and a linear solve per step, cheap for two assets and expensive for two thousand. Gradient methods dominate large problems for that reason, though they struggle when $\kappa$ runs into the thousands.

### Constrained problems with `scipy.optimize.minimize`

For constraints, `scipy.optimize.minimize` with `method="SLSQP"` (sequential least-squares quadratic programming; Kraft 1988) handles bounds, equalities and inequalities. It solves a sequence of quadratic models, each a small KKT problem. Verify a multiplier the way (7.6) defines it: re-solve with the constraint relaxed and divide the change in the optimum by the change in the constraint. Recent SciPy versions also report the multipliers in the result object.

```python
from scipy.optimize import minimize

def kelly_capped(cap):
    """Maximize exact sample growth subject to f1 + f2 <= cap and f >= 0."""
    res = minimize(lambda f: -g(f), x0=[0.5, 0.5], method="SLSQP",
                   bounds=[(0, None), (0, None)],
                   constraints=[{"type": "ineq",
                                 "fun": lambda f: cap - f.sum()}])
    return res.x, -res.fun

f_cap, g_cap = kelly_capped(2.0)
print(f_cap.round(4), f"{g_cap:.4%}")    # [2. 0.] 38.8741%
nu_cap = (kelly_capped(2.01)[1] - kelly_capped(1.99)[1]) / 0.02
print(f"shadow price of the cap: {nu_cap:.4f}")    # 0.1461
```

The exact solution agrees with [[eg:cap]]: the cap and the no-short rule bind, and the cap's shadow price is 0.146 against the model's 0.147.

## Summary {-}

- Partial derivatives are sensitivities with the other inputs frozen; the total differential (7.1) adds them to first order. For 00679B in 2022 it missed a cross term of −3.24 points.
- The gradient points uphill, perpendicular to level curves; the Hessian measures curvature. The second-order expansion (7.2) explained nearly all of the rise in the 42/58 mix's variance, first-order terms 74%.
- The quadratic growth model (7.3) is accurate to about 1 bp unlevered. At full Kelly it overstates growth by 2.02 points: 0.35 from the dropped squared daily mean, 1.67 from higher-order terms.
- Interior optima have $\nabla h=\mathbf 0$; the $2\times2$ test (7.4) classifies them through $h_{xx}$ and $\det H$, and convexity makes a local minimum global.
- At a constrained optimum $\nabla h=\lambda\nabla\psi$ (7.5), and the multiplier is the shadow price $J'(b)=\lambda$ (7.6): a marginal cost, or the best Sharpe ratio for a volatility budget.
- The minimum-variance weight (7.7) needs no expected returns, and $\lambda^*=2\sigma_{\min}^2$. Fitted on 2017–2021 it predicted 9.22% volatility; 2022–2026 delivered 12.44%, mostly because the inputs changed. By (7.9), the 17-point weight error cost only 0.9 points.
- Kelly fractions (7.12) are the tangency portfolio (7.10) levered by $L^*$, with $g^*=r_f+\tfrac12\mathrm{SR}^{*2}$ from (7.11). Their estimates swing violently between windows: in-sample optima are not recommendations.
- The KKT conditions (7.13) add non-negative multipliers and complementary slackness. Enumerating binding sets solves small problems, Newton's method and SLSQP larger ones, and bumping a constraint checks its multiplier.

## Solutions {-}

1. **B** is correct. $h_x=2xy+3y^2$, so $h_{xy}=2x+6y=2+12=14$; $h_{yx}=2x+6y$ as well. A is $h_{xx}=2y=4$, and C is $h_x(1,2)=2xy+3y^2=16$, the first derivative rather than the mixed one.

2. Exactly, $0.95\times1.03-1=-2.15\%$. The linear approximation is $x+y=-2.00\%$ and the cross term $xy=-0.15\%$. With $h_y=1+x=0.95$, an extra 1 point of currency gain adds 0.95 points, giving $-2.15\%+0.95\%=-1.20\%$; exactly, $0.95\times1.04-1=-1.20\%$. The estimate is exact because $h$ is linear in $y$ for fixed $x$.

3. At $(0,0)$: $h=0$, $h_x=h_y=\tfrac12$, $h_{xx}=h_{yy}=-\tfrac14$ and $h_{xy}=\tfrac14$. By (7.2), $h\approx\tfrac12(x+y)-\tfrac18(x^2-2xy+y^2)=\tfrac12(x+y)-\tfrac18(x-y)^2$. At $(0.20,-0.10)$: $0.05-0.01125=3.875\%$, against the exact $\sqrt{1.08}-1=3.923\%$, an error of 4.8 bp from third- and higher-order terms. The two returns have mean $\tfrac12(x+y)$ and population standard deviation $s=\lvert x-y\rvert/2=15\%$, so the quadratic term $-\tfrac18(x-y)^2=-s^2/2$ is exactly the volatility drag of [[LM3]] (3.9).

4. $h_x=3x^2-3=0$ gives $x=\pm1$; $h_y=3y^2-12=0$ gives $y=\pm2$. With $h_{xx}=6x$, $h_{yy}=6y$ and $h_{xy}=0$, $\det H=36xy$. At $(1,2)$: $\det H=72>0$, $h_{xx}=6>0$, a local minimum with $h=2$. At $(-1,-2)$: $\det H=72>0$, $h_{xx}=-6<0$, a local maximum with $h=38$. At $(1,-2)$ and $(-1,2)$: $\det H=-72<0$, saddles, with $h=34$ and $h=6$.

5. **A** is correct. By (7.6), the minimum variance rises by about $\lambda^*\Delta b=0.02\times0.05=0.0010$. Since $\lambda^*=2\sigma_{\min}^2$, $\sigma_{\min}^2=0.01$, and exactly $(1.05^2-1)\times0.01=0.001025$; the difference is second order. B takes $\lambda^*$ for $\sigma_{\min}^2$; C drops the 2 in $\lambda^*=2\sigma_{\min}^2$.

6. **C** is correct. The numerator of (7.7) for asset 1 is negative when $\rho>\sigma_2/\sigma_1=0.5$. Only 0.60 exceeds the threshold.

7. $\sigma_{12}=0.25\times0.20\times0.08=0.004$. The numerators of (7.10) are $\sigma_2^2e_1-\sigma_{12}e_2=0.000384-0.00008=0.000304$ and $\sigma_1^2e_2-\sigma_{12}e_1=0.0008-0.00024=0.00056$, which sum to 0.000864. So $\mathbf w^{\text{tan}}=(35.2\%,\,64.8\%)$. With $\mathrm{SR}_1=0.30$ and $\mathrm{SR}_2=0.25$, (7.11) gives $\mathrm{SR}^{*2}=(0.09+0.0625-0.0375)/0.9375=0.1227$ and $\mathrm{SR}^*=0.350$, above both.

8. $\det\Sigma=0.04\times0.0064-0.004^2=0.00024$. From (7.12), $f_1^*=0.000304/0.00024=1.267$ and $f_2^*=0.00056/0.00024=2.333$, so $L^*=3.6$. Then $L^*\mathbf w^{\text{tan}}=3.6\times(0.3519,\,0.6481)=(1.267,\,2.333)$, as required. The model growth is $g^*=0.02+\tfrac12(0.1227)=8.13\%$.

9. ## Glossary {-}

| Term | 中文 | Meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Covariance | 共變異數 | Average product of two returns' deviations from their means. |
| Mixed partial derivative | 混合偏導數 | $h_{xy}$; equal to $h_{yx}$ under continuity. |
| Gradient | 梯度 | Vector of partial derivatives; direction of steepest ascent. |
| Hessian | 海森矩陣 | Matrix of second partial derivatives; curvature in every direction. |
| Positive definite | 正定 | $\boldsymbol\Delta^{\top}H\boldsymbol\Delta>0$ for every $\boldsymbol\Delta\neq\mathbf 0$. |
| Convex function | 凸函數 | Positive semidefinite Hessian everywhere; local minima are global. |
| Lagrangian | 拉格朗日函數 | $\mathcal L=h-\lambda(\psi-b)$; its critical points give constrained optima. |
| Shadow price | 影子價格 | Rate at which the optimal value improves as a constraint is relaxed, (7.6). |
| Envelope theorem | 包絡定理 | An optimal value's derivative in a parameter equals the Lagrangian's partial derivative there. |
| KKT conditions | KKT 條件 | Stationarity, feasibility, non-negative multipliers, complementary slackness, (7.13). |
| Binding (active) constraint | 緊限制式（有效限制） | Inequality that holds with equality at the optimum. |
| Minimum-variance portfolio | 最小變異數投資組合 | Fully invested mix with the lowest variance, (7.7). |
| Tangency portfolio | 切點投資組合 | Fully invested mix with the highest Sharpe ratio, (7.10). |
| Kelly fractions | 凱利比例 | Growth-maximizing exposures, (7.12). |
| In-sample / out-of-sample | 樣本內／樣本外 | Data used to estimate a rule versus data used to test it. |
