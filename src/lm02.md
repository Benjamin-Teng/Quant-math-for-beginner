## Introduction

A derivative answers one question: when an input moves a little, how much does an output move, per unit of input? Traders answer it daily without naming it. One TX contract gains NT\$200 per index point; a bond with modified duration 9 loses about 9% per percentage point of yield, to first order.

This module builds the mathematics behind such numbers — limits, derivatives and elasticities — and shows where the linear picture breaks.

> [!case] A 1% day that becomes a 7.6% day
> On 11 June 2025 TAIEX closed at 22,470.10, up 1.025%. The June TAIEX futures contract (TX) settled at 22,344, up 228 points. TX is marked to market at NT\$200 per index point, so each long contract was credited NT\$45,600 that evening. It was an ordinary session: 56 of the 145 sessions from 2 April to 31 October 2025 moved more.
>
> Two traders were each long one contract at the previous day's settlement. Trader A had NT\$600,000 of account equity and earned **+7.60%** on the day. Trader B had NT\$900,000 and earned **+5.07%**. Both accounts exceeded an initial margin of NT\$350,000 per contract. That margin is an *illustrative assumption*: TAIFEX resets margins often, and Section 6 quotes a dated official figure.
>
> Two months earlier the same position had been a disaster. On 7 April 2025, the largest daily fall of the sample, TX settled at its −10% daily limit. Trader A lost **71%** of equity in one session and Trader B lost **47%**.
>
> Why is the multiple 7.4 for one trader and 4.9 for the other? Why does it not stay fixed? And why did a 19% fall in the futures price over three sessions lift B's leverage from 4.7 to 44?
>
> Each answer is one derivative. Section 6 resolves the case, and Section 7 explains why the linear answer failed in April.

## Limits and Continuity {lo=1}

### Where a function is heading

The slope of a chord of $e^x$ from $0$ to $x$ is $(e^x-1)/x$. At $x=0$ the expression is $0/0$ and means nothing. For $x=0.01$ it is 1.005017, and for $x=-0.01$ it is 0.995017: the values head toward 1 from both sides. A limit makes "heads toward" precise, and it does not care what happens *at* the point.

> [!definition] Limit
> $\lim_{x\to x_0}h(x)=\ell$ means that $h(x)$ is as close to $\ell$ as we like whenever $x$ is close enough to $x_0$, with $x\ne x_0$. Precisely: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a $\delta>0$ such that $0<|x-x_0|<\delta$ implies $|h(x)-\ell|<\varepsilon$. The **one-sided limits** $\lim_{x\to x_0^-}$ and $\lim_{x\to x_0^+}$ let $x$ approach from one side only; the limit exists exactly when both exist and agree. $\lim_{x\to\infty}h(x)=\ell$ means that for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a number $x_\varepsilon$ such that $x>x_\varepsilon$ implies $|h(x)-\ell|<\varepsilon$.

Limits combine as expected: if $\lim h=\ell_1$ and $\lim u=\ell_2$, then $\lim(h\pm u)=\ell_1\pm\ell_2$, $\lim hu=\ell_1\ell_2$, and $\lim h/u=\ell_1/\ell_2$ when $\ell_2\ne0$. These **limit laws** are stated without proof (Stewart, chapter 2). One more tool does the heavy lifting below.

> [!theorem] Squeeze theorem
> If $h_1(x)\le h(x)\le h_2(x)$ for all $x\ne x_0$ near $x_0$, and $\lim_{x\to x_0}h_1(x)=\lim_{x\to x_0}h_2(x)=\ell$, then $\lim_{x\to x_0}h(x)=\ell$.

> [!deepdive]- The ε–δ definition at work
> **A limit from the definition.** To show $\lim_{x\to2}(3x-1)=5$, let $\varepsilon>0$ and choose $\delta=\varepsilon/3$. Then $0<|x-2|<\delta$ gives $|(3x-1)-5|=3|x-2|<3\delta=\varepsilon$. The proof is a recipe: given a tolerance $\varepsilon$ on the output, produce a tolerance $\delta$ on the input.
>
> **Proof of the squeeze theorem.** Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $\delta$ so small that $0<|x-x_0|<\delta$ implies $h_1(x)>\ell-\varepsilon$ and $h_2(x)<\ell+\varepsilon$; both are possible because $h_1$ and $h_2$ tend to $\ell$. On that set $\ell-\varepsilon<h_1(x)\le h(x)\le h_2(x)<\ell+\varepsilon$, so $|h(x)-\ell|<\varepsilon$. $\blacksquare$

### Continuity

> [!definition] Continuity
> $h$ is **continuous at $x_0$** if $h(x_0)$ is defined, $\lim_{x\to x_0}h(x)$ exists, and the two are equal. $h$ is continuous on an interval if it is continuous at each of its points.

Polynomials, $e^x$ and $\ln x$ (for $x>0$) are continuous. So are sums, products, quotients and compositions of continuous functions, wherever they are defined. Continuity also lets a limit pass inside a function: if $\lim_{x\to x_0}u(x)=\ell$ and $h$ is continuous at $\ell$, then $\lim_{x\to x_0}h(u(x))=h(\ell)$. All three facts are stated without proof (Stewart, chapter 2).

A function can fail to be continuous in three ways, and each has a financial example.

- **Removable discontinuity.** $(e^x-1)/x$ is undefined at 0, but its limit exists (it is 1, as shown below). Defining the value at 0 as 1 repairs it.
- **Jump.** A digital option pays 1 if the index ends above the strike $K$ and 0 otherwise. Its payoff jumps at $K$, where the left and right limits are 0 and 1.
- **Infinite discontinuity.** The leverage $f=NmF/E$ of a futures account grows without bound as equity approaches zero. For Trader A on 11 June, equity is zero at $F=22{,}116-600{,}000/200=19{,}116$, and $f\to+\infty$ as $F$ falls toward that level.

Continuity is weaker than smoothness. The call payoff $\max(S-K,0)$ is continuous at the strike but has a corner there; Section 3 shows that it has no derivative at $K$.

### Two limits that carry the module

By (1.6) of [[LM1]], $e^x=\lim_{n\to\infty}(1+x/n)^n$ for every real $x$. Everything we need about the slope of $e^x$ then follows from **Bernoulli's inequality**: for every integer $n\ge1$ and every $x\ge-1$,
$$(1+x)^n\ge1+nx .$$
The proof is by induction. It holds with equality for $n=1$. If it holds for $n$, then, because $1+x\ge0$, $(1+x)^{n+1}\ge(1+nx)(1+x)=1+(n+1)x+nx^2\ge1+(n+1)x$.

Replace $x$ by $x/n$ with $n\ge|x|$: $(1+x/n)^n\ge1+x$. Letting $n\to\infty$ preserves the inequality, so $e^x\ge1+x$ for every real $x$. Applied to $-x$ it gives $e^{-x}\ge1-x$, which is positive when $x<1$. Since $e^xe^{-x}=1$ by the laws of exponents ([[LM1]]),
$$1+x\;\le\;e^x\;\le\;\frac{1}{1-x}\qquad(x<1). \tag{2.1}$$

Subtract 1 and divide by $x$. For $0<x<1$ this gives $1\le(e^x-1)/x\le1/(1-x)$. For $x<0$ the division flips both inequalities: $1/(1-x)\le(e^x-1)/x\le1$. In both cases the bounds tend to 1 ([[ex:elimit]] shows them closing in), so the squeeze theorem yields
$$\lim_{x\to0}\frac{e^x-1}{x}=1. \tag{2.2}$$

A second limit follows by substitution. Let $z=\ln(1+x)$, so that $x=e^z-1$. As $x\to0$, $z\to0$ because $\ln$ is continuous at 1, and $z\ne0$ whenever $x\ne0$. Then $\ln(1+x)/x=z/(e^z-1)=1\big/\big[(e^z-1)/z\big]$, and the quotient law with (2.2) gives
$$\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\ln(1+x)}{x}=1. \tag{2.3}$$

> | +0.100 | 1.051709 | 1 | 1.111111 | 0.953102 |

In financial language, (2.2) says that a continuously compounded rate $x$ produces a simple return $e^x-1$ whose ratio to $x$ tends to 1. Equation (2.3) says the same of a log return and a simple return. Both are "≈" statements about small moves; [[LM3]] measures their error.

### Limits at infinity

Limits at infinity describe long horizons, such as the value of an income stream that never stops.

> [!example] From annuity to perpetuity {#annuity}
> An annuity pays 1 at the end of each of $n$ years. At the periodic rate $i=2\%$ its present value is the annuity factor $a_{n,i}=\big(1-(1+i)^{-n}\big)/i$, which [[LM6]] derives. Compute $a_{n,i}$ for $n=10$, 30, 100 and 1,000, and find its limit as $n\to\infty$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The values are 8.9826, 22.3965, 43.0984 and 50.0000. Because $0<1/1.02<1$, the term $1.02^{-n}$ tends to 0, and the limit laws give $\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n,i}=1/0.02=50$: the value of a **perpetuity**.
> >
> > The gap $50-a_{n,i}=50\times1.02^{-n}$ shrinks by the same factor $1/1.02$ every year, so convergence is steady but slow. After 100 years, $50-43.0984=6.9016$, or 13.80% of the perpetuity's value, is still missing. The 10-year factor, 8.9826, will reappear in Section 5 as the duration of a 10-year bond.

> [!pitfall] Market data have no limits
> Prices move in ticks (one TX tick is one index point, NT\$200), print at discrete times, and gap. On 7 April 2025 TX settled at its −10% daily limit, 2,129 points below a settlement struck five calendar days earlier. Limits and derivatives belong to a *model* — a pricing formula, a fitted curve, an account-value function — not to a raw price series. "The derivative of the price path" is a modelling choice and should be stated as one.

> [!zh] 極限：看「趨近」，不看「等於」
> 極限描述的是函數「往哪裡去」，不在乎那一點本身有沒有定義。$(e^x-1)/x$ 在 $x=0$ 是 $0/0$，但從兩邊都被夾到 1（夾擠定理 squeeze theorem），這就是「連續複利 $x$ 很小時，簡單報酬 $e^x-1\approx x$」的嚴格版本。不連續有三種：可移除（$(e^x-1)/x$ 在 0）、跳躍（二元選擇權在履約價）、無窮（權益趨近 0 時的槓桿）；買權在履約價連續但不可微。記住：市場資料是一格一格跳的，極限與導數是「模型」的性質，不是報價本身的性質。

> [!kc]
> Use (2.2) to evaluate $\lim_{\Delta t\to0}\big(e^{0.05\Delta t}-1\big)/\Delta t$. What does the answer say about a 5% continuously compounded rate over a short period of $\Delta t$ years?
>
> > [!answer]-

## The Derivative {lo=2}

### From average to instantaneous rate

The average rate of change of $h$ between $x_0$ and $x_0+\Delta x$ is the **difference quotient** $[h(x_0+\Delta x)-h(x_0)]/\Delta x$: the slope of the secant through the two points. As $\Delta x$ shrinks, the secant pivots about $(x_0,h(x_0))$. If it settles on a limiting line, that line is the tangent and its slope is the derivative.

> [!definition] Derivative
> $h$ is **differentiable at $x_0$** if the finite limit
> $$h'(x_0)=\lim_{\Delta x\to0}\frac{h(x_0+\Delta x)-h(x_0)}{\Delta x} \tag{2.4}$$
> exists. $h'(x_0)$ is the **derivative** of $h$ at $x_0$, also written $\frac{dh}{dx}\big|_{x=x_0}$. If $h$ is differentiable at every point of an interval, $x\mapsto h'(x)$ is a new function; differentiating it again gives the second derivative $h''$.

A derivative carries units: output units per input unit. That is why one number answers three questions.

- **Slope** (geometry): the steepness of the tangent line.
- **Marginal rate** (economics): the extra output from one more unit of input, such as the extra interest from lengthening a loan.
- **Sensitivity** (risk): the change in a value per unit change in a risk factor. A TX contract has a sensitivity of NT\$200 per index point.

Two derivatives from the definition show the mechanics. For $h(x)=x^2$, $[(x+\Delta x)^2-x^2]/\Delta x=2x+\Delta x\to2x$. For $h(x)=1/x$ with $x\ne0$, $[1/(x+\Delta x)-1/x]/\Delta x=-1/[x(x+\Delta x)]\to-1/x^2$.

> [!example] A derivative from the definition: the one-year zero {#zero}
> A one-year zero-coupon bond pays 100 at maturity, so its price at annual yield $y$ is $P(y)=100/(1+y)$. Find $P'(y)$ from (2.4) and evaluate it at $y=2\%$. How much does the price fall per basis point for NT\$100 million of face value?
>
> > [!solution]
> > The difference quotient is
> > $$\frac{1}{\Delta y}\Big[\frac{100}{1+y+\Delta y}-\frac{100}{1+y}\Big]=\frac{100\,[(1+y)-(1+y+\Delta y)]}{\Delta y\,(1+y)(1+y+\Delta y)}=\frac{-100}{(1+y)(1+y+\Delta y)} .$$
> > As $\Delta y\to0$ it tends to $P'(y)=-100/(1+y)^2$. At $y=2\%$, $P'(0.02)=-96.1169$ per 100 face per unit of yield.
> >
> > A basis point is $0.0001$ units of yield, so the price falls by $0.009612$ per 100 face per bp. For NT\$100 million of face, that is NT\$9,612 per bp. This number — the price fall for a one-basis-point rise in yield — is called the **DV01**.

The figure below lets you watch the secant become the tangent. Its opening view uses $h(x)=e^x$ at $x_0=0$ with a forward step $\Delta x=0.5$: the secant slope is 1.29744 while the tangent slope is 1. Shrink $\Delta x$ and the error falls in proportion to $\Delta x$; switch to central differences and it falls in proportion to $\Delta x^2$.

### Differentiable implies continuous

If $h$ is differentiable at $x_0$, then $h(x_0+\Delta x)-h(x_0)=\Delta x\cdot\frac{h(x_0+\Delta x)-h(x_0)}{\Delta x}\to0\cdot h'(x_0)=0$, so $h$ is continuous at $x_0$. The converse fails.

The **one-sided derivatives** $h'_\pm(x_0)=\lim_{\Delta x\to0^\pm}[h(x_0+\Delta x)-h(x_0)]/\Delta x$ can exist and differ. For the call payoff $\max(S-K,0)$ the right derivative at $K$ is 1 and the left derivative is 0. The payoff therefore has no derivative at $K$. The digital payoff is not even continuous at $K$, so it cannot be differentiable there.

### The mean value theorem

> [!theorem] Mean value theorem
> If $h$ is continuous on $[x_1,x_2]$ and differentiable on $(x_1,x_2)$, there is a point $\xi\in(x_1,x_2)$ with
> $$h'(\xi)=\frac{h(x_2)-h(x_1)}{x_2-x_1}.$$

Somewhere, the instantaneous rate equals the average rate. When the bond's yield rises from 2% to 3%, its price falls from 100 to 91.469797, an average slope of −853.0203 per unit of yield. The theorem guarantees a yield between 2% and 3% at which $P'$ takes exactly that value.

Two consequences are used repeatedly. If $h'>0$ on an interval, $h$ is increasing there. If $|h'|\le M$ on $[x_1,x_2]$, then $|h(x_2)-h(x_1)|\le M\,|x_2-x_1|$, which is how Sections 7 and 8 bound errors.

> [!deepdive]- Proof of the mean value theorem
> **Rolle's theorem.** Suppose also that $h(x_1)=h(x_2)$. A continuous function on a closed interval attains a maximum and a minimum (the extreme value theorem, stated without proof).
>
> If both occur at the endpoints, $h$ is constant and $h'=0$ everywhere. Otherwise an extremum, say a maximum, occurs at an interior point $\xi$. There $[h(\xi+\Delta x)-h(\xi)]/\Delta x\le0$ for $\Delta x>0$ and $\ge0$ for $\Delta x<0$, so the limit $h'(\xi)$ is both $\le0$ and $\ge0$: $h'(\xi)=0$.
>
> **The general case.** Apply Rolle's theorem to $H(x)=h(x)-h(x_1)-\frac{h(x_2)-h(x_1)}{x_2-x_1}(x-x_1)$, which satisfies $H(x_1)=H(x_2)=0$. Some $\xi\in(x_1,x_2)$ has $H'(\xi)=h'(\xi)-\frac{h(x_2)-h(x_1)}{x_2-x_1}=0$. $\blacksquare$

> [!pitfall] Per unit, per percent, per basis point
> $P'(2\%)=-898.2585$ is a price change per *unit* of yield, that is, per 100 percentage points. Per percentage point it is −8.98; per basis point it is −0.0898. Futures delta has the same trap: NT\$200 per index point for one TX contract, but NT\$44,688 per 1% move at $F=22{,}344$. Most sensitivity errors in practice are unit errors, so write the units next to every derivative.

> [!kc]
> Use the definition (2.4) to find the derivative of $h(x)=x^3$ at $x=2$.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > $(2+\Delta x)^3-8=12\Delta x+6\Delta x^2+\Delta x^3$, so the difference quotient is $12+6\Delta x+\Delta x^2$, which tends to 12. Hence $h'(2)=12$, in agreement with the power rule of Section 4.

## Differentiation Rules {lo=3}

Six rules, each proved once from (2.4), differentiate every function built from powers, exponentials and logarithms by sums, products, quotients and compositions.

### Linearity and integer powers

From the limit laws, $(\kappa h)'=\kappa h'$ for any constant $\kappa$, and $(h+u)'=h'+u'$: differentiation is **linear**. For a positive integer $j$, the binomial theorem gives $(x+\Delta x)^j=x^j+j\,x^{j-1}\Delta x+\Delta x^2\times(\text{a polynomial in }x\text{ and }\Delta x)$. Dividing by $\Delta x$ and letting $\Delta x\to0$ leaves $\frac{d}{dx}x^j=j\,x^{j-1}$.

### The exponential and the logarithm

> [!proposition] Derivatives of $e^{\kappa x}$ and $\ln x$
> For any constant $\kappa$,
> $$\frac{d}{dx}e^{\kappa x}=\kappa\,e^{\kappa x} \quad\text{for all }x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\ln x=\frac1x \quad\text{for }x>0.$$

*Proof.* For $\kappa\ne0$, the law of exponents gives $[e^{\kappa(x+\Delta x)}-e^{\kappa x}]/\Delta x=\kappa\,e^{\kappa x}\,(e^{\kappa\Delta x}-1)/(\kappa\Delta x)$, which tends to $\kappa e^{\kappa x}$ by (2.2) with $\kappa\Delta x$ in place of $x$. For $\kappa=0$ both sides are 0.

For the logarithm, $\ln(x+\Delta x)-\ln x=\ln(1+\Delta x/x)$. With $z=\Delta x/x$,
$$\frac{\ln(x+\Delta x)-\ln x}{\Delta x}=\frac1x\cdot\frac{\ln(1+z)}{z}\;\longrightarrow\;\frac1x\cdot1$$
by (2.3), since $z\to0$ as $\Delta x\to0$. $\blacksquare$

With $\kappa=1$, the exponential is its own rate of change. Wealth $W(t)=W_0e^{gt}$ grows at $W'(t)=g\,W(t)$: a constant *proportional* rate $g$, the log growth rate of [[LM1]]. Its logarithm, $\ln W(t)=\ln W_0+g\,t$, is a straight line with slope $g$.

### Products and quotients

> [!proposition] Product and quotient rules
> If $u$ and $h$ are differentiable at $x$, then $(uh)'=u'h+uh'$, and, where $h(x)\ne0$, $\big(u/h\big)'=\big(u'h-uh'\big)/h^2$.

*Proof.* Add and subtract $u(x)h(x+\Delta x)$:
$$\frac{u(x+\Delta x)h(x+\Delta x)-u(x)h(x)}{\Delta x}=\frac{u(x+\Delta x)-u(x)}{\Delta x}\,h(x+\Delta x)+u(x)\,\frac{h(x+\Delta x)-h(x)}{\Delta x}.$$
Because $h$ is differentiable, it is continuous, so $h(x+\Delta x)\to h(x)$ and the right side tends to $u'h+uh'$. For the quotient, first
$$\frac{1}{\Delta x}\Big[\frac{1}{h(x+\Delta x)}-\frac{1}{h(x)}\Big]=-\frac{h(x+\Delta x)-h(x)}{\Delta x}\cdot\frac{1}{h(x+\Delta x)\,h(x)}\;\longrightarrow\;-\frac{h'}{h^2},$$
and then the product rule applied to $u\cdot(1/h)$ gives $u'/h-uh'/h^2=(u'h-uh')/h^2$. $\blacksquare$

### The chain rule

Most pricing functions are nested: a bond price depends on discount factors, which depend on the yield. The chain rule differentiates compositions.

> [!theorem] Chain rule
> If $u$ is differentiable at $x$ and $h$ is differentiable at $u(x)$, then $h(u(x))$ is differentiable at $x$ and
> $$\frac{d}{dx}h(u(x))=h'\big(u(x)\big)\,u'(x),\qquad\text{or}\qquad \frac{dh}{dx}=\frac{dh}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}. \tag{2.5}$$

The intuitive argument writes $\Delta h/\Delta x=(\Delta h/\Delta u)(\Delta u/\Delta x)$ and lets $\Delta x\to0$. It is correct whenever $\Delta u\ne0$ for small $\Delta x$, which can fail if $u$ oscillates. The Deep Dive gives a proof without that gap.

> [!deepdive]- A proof of the chain rule without dividing by zero
> **Lemma (Carathéodory).** $h$ is differentiable at $x_0$ if and only if $h(x)-h(x_0)=\bar h(x)\,(x-x_0)$ near $x_0$ for some function $\bar h$ continuous at $x_0$. Then $\bar h(x_0)=h'(x_0)$. If $h$ is differentiable, set $\bar h(x)=[h(x)-h(x_0)]/(x-x_0)$ for $x\ne x_0$ and $\bar h(x_0)=h'(x_0)$; continuity of $\bar h$ at $x_0$ is the definition (2.4). Conversely, dividing by $x-x_0$ and letting $x\to x_0$ gives $h'(x_0)=\bar h(x_0)$.
>
> **Proof of (2.5).** Let $z_0=u(x_0)$. Write $u(x)-u(x_0)=\bar u(x)(x-x_0)$ and $h(z)-h(z_0)=\bar h(z)(z-z_0)$, with $\bar u$ continuous at $x_0$ and $\bar h$ continuous at $z_0$. Then
> $$h(u(x))-h(u(x_0))=\bar h(u(x))\,\big(u(x)-u(x_0)\big)=\bar h(u(x))\,\bar u(x)\,(x-x_0).$$
> The factor $\bar h(u(x))\,\bar u(x)$ is continuous at $x_0$, because $u$ is continuous at $x_0$, and its value there is $\bar h(z_0)\bar u(x_0)=h'(u(x_0))\,u'(x_0)$. By the lemma, $h(u(x))$ is differentiable at $x_0$ with that derivative. $\blacksquare$

The chain rule completes the toolkit.

- **Real powers.** For $x>0$, $x^\alpha=e^{\alpha\ln x}$, so $\frac{d}{dx}x^\alpha=e^{\alpha\ln x}\cdot\alpha/x=\alpha\,x^{\alpha-1}$ for any real $\alpha$.
- **Other bases.** For a base $\beta>0$, $\beta^x=e^{x\ln\beta}$, so $\frac{d}{dx}\beta^x=\beta^x\ln\beta$.
- **Discount factors.** $\frac{d}{dy}(1+y)^{-t}=-t\,(1+y)^{-t-1}$ and $\frac{d}{dr}e^{-rt}=-t\,e^{-rt}$.
- **Logarithmic derivative.** For $h>0$, $\frac{d}{dx}\ln h(x)=h'(x)/h(x)$: the *relative* rate of change, the basis of elasticities in Section 6.

> [!example] Forward rates from a yield curve {#forward}
> A continuously compounded zero-coupon yield curve is $y(T)=2.0\%-0.8\%\,e^{-T/3}$ for maturity $T$ in years (an illustrative curve, not market data). The discount factor is $P(T)=e^{-y(T)\,T}$. The instantaneous forward rate $r(T):=-\frac{d}{dT}\ln P(T)$ is the marginal rate earned by extending a deposit's maturity beyond $T$. Find $r(T)$ and evaluate it at $T=1$, 5 and 10.
>
> > [!solution]
> > Since $\ln P(T)=-y(T)\,T$, the product rule gives $r(T)=y(T)+T\,y'(T)$. The derivative of $e^{-T/3}$ is $-\tfrac13e^{-T/3}$, so $y'(T)=\tfrac{0.8\%}{3}e^{-T/3}$.
> >
> > | $T$ | $y(T)$ | $y'(T)$ per year | $r(T)=y+Ty'$ |
> > |--:|--:|--:|--:|
> > | 1 | 1.42677% | 0.191075% | 1.61785% |
> > | 5 | 1.84890% | 0.050367% | 2.10073% |
> > | 10 | 1.97146% | 0.009513% | 2.06659% |
> >
> > The forward rate exceeds the yield wherever the curve slopes up. The yield is an *average* rate over $[0,T]$; the forward rate is the *marginal* rate at $T$. Whenever a total equals $T$ times an average, $\text{marginal}=\text{average}+T\times\text{slope of the average}$.

> [!example] How leverage changes with the futures price {#levfun}
> An account holds $N$ contracts with multiplier $m$, opened at $F_0$ with equity $E_0$. Its equity is $E(F)=E_0+Nm(F-F_0)$ and its leverage, exposure ÷ equity, is $f(F)=NmF/E(F)$. Use the quotient rule to find $f'(F)$. Evaluate it for Trader A on 10 June 2025 ($N=1$, $m=200$, $F_0=22{,}116$, $E_0=$ NT\$600,000).
>
> > [!solution]
> > The quotient rule gives
> > $$f'(F)=\frac{Nm\,E-NmF\cdot Nm}{E^2}=\frac{Nm\,(E-NmF)}{E^2}=\frac{f}{F}\,(1-f).$$
> > For Trader A, $f=4{,}423{,}200/600{,}000=7.3720$, so $f'(F_0)=-7.3720\times6.3720/22{,}116=-0.0021240$ per index point. A 100-point rise should cut leverage by about 0.2124. Exact repricing gives $f(22{,}216)=4{,}443{,}200/620{,}000=7.1665$, a fall of 0.2055.
> >
> > For $f>1$ the derivative is negative: a fixed long position deleverages as the market rises and leverages up as it falls. Section 6 turns this into a one-line elasticity, and Section 7 shows how it misbehaves in a crash.

> [!zh] 連鎖律：敏感度相乘
> 很多定價函數是「一層套一層」：債券價格 → 折現因子 → 殖利率；台幣價值 → 美元價值 × 匯率。連鎖律說：外層對內層的敏感度，乘上內層對變數的敏感度，就是整體的敏感度。乘法法則與除法法則看起來是代數，其實是「邊際 = 平均 + 期限 × 平均的斜率」（遠期利率）和「槓桿隨價格變動」這類金融結論的來源。後面的 Greeks、存續期間與 [[LM37]] 的 Itô 引理，全部建立在連鎖律之上。

> [!kc]
> Differentiate $\ln(1+fR)$ with respect to $f$, treating the return $R$ as a constant. What is the derivative at $f=0$?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > By the chain rule, $\frac{d}{df}\ln(1+fR)=R/(1+fR)$, which equals $R$ at $f=0$. Averaged over the possible returns, this derivative is $g'(f)$, the slope of the growth curve $g(f)$ of [[LM4]]; its zero is the Kelly fraction $f^*$.

## Derivatives as Sensitivities {lo=4}

In risk management a derivative is a **sensitivity**: the change in a value per unit change in one risk factor, other factors held fixed. This section computes three that desks quote daily: futures delta, bond duration and the rate sensitivity of a present value.

### Delta of a futures position

An account holding $N$ contracts opened at $F_0$ with equity $E_0$ has equity $E(F)=E_0+Nm\,(F-F_0)$ after marking to market. Its derivative is the position's **delta**:
$$E'(F)=Nm\quad\text{NT\$ per index point}.$$
The sensitivity is constant because $E$ is linear in $F$. By linearity of the derivative, the delta of several positions is the sum of their deltas. [[ex:contracts]] lists the three TAIEX futures contracts.

> [!exhibit] TAIEX futures contracts: multiplier and delta {#contracts}
> | Contract | Code | Multiplier (NT\$ per index point) | Tick | Delta of one long contract |
> |:--|:--|--:|:--|--:|
> | TAIEX Futures | TX | 200 | 1 point = NT\$200 | NT\$200 per point |
> | Mini-TAIEX Futures | MTX | 50 | 1 point = NT\$50 | NT\$50 per point |
> | Micro TAIEX Futures | TMF | 10 | 1 point = NT\$10 | NT\$10 per point |
>
> Source: TAIFEX contract specifications for TX, MTX and TMF (taifex.com.tw, retrieved 10 October 2026).

Delta per index point converts to delta per 1% move by multiplying by $F/100$. At $F=22{,}344$ a 1% move is 223.44 points, worth NT\$44,688 to one TX contract. The quantity $NmF$ is the position's **notional**, or exposure, and "NT\$ per 1%" is notional ÷ 100.

> [!example] Delta of a mixed position {#delta}
> A trader is long 2 TX, short 3 MTX and long 4 TMF, all in the same expiry month. Compute the position's delta, its profit on a 150-point rise, its delta per 1% move at $F=22{,}344$, and the TMF trade that makes it delta-neutral.
>
> > [!solution]
> > Delta is $2\times200-3\times50+4\times10=290$ NT\$ per point, the same exposure as 1.45 TX contracts. A 150-point rise earns $150\times290=$ NT\$43,500. Per 1% move, delta is $290\times223.44=$ NT\$64,798. Selling 29 TMF ($29\times10=290$) brings delta to zero. All of this is exact, because each contract's value is linear in $F$.

### Duration: the derivative of a bond price

A bond with annual cash flows $CF_t$ at $t=1,\dots,n$ is priced at yield $y$ as $P(y)=\sum_t CF_t\,(1+y)^{-t}$. By linearity and the discount-factor rule,
$$P'(y)=-\sum_{t=1}^{n}t\,CF_t\,(1+y)^{-t-1}=-\frac{1}{1+y}\sum_{t=1}^{n}t\,PV_t, \qquad PV_t:=CF_t\,(1+y)^{-t}.$$
Dividing by $-P$ defines the **modified duration** and reveals the **Macaulay duration** inside it:
$$D_{\text{mod}}:=-\frac{P'(y)}{P(y)}=\frac{D_{\text{Mac}}}{1+y}, \qquad D_{\text{Mac}}:=\sum_{t=1}^{n}t\,w_t,\quad w_t:=\frac{PV_t}{P}. \tag{2.6}$$
Macaulay duration is the present-value-weighted average time of the cash flows. It is not an extra definition: it is what the derivative of the price function produces.

As a sensitivity, (2.6) says $\Delta P/P\approx-D_{\text{mod}}\,\Delta y$, with an error of order $\Delta y^2$ (Section 7). The price fall for a one-basis-point rise in yield, $-P'(y)\times0.0001$, is the bond's **DV01**. [[LM3]] adds the second-order term, convexity.

> [!example] Duration of the 10-year bond as a derivative {#duration}
> The 10-year bond of [[ex:quotients]] pays a 2% annual coupon and yields 2%. Compute $P'(2\%)$, $D_{\text{mod}}$, $D_{\text{Mac}}$ and the DV01 for NT\$100 million of face. Then compare the first-order estimate of the price change with exact repricing for yield changes of ±25 bp.
>
> > [!solution]
> > With coupons of 2 and principal 100, $P(0.02)=100$ and $P'(0.02)=-\sum_{t=1}^{10}2t/1.02^{t+1}-1000/1.02^{11}=-898.2585$. So $D_{\text{mod}}=8.982585$ and $D_{\text{Mac}}=1.02\times8.982585=9.1622$ years; the final payment alone carries weight $w_{10}=0.8368$. The DV01 is $0.08983$ per 100 face, or NT\$89,826 per NT\$100 million.
> >
> > For $\Delta y=+25$ bp the first-order estimate is $-8.982585\times0.0025=-2.2456\%$; exact repricing gives −2.2166%. For −25 bp the estimate is +2.2456% and the exact change +2.2753%. The exact changes are asymmetric, while the linear estimate is symmetric and too pessimistic on both sides, by about 3 bp of price. That asymmetry is convexity, the subject of [[LM3]].
> >
> > The duration 8.9826 equals the 10-year annuity factor of [[eg:annuity]]. This is no coincidence: a bond at par has $D_{\text{mod}}=\big(1-(1+y)^{-n}\big)/y$ (Practice Problem 9).

### Rate sensitivity of a present value

For a single payment due in $T$ years, the sensitivity depends on the compounding convention. At a continuously compounded rate $r$, $PV=e^{-rT}$ per unit of payment and $dPV/dr=-T\cdot PV$: the relative sensitivity is exactly $T$. At an annually compounded rate $y$, $PV=(1+y)^{-T}$ and $dPV/dy=-\tfrac{T}{1+y}PV$. For NT\$1,000,000 due in 5 years at 2%, the sensitivities are −452.42 and −443.99 per bp, on present values of 904,837.42 and 905,730.81.

The same reasoning applies to the index futures fair value $F=S\,e^{(r_f-q)\tau}$, which [[LM6]] derives by no-arbitrage. Holding $r_f$, $q$ and $\tau$ fixed, $dF/dS=e^{(r_f-q)\tau}$, close to 1.

Holding $S$, $r_f$ and $\tau$ fixed, $dF/dq=-\tau F$. For a three-month contract ($\tau=0.25$) at $F=22{,}000$, a rise of 0.1 percentage point in the expected dividend yield lowers fair value by 5.50 points. A derivative that holds the other inputs fixed is a *partial* derivative, treated properly in [[LM7]].

> [!kc]
> A trader is long 3 MTX and short 1 TX. What is the position's delta, and what is its profit if the futures price falls 120 points?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > Delta is $3\times50-200=-50$ NT\$ per point: the position is net short. A 120-point fall earns $(-50)\times(-120)=$ NT\$6,000.

## Elasticity and Leverage {lo=5}

### A unit-free derivative

A delta of NT\$200 per point cannot be compared with a duration of 9 years, and neither says what happens to an account in percent. Elasticity removes units by comparing *relative* changes.

> [!definition] Elasticity
> For a positive function $h$ of a positive variable $x$, the **elasticity** of $h$ with respect to $x$ is
> $$\eta_{h,x}:=\frac{dh/h}{dx/x}=\frac{x\,h'(x)}{h(x)}=\frac{d\ln h}{d\ln x}. \tag{2.7}$$
> It is the percentage change in $h$ per 1% change in $x$, to first order.

The last form follows from the chain rule. Put $z=\ln x$, so $x=e^z$ and $\ln h=\ln h(e^z)$. Then $\frac{d\ln h}{dz}=\frac{h'(e^z)\,e^z}{h(e^z)}=\frac{x\,h'(x)}{h(x)}$.

Because elasticities are derivatives of logarithms, the rules of Section 4 become rules of addition and multiplication. For positive functions $u$ and $h$:

- **power:** if $h$ is proportional to $x^\alpha$, then $\eta_{h,x}=\alpha$;
- **product and quotient:** $\eta_{uh}=\eta_u+\eta_h$ and $\eta_{u/h}=\eta_u-\eta_h$;
- **chain:** the elasticity of $h(u(x))$ with respect to $x$ is $\eta_{h,u}\cdot\eta_{u,x}$;
- **sum:** $\eta_{u+h}=\frac{u}{u+h}\eta_u+\frac{h}{u+h}\eta_h$, a value-weighted average.

The first three follow from $\ln(uh)=\ln u+\ln h$, $\ln x^\alpha=\alpha\ln x$ and (2.5). For the sum, $\eta_{u+h}=\frac{x(u'+h')}{u+h}=\frac{u}{u+h}\cdot\frac{xu'}{u}+\frac{h}{u+h}\cdot\frac{xh'}{h}$.

The sum rule is why portfolio sensitivities aggregate by value weights. A portfolio 60% in an index fund (elasticity 1) and 40% in cash (elasticity 0) has elasticity 0.6. [[ex:elastrules]] gives each rule a financial reading.

> [!exhibit] Elasticity rules and their financial readings {#elastrules}
> | Relation | Elasticity | Financial reading |
> |:--|:--|:--|
> | $h\propto x^{\alpha}$ | $\alpha$ | zero-coupon price $100(1+y)^{-T}$: elasticity $-T$ in $1+y$ |
> | $u\,h$ | $\eta_u+\eta_h$ | TWD value of a USD asset = USD value × exchange rate |
> | $u/h$ | $\eta_u-\eta_h$ | $f=NmF/E$ has elasticity $1-\eta_{E,F}$ |
> | $h(u(x))$ | $\eta_{h,u}\,\eta_{u,x}$ | account → futures → index |
> | $u+h$ | value-weighted average | portfolio sensitivity |
>
> Note: for a coupon bond, (2.6) gives $(1+y)P'(y)/P=-D_{\text{Mac}}$. Macaulay duration is minus the elasticity of price with respect to the gross yield $1+y$.

### The elasticity of a futures account is its leverage

An account holding $N$ contracts has equity $E(F)=E_0+Nm(F-F_0)$, so $E'(F)=Nm$. By (2.7),
$$\eta_{E,F}=\frac{F\cdot Nm}{E}=\frac{NmF}{E}=f. \tag{2.8}$$
The elasticity of equity with respect to the futures price is the notional divided by equity: the account's leverage. Because $E$ is linear in $F$, the one-day relation is exact, not approximate: $\Delta E/E=Nm\,\Delta F/E=f\cdot\Delta F/F$.

The index enters through the chain rule. With the cost-of-carry price $F=S\,e^{(r_f-q)\tau}$ and $r_f$, $q$, $\tau$ fixed, $\eta_{F,S}=S\,e^{(r_f-q)\tau}/F=1$. Therefore $\eta_{E,S}=\eta_{E,F}\,\eta_{F,S}=f$.

> [!result] Equity elasticity equals leverage
> For a futures account, $\eta_{E,F}=f=NmF/E$ exactly, and under cost of carry $\eta_{E,S}=f$. The same identity holds for any position financed with fixed debt. If a position worth $V$ is funded by equity $E$ and debt $V-E$, then $\eta_{E,V}=V/E=f$.

This covers margin purchases. Buy 1,000 shares of 0050 at an illustrative NT\$60 with NT\$24,000 of your own and NT\$36,000 borrowed: $f=60{,}000/24{,}000=2.50$, so a 4% fall removes 10% of equity.

> [!example] Resolving the motivating case {#case-solved}
> Use (2.8) and [[ex:case]] to explain both traders' returns on 11 June and on 7 April 2025. Then explain the ratio of each account's return to TAIEX's.
>
> > [!solution]
> > On 11 June the notional at the previous settlement was $200\times22{,}116=$ NT\$4,423,200. So $f=4{,}423{,}200/600{,}000=7.3720$ for A and $4{,}423{,}200/900{,}000=4.9147$ for B. The futures return was +1.0309%, so A earned $7.3720\times1.0309\%=7.600\%$ and B earned $4.9147\times1.0309\%=5.067\%$, exactly as observed.
> >
> > Against TAIEX's +1.0249%, the ratios are $7.600/1.0249=7.415$ and $5.067/1.0249=4.944$. They differ slightly from $f$ because the futures and the index moved by different percentages. The ratio of their returns, a measured $\eta_{F,S}$, was $1.0309/1.0249=1.0059$. On 7 April it was 1.0307, and the same arithmetic gives $7.0987\times(-9.9972\%)=-70.967\%$ for A and $4.7324\times(-9.9972\%)=-47.311\%$ for B.
> >
> > Over the 145 sessions from 2 April to 31 October 2025, a least-squares line through daily TX and TAIEX returns has slope 1.008 (correlation 0.978). Day by day the ratio scatters: on the 14 days when TAIEX moved between 0.9% and 1.1% in size, it ranged from 0.753 to 1.459.
> >
> > TAIEX closes on a 13:25–13:30 call auction; the TX settlement comes from the last minute of trading to 13:45. The basis also moves with dividend expectations. On average the measured $\eta_{F,S}$ is close to 1; on any one day it is noisy.
> >
> > The answer to "why 5–8%?" is therefore not the contract but the equity behind it: the multiple is notional ÷ equity. With the illustrative initial margin of NT\$350,000 as the only equity, $f$ on 11 June would have been 12.64.

The exchange's margins cap leverage. TAIFEX's table effective 12 August 2026 requires an initial margin of NT\$701,000 and a maintenance margin of NT\$538,000 per TX contract. The MTX and TMF figures, NT\$175,250 and NT\$35,050 initial, are exactly 1/4 and 1/20 of TX, in proportion to the multipliers.

TAIFEX recalculates margin levels after each regular session and may adjust them when the change reaches a set threshold. That is why this module uses an illustrative 2025 figure rather than a quoted one. Scaled by TAIFEX's ratio 538,000/701,000, the illustrative initial margin of NT\$350,000 corresponds to an illustrative maintenance margin of about NT\$268,600.

### Leverage moves: its elasticity is $1-f$

Leverage is a ratio, so its elasticity follows from the quotient rule for elasticities and (2.8):
$$\eta_{f,F}=\eta_{F,F}-\eta_{E,F}=1-f. \tag{2.9}$$
This is the elasticity form of [[eg:levfun]]. For $f>1$ it is negative.

A 1% rise in the futures price cuts Trader A's leverage of 7.3720 by about 6.4%, to a first-order 6.9023. The exact value is 6.9345. On 11 June itself, a 1.0309% rise took A from 7.3720 to 6.9219, against a linear prediction of 6.8877.

Over months the drift is large. [[ex:leverage]] follows both accounts from the close of 10 June to 31 October 2025, holding one near-month contract and rolling the day before each expiry. TAIEX rose 26.94% and the futures position gained 6,824 points, or NT\$1,364,800. A's leverage fell from 7.37 to 2.89 and B's from 4.91 to 2.51.

> [!pitfall] "My leverage is 7" is a statement about today
> Leverage, like any elasticity, is local. A fixed number of contracts deleverages in a rally and leverages up in a sell-off, at the rate $1-f$ of (2.9). A daily-reset fund with multiple $L$ does the opposite: it trades every day to hold $f=L$, which produces the volatility drag of [[LM3]]. Neither is "the" leverage, so a risk limit of "at most $f=5$" must say how often the position is rebalanced back to 5.

## Differentials and Linear Approximation {lo=6}

### The differential

A derivative turns into an approximation by running the definition backwards. If $\Delta x$ is small, $h(x_0+\Delta x)-h(x_0)$ is close to $h'(x_0)\,\Delta x$.

> [!definition] Differential and linear approximation
> If $h$ is differentiable at $x_0$, the **differential** $dh=h'(x_0)\,dx$ is the change along the tangent line for an input change $dx$. The **linear approximation** of $h$ at $x_0$ is $T_1(x):=h(x_0)+h'(x_0)\,(x-x_0)$, the first-order Taylor polynomial of [[LM3]].

By the definition of the derivative, the error $\operatorname{err}(x):=h(x)-T_1(x)$ satisfies $\operatorname{err}(x)/(x-x_0)\to0$: it vanishes faster than the step. When $h$ has a bounded second derivative, the mean value theorem makes this quantitative.

> [!result] Size of the linearization error
> If $h$ is twice differentiable between $x_0$ and $x$, with $|h''|\le M$ there, then
> $$\big|h(x)-T_1(x)\big|\le M\,(x-x_0)^2. \tag{2.10}$$
> The error of a linear approximation is $O\big((x-x_0)^2\big)$: halve the step and the bound falls by a factor of 4.

*Proof.* By the mean value theorem, $h(x)-h(x_0)=h'(\xi)(x-x_0)$ for some $\xi$ between $x_0$ and $x$. Hence $\operatorname{err}(x)=[h'(\xi)-h'(x_0)](x-x_0)$. Applying the theorem to $h'$ gives $h'(\xi)-h'(x_0)=h''(\zeta)(\xi-x_0)$, and $|\xi-x_0|\le|x-x_0|$. $\blacksquare$ [[LM3]] sharpens the constant from $M$ to $M/2$ and gives the error exactly.

Linearizing $e^x$ at 0 gives errors $e^x-(1+x)$ of 0.021403, 0.005171 and 0.001271 for $x=0.2$, 0.1 and 0.05. Each halving divides the error by about four (4.14, then 4.07).

### When the linear approximation fails

The bound (2.10) names the two enemies: a large step $|x-x_0|$ and a large curvature $M$. Four situations defeat a tangent line.

1. **Large moves.** The error grows like the square of the move. A tangent fitted at today's yield or price is a local tool.
2. **Singularities.** Near a point where $h$ blows up, $M$ is huge and no tangent follows the curve. Leverage near the zero-equity level is the example below.
3. **Kinks and jumps.** At a corner, such as a call payoff at the strike, no tangent exists. Across a jump, such as a gap or a limit-locked session, no continuous model applies.
4. **Stationary points.** If $h'(x_0)=0$, the tangent is flat and says nothing about the direction of change. The second derivative decides, which is the business of [[LM4]] (optima) and [[LM3]] (convexity).

April 2025 shows the second case in real data. Trader B held one April contract on NT\$900,000 at the close of 2 April, with $f=4.732$. Equity reaches zero at $F_{\mathrm z}=F_0-E_0/(Nm)=21{,}296-900{,}000/200=16{,}796$, where $f$ has a vertical asymptote. [[ex:crash]] compares B's exact leverage with the linear approximation $f(F_0)+f'(F_0)(F-F_0)$ built on 2 April from [[eg:levfun]].

## Numerical Differentiation {lo=7}

Many pricing functions are code with no usable derivative formula: a callable bond on a tree, a Monte Carlo option value, an interpolated curve. Practitioners then **bump and reprice**: they evaluate the function at nearby inputs and form a difference quotient. Doing this well requires knowing two errors that pull in opposite directions.

### Forward and central differences

For a step $\Delta x>0$, the **forward**, **backward** and **central** difference quotients are
$$\begin{gathered}Q_+(\Delta x)=\frac{h(x+\Delta x)-h(x)}{\Delta x},\qquad Q_-(\Delta x)=\frac{h(x)-h(x-\Delta x)}{\Delta x},\\ Q_c(\Delta x)=\frac{h(x+\Delta x)-h(x-\Delta x)}{2\Delta x}.\end{gathered} \tag{2.11}$$
The central quotient is the average of the other two.

**Truncation error of the forward quotient.** By the mean value theorem, $Q_+(\Delta x)=h'(\xi)$ for some $\xi\in(x,x+\Delta x)$. Applying the theorem again to $h'$, $Q_+(\Delta x)-h'(x)=h'(\xi)-h'(x)=h''(\zeta)\,(\xi-x)$. If $|h''|\le M_2$ near $x$, then $|Q_+(\Delta x)-h'(x)|\le M_2\,\Delta x$: the error is $O(\Delta x)$.

**Truncation error of the central quotient.** If $|h'''|\le M_3$ near $x$, then $|Q_c(\Delta x)-h'(x)|\le M_3\,\Delta x^2$: the error is $O(\Delta x^2)$. The odd symmetry of the central quotient cancels the first-order error term. The Deep Dive proves it with the mean value theorem alone.

> [!deepdive]- Why the central difference is second order
> Let $J(\theta)=h(x+\theta)-h(x-\theta)$, so $J(0)=0$ and $Q_c(\Delta x)=J(\Delta x)/(2\Delta x)$. By the mean value theorem, $J(\Delta x)=\Delta x\,J'(\theta)$ for some $\theta\in(0,\Delta x)$, and $J'(\theta)=h'(x+\theta)+h'(x-\theta)$. Therefore
> $$Q_c(\Delta x)-h'(x)=\tfrac12\Big[\big(h'(x+\theta)-h'(x)\big)-\big(h'(x)-h'(x-\theta)\big)\Big]=\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big[h''(\xi_1)-h''(\xi_2)\big]$$
> with $\xi_1\in(x,x+\theta)$ and $\xi_2\in(x-\theta,x)$, using the theorem on $h'$ twice. Once more, $|h''(\xi_1)-h''(\xi_2)|\le M_3\,|\xi_1-\xi_2|\le2\theta M_3$, hence $|Q_c(\Delta x)-h'(x)|\le\theta^2M_3\le M_3\Delta x^2$. $\blacksquare$
>
> Taylor's theorem in [[LM3]] sharpens the constants: the forward error is $\tfrac12h''(x)\,\Delta x$ and the central error $\tfrac16h'''(x)\,\Delta x^2$, up to higher-order terms.

The orders are visible in the numbers. For $h(x)=e^x$ at 0, the forward error divided by $\Delta x$ is 0.5171, 0.5084 and 0.5042 for $\Delta x=0.1$, 0.05 and 0.025, approaching $h''(0)/2=0.5$. The central error divided by $\Delta x^2$ is 0.16675, 0.16669 and 0.16667, approaching $h'''(0)/6=1/6$.

### Rounding error and the best step

Double precision stores about 16 significant digits. The gap between 1 and the next representable number is **machine epsilon**, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}=2.22\times10^{-16}$, so each computed $h(x\pm\Delta x)$ carries a rounding error of order $\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}|h(x)|$. Subtracting two nearly equal values keeps those errors while the true difference shrinks, and dividing by $\Delta x$ magnifies them.

The total error of the forward quotient is therefore about
$$\operatorname{err}_+(\Delta x)\approx\tfrac12|h''|\,\Delta x+\frac{2\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}|h|}{\Delta x},\qquad\text{minimized at } \Delta x_+^*=2\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}|h|/|h''|},$$
and that of the central quotient about
$$\operatorname{err}_c(\Delta x)\approx\tfrac16|h'''|\,\Delta x^2+\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}|h|}{\Delta x},\qquad\text{minimized at } \Delta x_c^*=\big(3\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}|h|/|h'''|\big)^{1/3}.$$
These are order-of-magnitude models, with the truncation constants taken from [[LM3]]. The lessons are robust: the best forward step scales like $\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}}=1.5\times10^{-8}$, the best central step like $\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}^{1/3}=6.1\times10^{-6}$, each times the scale of the problem.

For the bond, $|P|=100$, $P''(2\%)=9{,}399.5462$ and $P'''(2\%)=-108{,}918.1833$. The models give $\Delta y_+^*\approx3\times10^{-9}$ and $\Delta y_c^*\approx8\times10^{-7}$. [[ex:fderror]] scans $\Delta y$ from $10^{-16}$ to $10^{-1}$; the smallest errors occur at $\Delta y=10^{-8.25}$ (forward, relative error $2.4\times10^{-8}$) and $\Delta y=10^{-5.75}$ (central, relative error $6.1\times10^{-12}$).

## Derivatives in Python {lo=8}

Three habits make derivatives safe in code. Compare every numerical derivative with an exact one on a case where both exist. Bump with central differences and a step well above the rounding floor. And let a computer algebra system check hand-derived formulas.

The first block implements (2.11) and reproduces [[eg:bump]]:

```python
def bond_price(y, coupon=0.02, n=10, face=100.0):
    """Annual-coupon bond priced at yield y; the 10-year 2% bond by default."""
    return (sum(coupon * face / (1 + y) ** t for t in range(1, n + 1))
            + face / (1 + y) ** n)

def forward_diff(fun, x, dx):
    return (fun(x + dx) - fun(x)) / dx

def central_diff(fun, x, dx):
    return (fun(x + dx) - fun(x - dx)) / (2 * dx)

y0, dy = 0.02, 1e-4
P0 = bond_price(y0)
print(f"D_mod forward {-forward_diff(bond_price, y0, dy) / P0:.6f}")  # 8.977887
print(f"D_mod central {-central_diff(bond_price, y0, dy) / P0:.6f}")  # 8.982587
```

SymPy differentiates the same function symbolically and checks the leverage derivative of [[eg:levfun]]:

```python
import sympy as sp

y = sp.symbols("y", positive=True)
P = (sum(sp.Integer(2) / (1 + y) ** t for t in range(1, 11))
     + 100 / (1 + y) ** 10)
dP = sp.diff(P, y)                                     # exact derivative
print(sp.N(dP.subs(y, sp.Rational(1, 50)), 10))        # -898.2585006
print(sp.N((-dP / P).subs(y, sp.Rational(1, 50)), 6))  # 8.98259

F, E0, F0, N, m = sp.symbols("F E_0 F_0 N m", positive=True)
f = N * m * F / (E0 + N * m * (F - F0))                # exposure / equity
print(sp.simplify(sp.diff(f, F) + f * (f - 1) / F))    # 0, so f'(F) = -f(f-1)/F
```

## Summary {-}

- A limit describes where a function is heading. From $1+x\le e^x\le1/(1-x)$ and the squeeze theorem, $(e^x-1)/x\to1$ and $\ln(1+x)/x\to1$; these two limits drive every derivative of exponentials and logarithms.
- A function is continuous when its limit equals its value; discontinuities are removable, jumps (digital payoffs) or infinite (leverage at zero equity). A call payoff is continuous but not differentiable at the strike.
- The derivative (2.4) is a rate with units: a slope, a marginal rate and a sensitivity. Six rules proved from (2.4) differentiate every function built from powers, exponentials and logarithms.
- A futures position's delta is $Nm$ NT\$ per point (TX 200, MTX 50, TMF 10). Modified duration is $-P'(y)/P$, and Macaulay duration emerges as the present-value-weighted time; the 10-year 2% par bond has $D_{\text{mod}}=8.9826$.
- Elasticity is $d\ln h/d\ln x$. A futures account's equity elasticity equals its leverage $f=NmF/E$, exposure ÷ equity, exactly for one mark-to-market period. On 11 June 2025, $f=7.3720$ turned a 1.031% futures move into a 7.60% account move.
- The leverage of a fixed position has elasticity $1-f$. Account A's leverage fell from 7.37 to 2.89 in the June–October 2025 rally; account B's rose from 4.73 to 44.29 in three April sessions.
- A linear approximation has error $O((x-x_0)^2)$ and fails for large moves, near singularities, at kinks and jumps, and at stationary points.
- Numerical derivatives trade truncation error ($O(\Delta x)$ forward, $O(\Delta x^2)$ central) against rounding error (of order $\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}/\Delta x$). Best steps are near $10^{-8}$ and $10^{-6}$ times the problem's scale; a 1 bp central bump prices duration to within 0.000002.

## Solutions {-}

1. **B** is correct. With $z=2x$, $(e^{2x}-1)/x=2\,(e^z-1)/z\to2$ by (2.2). A ignores the factor 2; C confuses the limit with $e^{2x}$ at $x=1$.

2. **C** is correct. By the chain rule, $h'(x)=3/(1+3x)$, so $h'(0)=3$. A inverts the inner derivative; B forgets it.

3. $\dfrac{\sqrt{4+\Delta x}-2}{\Delta x}=\dfrac{(4+\Delta x)-4}{\Delta x\,(\sqrt{4+\Delta x}+2)}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{4+\Delta x}+2}\to\dfrac14$. So the derivative is 0.25, in agreement with $\tfrac12x^{-1/2}$ at $x=4$.

4. **A** is correct (Section 3). B fails for $|x|$ at 0. C fails because the left and right derivatives at $K$ are 0 and 1.

5. Delta is $200-3\times50+7\times10=120$ NT\$ per point. A 250-point fall loses $120\times250=$ NT\$30,000.

6. **C** is correct. Notional is $2\times50\times25{,}000=$ NT\$2,500,000, so $f=2{,}500{,}000/500{,}000=5.0$, and the return is $5.0\times1.2\%=6.0\%$ exactly. A is twice the move (the number of contracts); B is the leverage itself.

7. By (2.9) the elasticity is $1-8=-7$. Linear estimates: $8\times(1-7\times0.02)=6.88$ after the rise and $8\times(1+7\times0.02)=9.12$ after the fall. Exactly, $f=8\times1.02/(1+8\times0.02)=7.0345$ after the rise and $8\times0.98/(1-8\times0.02)=9.3333$ after the fall. The fall moves the account toward zero equity, where $f$ curves sharply upward (Section 7), so the tangent line falls further behind.

8. Annual compounding: $D_{\text{mod}}=5/1.02=4.9020$. Continuous compounding: $dPV/dr=-5\,PV$, so the duration is exactly 5.0000. The two differ by the factor $1+y$: modified duration is Macaulay duration (here 5) divided by $1+y$, and continuous compounding removes that factor.

9. Let $v=1/(1+y)$ and $a_{n,y}=(1-v^n)/y$, so that $P(y)=C\,a_{n,y}+100\,v^n$. The chain rule gives $\frac{d}{dy}v^n=-n\,v^{n+1}$, and the quotient rule gives $\frac{d}{dy}a_{n,y}=\big[n\,y\,v^{n+1}-(1-v^n)\big]/y^2$. At $y=y_0$, where $C=100\,y_0$:
   $$P'(y_0)=100\Big[\frac{n\,y_0\,v^{n+1}-(1-v^n)}{y_0}-n\,v^{n+1}\Big]=-100\,\frac{1-v^n}{y_0}=-100\,a_{n,y_0}.$$
   Since $P(y_0)=100$ at par, $D_{\text{mod}}=-P'/P=a_{n,y_0}$. For $y_0=2\%$ and $n=10$, $a_{n,y_0}=8.9826$, matching [[eg:duration]].

10. Exactly, $1.08\times0.95-1=+2.6000\%$. To first order, $8\%-5\%=+3.00\%$. The neglected term is the cross product $R^{\text{USD}}R^{\text{FX}}=0.08\times(-0.05)=-0.4000\%$, which is second order; log returns add without it.

11. Forward: $(\ln2.01-\ln2)/0.01=0.4987542$, error −0.0012458. Central: $(\ln2.01-\ln1.99)/0.02=0.500004167$, error $+4.167\times10^{-6}$. The exact value is $0.5$; the central estimate is about 300 times more accurate.

12. **B** is correct. The best central step scales like $\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}^{1/3}=6.1\times10^{-6}$. C is the best *forward* step, $\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}}$; A is machine epsilon itself, where rounding destroys the quotient.

13. $g'(f)=\dfrac{0.01}{1+0.02f}-\dfrac{0.005}{1-0.01f}$. Then $g'(0)=0.00500$, the arithmetic mean return; $g'(10)=0.002778$; $g'(25)=0$; $g'(40)=-0.002778$. Growth rises with $f$ up to $f=25$ and falls beyond it, so $f^*=25$ maximizes growth. [[LM4]] develops this first-order condition and explains why such a large $f$ should not be used as is.

14. By the quotient rule, $CY'=-C\,P'/P^2=(C/P)\cdot(-P'/P)=CY\cdot D_{\text{mod}}$. For the 10-year bond, $CY=2.0000\%$ and $CY'=0.02\times8.9826=0.17965$, so a 10 bp rise lifts the current yield by about $0.17965\times0.001=1.80$ bp. Exact repricing, $2/P(2.1\%)=2/99.1064$, gives 2.01803%, also +1.80 bp.

15. $F=22{,}470.10\,e^{(0.016-0.030)\times0.25}=22{,}391.6$. Since $F$ is proportional to $S$, $\eta_{F,S}=1$ exactly, although $dF/dS=e^{(r_f-q)\tau}=0.996506$. $dF/dq=-\tau F=-5{,}597.9$ per unit of $q$, so a rise of 0.001 in $q$ lowers $F$ by 5.60 points.

16. The scan reproduces [[ex:fdtable]]. The central error is smallest at $\Delta y=10^{-6}$ (relative error $6.9\times10^{-11}$). On this grid the forward error is smallest at $10^{-10}$ ($4.2\times10^{-8}$), but $10^{-9}$ ($5.3\times10^{-8}$) and $10^{-8}$ ($6.1\times10^{-8}$) are within a factor of 1.5 of it: a plateau around the crossover of the two error terms at $3\times10^{-9}$.

    At $10^{-8}$ truncation still dominates ($5.2\times10^{-8}$ against $4.9\times10^{-9}$ of rounding in the model). Below the crossover rounding dominates, and the low value at $10^{-10}$ (model $4.9\times10^{-7}$) is luck. The quarter-decade scan of [[ex:fderror]] puts the minima at $10^{-8.25}$ and $10^{-5.75}$, which match $\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}}=1.5\times10^{-8}$ and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}^{1/3}=6.1\times10^{-6}$ in order of magnitude.

## Glossary {-}

| Term | 中文 | Meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Limit | 極限 | Value $\ell$ approached by $h(x)$ as $x\to x_0$, regardless of $h(x_0)$. |
| One-sided limit | 單邊極限 | Limit as $x$ approaches $x_0$ from the left or from the right only. |
| Squeeze theorem | 夾擠定理 | A function trapped between two functions with a common limit shares that limit. |
| Continuity | 連續性 | $\lim_{x\to x_0}h(x)=h(x_0)$. |
| Difference quotient | 差商 | $[h(x_0+\Delta x)-h(x_0)]/\Delta x$, the slope of a secant line. |
| Derivative | 導數 | Limit of the difference quotient, (2.4); a rate with units. |
| Tangent line | 切線 | Line through $(x_0,h(x_0))$ with slope $h'(x_0)$. |
| Mean value theorem | 均值定理 | Some interior point has instantaneous rate equal to the average rate. |
| Chain rule | 連鎖律 | $\frac{d}{dx}h(u(x))=h'(u(x))\,u'(x)$, (2.5). |
| Sensitivity | 敏感度 | Change in value per unit change in one risk factor, others fixed. |
| Contract multiplier | 契約乘數 | NT\$ value of one index point for one futures contract. |
| Notional value | 名目價值（契約價值） | Multiplier × futures price × number of contracts: the exposure. |
| Delta | Delta | Derivative of a position's value with respect to the underlying price. |
| Modified duration | 修正存續期間 | $-P'(y)/P$, (2.6). |
| Macaulay duration | 麥考利存續期間 | Present-value-weighted average time of cash flows; $(1+y)D_{\text{mod}}$. |
| DV01 | 基點價值 | Price fall for a one-basis-point rise in yield, $-P'(y)\times0.0001$. |
| Elasticity | 彈性 | $d\ln h/d\ln x$, the percentage change in $h$ per 1% change in $x$, (2.7). |
| Leverage | 槓桿倍數 | Exposure ÷ equity, $f=NmF/E$; the equity elasticity of a futures account, (2.8). |
| Initial margin | 原始保證金 | Equity required per contract to open a futures position. |
| Maintenance margin | 維持保證金 | Equity level below which a margin call is triggered. |
| Differential | 微分 | $dh=h'(x_0)\,dx$, the change along the tangent line. |
| Linear approximation | 線性近似 | $T_1(x)=h(x_0)+h'(x_0)(x-x_0)$; error $O((x-x_0)^2)$, (2.10). |
| Forward difference | 前向差分 | $[h(x+\Delta x)-h(x)]/\Delta x$; truncation error $O(\Delta x)$. |
| Central difference | 中央差分 | $[h(x+\Delta x)-h(x-\Delta x)]/(2\Delta x)$; truncation error $O(\Delta x^2)$. |
| Truncation error | 截斷誤差 | Error from replacing a limit by a finite step. |
| Rounding error | 捨入誤差 | Error from finite-precision arithmetic. |
| Machine epsilon | 機器精度 | Gap between 1 and the next double-precision number, $2.22\times10^{-16}$. |
