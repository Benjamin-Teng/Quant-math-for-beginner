## Introduction

A derivative answers one question: when an input moves a little, how much does an output move, per unit of input? Traders answer it daily without naming it. One TX contract gains NT\$200 per index point; a bond with modified duration 9 loses about 9% per percentage point of yield.

This module builds the mathematics behind such numbers — limits, derivatives and elasticities — and shows where the linear picture breaks.

> [!case] A 1% day that becomes a 7.6% day
> On 11 June 2025 TAIEX closed at 22,470.10, up 1.02%. The June TAIEX futures contract (TX) settled at 22,344, up 228 points. TX is marked to market at NT\$200 per index point, so each long contract was credited NT\$45,600 that evening.
>
> Two traders were each long one contract at the previous day's settlement. Trader A had NT\$600,000 of account equity and earned **+7.60%** on the day. Trader B had NT\$900,000 and earned **+5.07%**. Both accounts exceeded an initial margin of NT\$350,000 per contract. That margin is an *illustrative assumption*: TAIFEX resets margins often, and Section 6 quotes a dated official figure.
>
> Two months earlier the same position had been a disaster. On 7 April 2025 TX settled at its −10% daily limit. Trader A lost **71%** of equity in one session and Trader B lost **47%**.
>
> Why is the multiple 7.4 for one trader and 4.9 for the other? Why does it not stay fixed? And why did a 19% fall in the futures price over three sessions lift B's leverage from 4.7 to 44?
>
> Each answer is one derivative. Section 6 resolves the case, and Section 7 explains why the linear answer failed in April.

## Limits and Continuity {lo=1}

### Where a function is heading

The slope of a chord of $e^x$ near $0$ is $(e^h-1)/h$. At $h=0$ the expression is $0/0$ and means nothing. For $h=0.01$ it is 1.005017, and for $h=-0.01$ it is 0.995017: the values head toward 1 from both sides. A limit makes "heads toward" precise, and it does not care what happens *at* the point.

> [!definition] Limit
> $\lim_{x\to a}g(x)=L$ means that $g(x)$ is as close to $L$ as we like whenever $x$ is close enough to $a$, with $x\ne a$. Precisely: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a $\delta>0$ such that $0<|x-a|<\delta$ implies $|g(x)-L|<\varepsilon$. The **one-sided limits** $\lim_{x\to a^-}$ and $\lim_{x\to a^+}$ let $x$ approach from one side only; the limit exists exactly when both exist and agree. $\lim_{x\to\infty}g(x)=L$ means $|g(x)-L|<\varepsilon$ for all sufficiently large $x$.

Limits combine as expected: if $\lim g=A$ and $\lim u=B$, then $\lim(g\pm u)=A\pm B$, $\lim gu=AB$, and $\lim g/u=A/B$ when $B\ne0$. If $\psi$ is continuous at $A$, then $\lim\psi(g(x))=\psi(A)$. These **limit laws** are stated without proof (Stewart, chapter 2); one more tool does the heavy lifting below.

> [!theorem] Squeeze theorem
> If $\ell(x)\le g(x)\le u(x)$ for all $x\ne a$ near $a$, and $\lim_{x\to a}\ell(x)=\lim_{x\to a}u(x)=A$, then $\lim_{x\to a}g(x)=A$.

> [!deepdive]- The ε–δ definition at work
> **A limit from the definition.** To show $\lim_{x\to2}(3x-1)=5$, let $\varepsilon>0$ and choose $\delta=\varepsilon/3$. Then $0<|x-2|<\delta$ gives $|(3x-1)-5|=3|x-2|<3\delta=\varepsilon$. The proof is a recipe: given a tolerance $\varepsilon$ on the output, produce a tolerance $\delta$ on the input.
>
> **Proof of the squeeze theorem.** Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $\delta$ so small that $0<|x-a|<\delta$ implies $\ell(x)>A-\varepsilon$ and $u(x)<A+\varepsilon$; both are possible because $\ell$ and $u$ tend to $A$. On that set $A-\varepsilon<\ell(x)\le g(x)\le u(x)<A+\varepsilon$, so $|g(x)-A|<\varepsilon$. $\blacksquare$

### Two limits that carry the module

Following [[LM1]], define $e^x$ as the limit of $(1+x/n)^n$ as $n\to\infty$. Everything we need about its slope then follows from **Bernoulli's inequality**: for every integer $n\ge1$ and every $u\ge-1$,
$$(1+u)^n\ge1+nu .$$
The proof is by induction. It holds with equality for $n=1$. If it holds for $n$, then, because $1+u\ge0$, $(1+u)^{n+1}\ge(1+nu)(1+u)=1+(n+1)u+nu^2\ge1+(n+1)u$.

Put $u=x/n$ with $n\ge|x|$: $(1+x/n)^n\ge1+x$. Letting $n\to\infty$ preserves the inequality, so $e^x\ge1+x$ for every real $x$. Applying this to $-x$ gives $e^{-x}\ge1-x$, which is positive when $x<1$. Since $e^xe^{-x}=1$,
$$1+x\;\le\;e^x\;\le\;\frac{1}{1-x}\qquad(x<1). \tag{2.1}$$

Subtract 1 and divide by $h$. For $0<h<1$ this gives $1\le(e^h-1)/h\le1/(1-h)$. For $h<0$ the division flips both inequalities: $1/(1-h)\le(e^h-1)/h\le1$. In both cases the bounds tend to 1 ([[ex:elimit]] shows them closing in), so the squeeze theorem yields
$$\lim_{h\to0}\frac{e^h-1}{h}=1. \tag{2.2}$$

A second limit follows by substitution. Let $k=\ln(1+u)$, so that $u=e^k-1$. As $u\to0$, $k\to0$ because $\ln$ is continuous at 1, and $k\ne0$ whenever $u\ne0$. Then $\ln(1+u)/u=k/(e^k-1)=1\big/\big[(e^k-1)/k\big]$, and the quotient law with (2.2) gives
$$\lim_{u\to0}\frac{\ln(1+u)}{u}=1. \tag{2.3}$$

> | +0.100 | 1.051709 | 1 | 1.111111 | 0.953102 |
> | +0.010 | 1.005017 | 1 | 1.010101 | 0.995033 |
> | +0.001 | 1.000500 | 1 | 1.001001 | 0.999500 |

In financial language, (2.2) says that a continuously compounded rate $h$ produces a simple return $e^h-1$ whose ratio to $h$ tends to 1. Equation (2.3) says the same of a log return and a simple return. Both are "≈" statements about small moves; [[LM3]] measures their error.

### Limits at infinity

Limits at infinity describe long horizons. The sequence $(1+1/n)^n$ of [[LM1]] climbs from 2 at $n=1$ to 2.61304 at $n=12$, 2.71457 at $n=365$ and 2.71828 at $n=10^6$, approaching $e$. The same idea values an income stream that never stops.

> [!example] From annuity to perpetuity {#annuity}
> An annuity pays 1 at the end of each of $n$ years. At a 2% annual rate its present value is the annuity factor $a_n=\big(1-1.02^{-n}\big)/0.02$ ([[LM6]] derives it). Compute $a_n$ for $n=10,30,100,1000$ and find its limit as $n\to\infty$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The values are 8.9826, 22.3965, 43.0984 and 50.0000. Because $0<1/1.02<1$, the term $1.02^{-n}$ tends to 0, and the limit laws give $\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=1/0.02=50$: the value of a **perpetuity**.
> >
> > The convergence is slow at first and then fast in present-value terms. Years 101 to 1,000 together add only $50-43.0984=6.9016$. The 10-year factor, 8.9826, will reappear in Section 5 as the duration of a 10-year bond.

### Continuity

> [!definition] Continuity
> $g$ is **continuous at $a$** if $g(a)$ is defined, $\lim_{x\to a}g(x)$ exists, and the two are equal. $g$ is continuous on an interval if it is continuous at each of its points.

Polynomials, $e^x$ and $\ln x$ (for $x>0$) are continuous. So are sums, products, quotients and compositions of continuous functions, wherever they are defined (stated without proof; Stewart, chapter 2). A function can fail in three ways, and each has a financial example.

- **Removable discontinuity.** $(e^h-1)/h$ is undefined at 0, but its limit exists. Defining its value at 0 as 1 repairs it.
- **Jump.** A digital option pays 1 if $S_T>K$ and 0 otherwise. Its payoff jumps at the strike, where the left and right limits are 0 and 1.
- **Infinite discontinuity.** The leverage $f=nmF/E$ of a futures account grows without bound as equity approaches zero. For Trader A on 11 June, equity is zero at $F=22{,}116-600{,}000/200=19{,}116$, and $f\to+\infty$ as $F$ falls toward that level.

Continuity is weaker than smoothness. The call payoff $\max(S-K,0)$ is continuous at the strike but has a corner there; Section 3 shows that it has no derivative at $K$.

> [!pitfall] Market data have no limits
> Prices move in ticks (one TX tick is one index point, NT\$200), print at discrete times, and gap. On 7 April 2025 TX settled at its −10% daily limit, 2,129 points below a settlement struck five calendar days earlier. Limits and derivatives belong to a *model* — a pricing formula, a fitted curve, an account-value function — not to a raw price series. "The derivative of the price path" is a modelling choice and should be stated as one.

> [!zh] 極限：看「趨近」，不看「等於」
> 極限描述的是函數「往哪裡去」，不在乎那一點本身有沒有定義。$(e^h-1)/h$ 在 $h=0$ 是 $0/0$，但從兩邊都被夾到 1（夾擠定理 squeeze theorem），這就是「連續複利 $h$ 很小時，簡單報酬 $e^h-1\approx h$」的嚴格版本。連續性的三種失敗在金融裡都看得到：二元選擇權在履約價跳一格、期貨帳戶權益數趨近 0 時槓桿爆到無限大、買權在履約價雖連續卻有尖角。記住：市場資料是一格一格跳的，極限與導數是「模型」的性質，不是報價本身的性質。

## The Derivative {lo=2}

### From average to instantaneous rate

The average rate of change of $g$ between $a$ and $a+h$ is the **difference quotient** $[g(a+h)-g(a)]/h$: the slope of the secant through the two points. As $h$ shrinks, the secant pivots about $(a,g(a))$. If it settles on a limiting line, that line is the tangent and its slope is the derivative.

> [!definition] Derivative
> $g$ is **differentiable at $a$** if the finite limit
> $$g'(a)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{g(a+h)-g(a)}{h} \tag{2.4}$$
> exists. $g'(a)$ is the **derivative** of $g$ at $a$, also written $\frac{dg}{dx}\big|_{x=a}$. If $g$ is differentiable at every point of an interval, $x\mapsto g'(x)$ is a new function; differentiating it again gives the second derivative $g''$.

A derivative carries units: output units per input unit. That is why one number answers three questions.

- **Slope** (geometry): the steepness of the tangent line.
- **Marginal rate** (economics): the extra output from one more unit of input, such as the extra interest from lengthening a loan.
- **Sensitivity** (risk): the change in a value per unit change in a risk factor. A TX contract has a sensitivity of NT\$200 per index point.

Two derivatives from the definition show the mechanics. For $g(x)=x^2$, $[(x+h)^2-x^2]/h=2x+h\to2x$. For $g(x)=1/x$ with $x\ne0$, $[1/(x+h)-1/x]/h=-1/[x(x+h)]\to-1/x^2$.

> [!example] A derivative from the definition: the one-year zero {#zero}
> A one-year zero-coupon bond pays 100 at maturity, so its price at annual yield $y$ is $P(y)=100/(1+y)$. Find $P'(y)$ from (2.4) and evaluate it at $y=2\%$. What is the price change per basis point for NT\$100 million of face value?
>
> > [!solution]
> > The difference quotient is
> > $$\frac{1}{h}\Big[\frac{100}{1+y+h}-\frac{100}{1+y}\Big]=\frac{100\,[(1+y)-(1+y+h)]}{h\,(1+y)(1+y+h)}=\frac{-100}{(1+y)(1+y+h)} .$$
> > As $h\to0$ it tends to $P'(y)=-100/(1+y)^2$. At $y=2\%$, $P'(0.02)=-96.1169$ per 100 face per unit of yield.
> >
> > A basis point is $0.0001$ units of yield, so the price falls by $0.009612$ per 100 face per bp. For NT\$100 million of face, that is NT\$9,612 per bp. This number — the price change for a one-basis-point rise — is called the **DV01**.

The figure below lets you watch the secant become the tangent. Its opening view uses $g(x)=e^x$ at $x_0=0$ with a forward step $h=0.5$: the secant slope is 1.29744 while the tangent slope is 1. Shrink $h$ and the error falls in proportion to $h$; switch to central differences and it falls in proportion to $h^2$.

### Differentiable implies continuous

If $g$ is differentiable at $a$, then $g(a+h)-g(a)=h\cdot\frac{g(a+h)-g(a)}{h}\to0\cdot g'(a)=0$, so $g$ is continuous at $a$. The converse fails.

The **one-sided derivatives** $g'_\pm(a)=\lim_{h\to0^\pm}[g(a+h)-g(a)]/h$ can exist and differ. For the call payoff $c(S)=\max(S-K,0)$ the right derivative at $K$ is 1 and the left derivative is 0, so $c'(K)$ does not exist. The digital payoff is not even continuous at $K$, so it cannot be differentiable there.

### The mean value theorem

> [!theorem] Mean value theorem
> If $g$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, there is a point $\xi\in(a,b)$ with
> $$g'(\xi)=\frac{g(b)-g(a)}{b-a}.$$

Somewhere, the instantaneous rate equals the average rate. When the bond's yield rises from 2% to 3%, its price falls from 100 to 91.4698, an average slope of −853.0203 per unit of yield. The theorem guarantees a yield between 2% and 3% at which $P'$ takes exactly that value.

Two consequences are used repeatedly. If $g'>0$ on an interval, $g$ is increasing there. If $|g'|\le M$ on $[a,b]$, then $|g(b)-g(a)|\le M\,|b-a|$, which is how Sections 7 and 8 bound errors.

> [!deepdive]- Proof of the mean value theorem
> **Rolle's theorem.** Suppose also that $g(a)=g(b)$. A continuous function on a closed interval attains a maximum and a minimum (the extreme value theorem, stated without proof).
>
> If both occur at the endpoints, $g$ is constant and $g'=0$ everywhere. Otherwise an extremum, say a maximum, occurs at an interior point $\xi$. There $[g(\xi+h)-g(\xi)]/h\le0$ for $h>0$ and $\ge0$ for $h<0$, so the limit $g'(\xi)$ is both $\le0$ and $\ge0$: $g'(\xi)=0$.
>
> **The general case.** Let $s=[g(b)-g(a)]/(b-a)$ and apply Rolle's theorem to $k(x)=g(x)-g(a)-s\,(x-a)$, which satisfies $k(a)=k(b)=0$. Some $\xi\in(a,b)$ has $k'(\xi)=g'(\xi)-s=0$. $\blacksquare$

> [!pitfall] Per unit, per percent, per basis point
> $P'(2\%)=-898.2585$ is a price change per *unit* of yield, that is, per 100 percentage points. Per percentage point it is −8.98; per basis point it is −0.0898. Futures delta has the same trap: NT\$200 per index point for one TX contract, but NT\$44,688 per 1% of the index at 22,344. Most sensitivity errors in practice are unit errors, so write the units next to every derivative.

> [!kc]
> Use the definition (2.4) to find the derivative of $g(x)=x^3$ at $x=2$.
>

> > $(2+h)^3-8=12h+6h^2+h^3$, so the difference quotient is $12+6h+h^2$, which tends to 12. Hence $g'(2)=12$, in agreement with the power rule of Section 4.

## Differentiation Rules {lo=3}

Taking a limit for every function would be slow. Six rules, each proved once from (2.4), differentiate every closed-form function in this book.

### Linearity and integer powers

From the limit laws, $(c\,g)'=c\,g'$ and $(g+u)'=g'+u'$: differentiation is **linear**. For a positive integer $n$, the binomial theorem gives $(x+h)^n=x^n+n\,x^{n-1}h+h^2\,q(x,h)$, where $q$ is a polynomial. Dividing by $h$ and letting $h\to0$ leaves $\frac{d}{dx}x^n=n\,x^{n-1}$.

### The exponential and the logarithm

> [!proposition] Derivatives of $e^x$ and $\ln x$
> $$\frac{d}{dx}e^x=e^x \quad\text{for all }x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\ln x=\frac1x \quad\text{for }x>0.$$

*Proof.* By the law of exponents, $[e^{x+h}-e^x]/h=e^x\,(e^h-1)/h$, which tends to $e^x$ by (2.2). For the logarithm, $\ln(x+h)-\ln x=\ln(1+h/x)$. With $u=h/x$,
$$\frac{\ln(x+h)-\ln x}{h}=\frac1x\cdot\frac{\ln(1+u)}{u}\;\longrightarrow\;\frac1x\cdot1$$
by (2.3), since $u\to0$ as $h\to0$. $\blacksquare$

The exponential is its own rate of change: wealth $W(t)=W_0e^{gt}$ grows at $W'(t)=g\,W(t)$, a constant *proportional* rate $g$. The logarithm converts proportional rates into plain rates: $\frac{d}{dt}\ln W(t)=W'(t)/W(t)=g$. That is why log returns are the natural "rates" of [[LM1]].

### Products and quotients

> [!proposition] Product and quotient rules
> If $u$ and $v$ are differentiable at $x$, then $(uv)'=u'v+uv'$, and, where $v(x)\ne0$, $\big(u/v\big)'=\big(u'v-uv'\big)/v^2$.

*Proof.* Add and subtract $u(x)v(x+h)$:
$$\frac{u(x+h)v(x+h)-u(x)v(x)}{h}=\frac{u(x+h)-u(x)}{h}\,v(x+h)+u(x)\,\frac{v(x+h)-v(x)}{h}.$$
Because $v$ is differentiable, it is continuous, so $v(x+h)\to v(x)$ and the right side tends to $u'v+uv'$. For the quotient, first
$$\frac{1}{h}\Big[\frac{1}{v(x+h)}-\frac{1}{v(x)}\Big]=-\frac{v(x+h)-v(x)}{h}\cdot\frac{1}{v(x+h)\,v(x)}\;\longrightarrow\;-\frac{v'}{v^2},$$
and then the product rule applied to $u\cdot(1/v)$ gives $u'/v-uv'/v^2=(u'v-uv')/v^2$. $\blacksquare$

### The chain rule

Most pricing functions are nested: a bond price depends on discount factors, which depend on the yield. The chain rule differentiates compositions.

> [!theorem] Chain rule
> If $u=\varphi(x)$ is differentiable at $x$ and $\psi$ is differentiable at $\varphi(x)$, then $y=\psi(\varphi(x))$ is differentiable at $x$ and
> $$\frac{dy}{dx}=\psi'\big(\varphi(x)\big)\,\varphi'(x)=\frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}. \tag{2.5}$$

The intuitive argument writes $\Delta y/\Delta x=(\Delta y/\Delta u)(\Delta u/\Delta x)$ and lets $\Delta x\to0$. It is correct whenever $\Delta u\ne0$ for small $\Delta x$, which can fail if $\varphi$ oscillates. The Deep Dive gives a proof without that gap.

> [!deepdive]- A proof of the chain rule without dividing by zero
> **Lemma (Carathéodory).** $g$ is differentiable at $a$ if and only if $g(x)-g(a)=q(x)\,(x-a)$ near $a$ for some function $q$ continuous at $a$. Then $q(a)=g'(a)$. If $g$ is differentiable, set $q(x)=[g(x)-g(a)]/(x-a)$ for $x\ne a$ and $q(a)=g'(a)$; continuity of $q$ at $a$ is the definition (2.4). Conversely, dividing by $x-a$ and letting $x\to a$ gives $g'(a)=q(a)$.
>
> **Proof of (2.5).** Let $b=\varphi(a)$. Write $\varphi(x)-\varphi(a)=q(x)(x-a)$ and $\psi(u)-\psi(b)=r(u)(u-b)$ with $q$ continuous at $a$ and $r$ continuous at $b$. Then
> $$\psi(\varphi(x))-\psi(\varphi(a))=r(\varphi(x))\,\big(\varphi(x)-\varphi(a)\big)=r(\varphi(x))\,q(x)\,(x-a).$$
> The factor $r(\varphi(x))\,q(x)$ is continuous at $a$, because $\varphi$ is continuous at $a$, and its value there is $r(b)q(a)=\psi'(\varphi(a))\,\varphi'(a)$. By the lemma, $\psi\circ\varphi$ is differentiable at $a$ with that derivative. $\blacksquare$

The chain rule completes the toolkit.

- **Real powers.** For $x>0$, $x^\alpha=e^{\alpha\ln x}$, so $\frac{d}{dx}x^\alpha=e^{\alpha\ln x}\cdot\alpha/x=\alpha\,x^{\alpha-1}$ for any real $\alpha$.
- **Other bases.** $a^x=e^{x\ln a}$, so $\frac{d}{dx}a^x=a^x\ln a$.
- **Discount factors.** $\frac{d}{dy}(1+y)^{-t}=-t\,(1+y)^{-t-1}$ and $\frac{d}{dr}e^{-rt}=-t\,e^{-rt}$.
- **Logarithmic derivative.** For $g>0$, $\frac{d}{dx}\ln g(x)=g'(x)/g(x)$: the *relative* rate of change, the basis of elasticities in Section 6.

> | Function | Derivative | Where it appears |
> |:--|:--|:--|
> | $x^\alpha$ | $\alpha x^{\alpha-1}$ | discount factors $(1+y)^{-t}$, annualizing |
> | $e^{kx}$ | $k\,e^{kx}$ | continuous compounding and discounting |
> | $a^x$ | $a^x\ln a$ | discrete compounding as a function of time |

> [!example] Forward rates from a yield curve {#forward}
> A continuously compounded zero-coupon yield curve is $y(T)=2.0\%-0.8\%\,e^{-T/3}$ for maturity $T$ in years (an illustrative curve, not market data). The discount factor is $P(T)=e^{-y(T)\,T}$. The instantaneous forward rate $\phi(T):=-\frac{d}{dT}\ln P(T)$ is the marginal rate earned by extending a deposit's maturity beyond $T$. Find $\phi(T)$ and evaluate it at $T=1,5,10$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > Since $\ln P(T)=-y(T)\,T$, the product rule gives $\phi(T)=y(T)+T\,y'(T)$. The chain rule gives $y'(T)=\tfrac{0.8\%}{3}e^{-T/3}$.
> >
> > | $T$ | $y(T)$ | $y'(T)$ per year | $\phi(T)=y+Ty'$ |
> > |--:|--:|--:|--:|
> > | 1 | 1.42677% | 0.191075% | 1.61785% |
> > | 5 | 1.84890% | 0.050367% | 2.10073% |
> > | 10 | 1.97146% | 0.009513% | 2.06659% |
> >
> > The forward rate exceeds the yield wherever the curve slopes up. The yield is an *average* rate over $[0,T]$; the forward rate is the *marginal* rate at $T$. Whenever a total equals $T$ times an average, $\text{marginal}=\text{average}+T\times\text{slope of the average}$.

> [!example] How leverage changes with the futures price {#levfun}
> An account holds $n$ contracts with multiplier $m$, opened at $F_0$ with equity $E_0$. Its equity is $E(F)=E_0+nm(F-F_0)$ and its leverage is $f(F)=nmF/E(F)$. Use the quotient rule to find $f'(F)$. Evaluate it for Trader A on 10 June 2025 ($n=1$, $m=200$, $F_0=22{,}116$, $E_0=$ NT\$600,000).
>
> > [!solution]
> > The quotient rule gives
> > $$f'(F)=\frac{nm\,E-nmF\cdot nm}{E^2}=\frac{nm\,(E-nmF)}{E^2}=\frac{f}{F}\,(1-f).$$
> > For Trader A, $f=4{,}423{,}200/600{,}000=7.3720$, so $f'(F_0)=-7.3720\times6.3720/22{,}116=-0.0021240$ per index point. A 100-point rise should cut leverage by about 0.2124. Exact repricing gives $f(22{,}216)=4{,}443{,}200/620{,}000=7.1665$, a fall of 0.2055.
> >
> > For $f>1$ the derivative is negative: a fixed long position deleverages as the market rises and leverages up as it falls. Section 6 turns this into a one-line elasticity, and Section 7 shows how it misbehaves in a crash.

## Derivatives as Sensitivities {lo=4}

In risk management a derivative is a **sensitivity**: the change in a value per unit change in one risk factor, other factors held fixed. This section computes three sensitivities that futures and bond desks quote daily: futures delta, bond duration and the rate sensitivity of a present value.

### Delta of a futures position

A position of $n$ contracts opened at $F_0$ is worth $V(F)=nm\,(F-F_0)$ in mark-to-market profit. Its derivative is
$$V'(F)=nm\quad\text{NT\$ per index point}.$$
The sensitivity is constant because $V$ is linear in $F$. By linearity of the derivative, the delta of several positions is the sum of their deltas. [[ex:contracts]] lists the three TAIEX futures contracts.

> [!exhibit] TAIEX futures contracts: multiplier and delta {#contracts}
> | Contract | Code | Multiplier (NT\$ per index point) | Tick | Delta of one long contract |
> |:--|:--|--:|:--|--:|
> | TAIEX Futures | TX | 200 | 1 point = NT\$200 | NT\$200 per point |
> | Mini-TAIEX Futures | MTX | 50 | 1 point = NT\$50 | NT\$50 per point |
> | Micro TAIEX Futures | TMF | 10 | 1 point = NT\$10 | NT\$10 per point |
>
> Source: TAIFEX contract specifications for TX, MTX and TMF (taifex.com.tw, retrieved 10 October 2026).

Delta per index point converts to delta per 1% of the index by multiplying by $F/100$. At $F=22{,}344$ a 1% move is 223.44 points, worth NT\$44,688 to one TX contract. The quantity $nmF$ is the position's **notional**, and "NT\$ per 1%" is notional ÷ 100. That observation leads straight to elasticity.

> [!example] Delta of a mixed position {#delta}
> A trader is long 2 TX, short 3 MTX and long 4 TMF, all in the same expiry month. Compute the position's delta, its profit on a 150-point rise, its delta per 1% at $F=22{,}344$, and the TMF trade that makes it delta-neutral.
>
> > [!solution]
> > Delta is $2\times200-3\times50+4\times10=290$ NT\$ per point, the same exposure as 1.45 TX contracts. A 150-point rise earns $150\times290=$ NT\$43,500. Per 1% of the index, delta is $290\times223.44=$ NT\$64,798. Selling 29 TMF ($29\times10=290$) brings delta to zero. All of this is exact, because each contract's value is linear in $F$.

### Duration: the derivative of a bond price

A bond with annual cash flows $CF_t$ at $t=1,\dots,N$ is priced at yield $y$ as $P(y)=\sum_t CF_t\,(1+y)^{-t}$. By linearity and the discount-factor rule,
$$P'(y)=-\sum_{t=1}^{N}t\,CF_t\,(1+y)^{-t-1}=-\frac{1}{1+y}\sum_{t=1}^{N}t\,PV_t, \qquad PV_t:=CF_t\,(1+y)^{-t}.$$
Dividing by $-P$ defines the **modified duration** and reveals the **Macaulay duration** inside it:
$$D_{\text{mod}}:=-\frac{P'(y)}{P(y)}=\frac{D_{\text{Mac}}}{1+y}, \qquad D_{\text{Mac}}:=\sum_{t=1}^{N}t\,w_t,\quad w_t:=\frac{PV_t}{P}. \tag{2.6}$$
Macaulay duration is the present-value-weighted average time of the cash flows. It is not an extra definition: it is what the derivative of the price function produces.

As a sensitivity, (2.6) says $\Delta P/P\approx-D_{\text{mod}}\,\Delta y$, with an error of order $(\Delta y)^2$ (Section 7). The price change per basis point, $-P'(y)\times0.0001$, is the bond's **DV01**. [[LM3]] adds the second-order term, convexity.

> [!example] Duration of the 10-year bond as a derivative {#duration}
> The 10-year bond of [[ex:quotients]] pays a 2% annual coupon and yields 2%. Compute $P'(2\%)$, $D_{\text{mod}}$, $D_{\text{Mac}}$ and the DV01 for NT\$100 million of face. Then compare the first-order estimate of the price change with exact repricing for yield changes of ±25 bp.
>
> > [!solution]
> > With coupons of 2 and principal 100, $P(0.02)=100$ and $P'(0.02)=-\sum_{t=1}^{10}2t/1.02^{t+1}-1000/1.02^{11}=-898.2585$. So $D_{\text{mod}}=8.9826$ and $D_{\text{Mac}}=1.02\times8.9826=9.1622$ years; the final payment alone carries weight $w_{10}=0.8368$. The DV01 is $0.08983$ per 100 face, or NT\$89,826 per NT\$100 million.
> >
> > For $\Delta y=+25$ bp the first-order estimate is $-8.9826\times0.0025=-2.2456\%$; exact repricing gives −2.2166%. For −25 bp the estimate is +2.2456% and the exact change +2.2753%. The linear estimate is symmetric and too pessimistic on both sides, by about 3 bp of price. That asymmetry is convexity, the subject of [[LM3]].
> >
> > The duration 8.9826 equals the 10-year annuity factor of [[eg:annuity]]. This is no coincidence: for a bond at par, $D_{\text{mod}}=\big(1-(1+y)^{-N}\big)/y$ (Practice Problem 9).

### Rate sensitivity of a present value

For a single payment $C$ due at $T$ the sensitivity depends on the compounding convention. Under continuous compounding $PV=C\,e^{-rT}$, so $dPV/dr=-T\cdot PV$: the relative sensitivity is exactly $T$. Under annual compounding $PV=C(1+r)^{-T}$, so $dPV/dr=-\tfrac{T}{1+r}PV$, a modified duration of $T/(1+r)$. For NT\$1,000,000 due in 5 years at 2%, the continuous version gives $PV=904{,}837.42$ and a sensitivity of −452.42 per bp; the annual version gives $PV=905{,}730.81$ and −443.99 per bp, with modified duration 4.9020.

The same reasoning applies to the index futures fair value $F=S\,e^{(r-q)T}$, which [[LM6]] derives by no-arbitrage. Holding $r$, $q$ and $T$ fixed, $dF/dS=e^{(r-q)T}$, close to 1.

Holding $S$, $r$ and $T$ fixed, $dF/dq=-T\,F$. For a three-month contract at $F=22{,}000$, a rise of 0.1 percentage point in the expected dividend yield lowers fair value by 5.50 points. A derivative that holds the other inputs fixed is a *partial* derivative, treated properly in [[LM7]].

> [!kc]
> A trader is long 3 MTX and short 1 TX. What is the position's delta, and what is its profit if the index falls 120 points?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > Delta is $3\times50-200=-50$ NT\$ per point: the position is net short. A 120-point fall earns $(-50)\times(-120)=$ NT\$6,000.

## Elasticity and Leverage {lo=5}

### A unit-free derivative

A delta of NT\$200 per point cannot be compared with a duration of 9 years, and neither says what happens to an account in percent. Elasticity removes units by comparing *relative* changes.

> [!definition] Elasticity
> For positive $x$ and $y=g(x)$, the **elasticity** of $y$ with respect to $x$ is
> $$\eta_{y,x}:=\frac{dy/y}{dx/x}=\frac{x\,g'(x)}{g(x)}=\frac{d\ln y}{d\ln x}. \tag{2.7}$$
> It is the percentage change in $y$ per 1% change in $x$, to first order.

The last form follows from the chain rule. Put $s=\ln x$, so $x=e^s$ and $\ln y=\ln g(e^s)$. Then $\frac{d\ln y}{ds}=\frac{g'(e^s)\,e^s}{g(e^s)}=\frac{x\,g'(x)}{y}$.

Because elasticities are derivatives of logarithms, the rules of Section 4 become rules of addition and multiplication. For positive $u$ and $v$:

- **power:** $y=A\,x^\alpha$ gives $\eta_{y,x}=\alpha$;
- **product and quotient:** $\eta_{uv}=\eta_u+\eta_v$ and $\eta_{u/v}=\eta_u-\eta_v$;
- **chain:** if $y=\psi(u)$ and $u=\varphi(x)$, then $\eta_{y,x}=\eta_{y,u}\cdot\eta_{u,x}$;
- **sum:** $\eta_{u+v}=\frac{u}{u+v}\eta_u+\frac{v}{u+v}\eta_v$, a value-weighted average.

The first three follow from $\ln(uv)=\ln u+\ln v$, $\ln x^\alpha=\alpha\ln x$ and (2.5). For the sum, $\eta_{u+v}=\frac{x(u'+v')}{u+v}=\frac{u}{u+v}\cdot\frac{xu'}{u}+\frac{v}{u+v}\cdot\frac{xv'}{v}$.

The sum rule is why portfolio sensitivities aggregate by value weights. A portfolio 60% in an index fund (elasticity 1) and 40% in cash (elasticity 0) has elasticity 0.6. [[ex:elastrules]] gives each rule a financial reading.

> [!exhibit] Elasticity rules and their financial readings {#elastrules}
> | Relation | Elasticity | Financial reading |
> |:--|:--|:--|
> | $y=A\,x^{\alpha}$ | $\alpha$ | zero-coupon price $100(1+y)^{-T}$: elasticity $-T$ in $1+y$ |
> | $y=u\,v$ | $\eta_u+\eta_v$ | TWD value of a USD asset = USD value × exchange rate |
> | $y=u/v$ | $\eta_u-\eta_v$ | leverage $nmF/E$ has elasticity $1-\eta_{E,F}$ |
> | $y=\psi(\varphi(x))$ | $\eta_{y,u}\,\eta_{u,x}$ | account → futures → index |
> | $y=u+v$ | value-weighted average | portfolio sensitivity |
>
> Note: for a coupon bond, (2.6) gives $(1+y)P'(y)/P=-D_{\text{Mac}}$. Macaulay duration is minus the elasticity of price with respect to the gross yield $1+y$.

### The elasticity of a futures account is its leverage

An account holding $n$ contracts has equity $E(F)=E_0+nm(F-F_0)$, so $E'(F)=nm$. By (2.7),
$$\eta_{E,F}=\frac{F\cdot nm}{E}=\frac{nmF}{E}=f. \tag{2.8}$$
The elasticity of equity with respect to the futures price is the notional divided by equity: the account's leverage. Because $E$ is linear in $F$, the one-day relation is exact, not approximate: $\Delta E/E=nm\,\Delta F/E=f\cdot\Delta F/F$.

The index enters through the chain rule. With the cost-of-carry price $F=S\,e^{(r-q)T}$ and $r$, $q$, $T$ fixed, $\eta_{F,S}=S\,e^{(r-q)T}/F=1$. Therefore $\eta_{E,S}=\eta_{E,F}\,\eta_{F,S}=f$.

> [!result] Equity elasticity equals leverage
> For a futures account, $\eta_{E,F}=f=nmF/E$ exactly, and under cost of carry $\eta_{E,S}=f$. The same identity holds for any position financed with fixed debt: if assets $A$ are funded by equity $E$ and debt $A-E$, then $\eta_{E,A}=A/E$.

The last sentence covers margin purchases of shares. Buy 1,000 shares of 0050 at an illustrative NT\$60 with NT\$24,000 of your own money and NT\$36,000 borrowed: assets 60,000, equity 24,000, $f=2.50$. A 4% fall in 0050 removes 10% of equity. It is also the levered-beta relation of corporate finance: with riskless debt, equity beta equals asset beta times $A/E$.

> [!example] Resolving the motivating case {#case-solved}
> Use (2.8) and [[ex:case]] to explain both traders' returns on 11 June and on 7 April 2025. Then explain the ratio of each account's return to TAIEX's.
>
> > [!solution]
> > On 11 June the notional at the previous settlement was $200\times22{,}116=$ NT\$4,423,200. Leverage was $4{,}423{,}200/600{,}000=7.3720$ for A and $4{,}423{,}200/900{,}000=4.9147$ for B. The futures return was +1.0309%, so A earned $7.3720\times1.0309\%=7.60\%$ and B earned $4.9147\times1.0309\%=5.07\%$, exactly as observed.
> >
> > The ratios to TAIEX's return, 7.42 and 4.94, differ slightly from $f$: the futures and the index did not move by the same percentage. The ratio of their returns, a measured $\eta_{F,S}$, was 1.0059 that day. On 7 April it was 1.0307, and the same arithmetic gives $7.0987\times(-9.997\%)=-70.97\%$ for A and $4.7324\times(-9.997\%)=-47.31\%$ for B.
> >
> > Over the 145 trading days from April to October 2025, a least-squares line through daily TX and TAIEX returns has slope 1.008 (correlation 0.978). Day by day the ratio scatters: on the 14 days when TAIEX moved between 0.9% and 1.1% in size, it ranged from 0.753 to 1.459.
> >
> > TX's daily settlement price is the volume-weighted average of its last minute of trading, which ends at 13:45; TWSE closes at 13:30. The basis also moves with dividend expectations. On average the measured $\eta_{F,S}$ is close to 1; on any one day it is noisy.
> >
> > The answer to "why 5–8%?" is therefore not the contract but the equity behind it. The multiple is notional ÷ equity. With the illustrative initial margin of NT\$350,000 as the only equity, leverage on 11 June would have been 12.64.

How much leverage is allowed is set by the exchange's margin. TAIFEX's table effective 12 August 2026 requires an initial margin of NT\$701,000 and a maintenance margin of NT\$538,000 per TX contract. The MTX and TMF figures, NT\$175,250 and NT\$35,050 initial, are exactly 1/4 and 1/20 of TX, in proportion to the multipliers.

TAIFEX recalculates margin levels after each regular session and may adjust them when the change reaches a set threshold. That is why this module uses an illustrative figure for 2025 rather than a quoted one.

### Leverage moves: its elasticity is $1-f$

Leverage is a ratio, so its elasticity follows from the quotient rule for elasticities and (2.8):
$$\eta_{f,F}=\eta_{F,F}-\eta_{E,F}=1-f. \tag{2.9}$$
This is the elasticity form of [[eg:levfun]]. For $f>1$ it is negative.

A 1% rise in the futures price cuts Trader A's leverage of 7.3720 by about 6.4%, to a first-order 6.9023. The exact value is 6.9345. On 11 June itself, a 1.0309% rise took A from 7.3720 to 6.9219, against a linear prediction of 6.8877.

Over months the drift is large. [[ex:leverage]] follows both accounts from the close of 10 June to 31 October 2025, holding one near-month contract and rolling the day before each expiry. TAIEX rose 26.94% and the futures position gained 6,824 points, or NT\$1,364,800. A's leverage fell from 7.37 to 2.89 and B's from 4.91 to 2.51.

> <div data-chart="lm02-leverage"></div>

Its return on the *initial* equity is therefore $f_0$ times the points gained divided by $F_0$: $7.3720\times6{,}824/22{,}116=227.47\%$. > [!pitfall] "My leverage is 7" is a statement about today
> Leverage, like any elasticity, is local. A fixed number of contracts deleverages in a rally and leverages up in a sell-off, at the rate $1-f$ of (2.9). A daily-reset fund does the opposite: it trades every day to hold its multiple fixed, which is what produces the volatility drag of [[LM3]]. Neither is "the" leverage, so a risk limit of "at most $f=5$" must say how often the position is rebalanced back to 5.

Its market price can also drift from its net asset value. An elasticity that is designed into a product still has to be measured.

> [!example] A TWD investor in a USD asset {#fx}
> A Taiwan investor holds a US equity fund. Over a quarter the fund returns +6.00% in USD while the TWD price of one USD falls 6.00% (illustrative numbers). Compute the investor's return in TWD exactly and to first order, and describe what a currency hedge changes.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The TWD value is $V_{\text{TWD}}=V_{\text{USD}}\times X$, where $X$ is TWD per USD. By the product rule for elasticities, $\eta_{V_{\text{TWD}},V_{\text{USD}}}=\eta_{V_{\text{TWD}},X}=1$, and log returns add exactly: $r_{\text{TWD}}=\ln1.06+\ln0.94=0.05827-0.06188=-0.00361$. The simple return is $1.06\times0.94-1=-0.36\%$.
> >
> > The first-order answer, $6\%-6\%=0$, misses the cross term $R_{\text{USD}}R_X=-0.36\%$, which is second order. A hedge sells USD forward. It removes $r_X$ from the TWD return and replaces it with the forward premium, which no-arbitrage ties to the interest-rate differential ([[LM6]]).

> [!zh] 彈性 = 槓桿倍數
> 彈性是「百分比對百分比」的導數，沒有單位，所以能比較台指期、債券與外幣資產。期貨帳戶權益數對期貨價格的彈性，恰好等於名目本金 ÷ 權益數 $=f$：同一口台指期，掛在 60 萬權益數上是 7.37 倍，掛在 90 萬上只有 4.91 倍，所以 1% 的行情變成 7.6% 或 5.1%。關鍵是槓桿本身也會動：$\eta_{f,F}=1-f$，固定口數時，漲了自動降槓桿，跌了槓桿暴增。每日重設的槓桿 ETF 反過來每天調整部位維持固定倍數，這就是 [[LM3]] 波動拖累 (volatility drag) 的來源。

## Differentials and Linear Approximation {lo=6}

### The differential

A derivative turns into an approximation by running the definition backwards. If $h$ is small, $g(a+h)-g(a)$ is close to $g'(a)\,h$.

> [!definition] Differential and linear approximation
> If $g$ is differentiable at $a$, the **differential** $dy=g'(a)\,dx$ is the change along the tangent line for an input change $dx$. The **linear approximation** of $g$ at $a$ is $T_1(x):=g(a)+g'(a)\,(x-a)$, the first-order Taylor polynomial of [[LM3]].

By the definition of the derivative, the error $e(x):=g(x)-T_1(x)$ satisfies $e(x)/(x-a)\to0$: it vanishes faster than the step. When $g$ has a bounded second derivative, the mean value theorem makes this quantitative.

> [!result] Size of the linearization error
> If $|g''|\le M$ between $a$ and $x$, then
> $$\big|g(x)-T_1(x)\big|\le M\,(x-a)^2. \tag{2.10}$$
> The error of a linear approximation is $O\big((x-a)^2\big)$: halve the step and the bound falls by a factor of 4.

*Proof.* By the mean value theorem, $g(x)-g(a)=g'(\xi)(x-a)$ for some $\xi$ between $a$ and $x$. Hence $e(x)=[g'(\xi)-g'(a)](x-a)$. Applying the theorem to $g'$ gives $g'(\xi)-g'(a)=g''(\zeta)(\xi-a)$, and $|\xi-a|\le|x-a|$. $\blacksquare$ [[LM3]] sharpens the constant from $M$ to $M/2$ and gives the error exactly.

Linearizing $e^x$ at 0 gives errors $e^{dx}-(1+dx)$ of 0.021403, 0.005171 and 0.001271 for $dx=0.2$, 0.1 and 0.05. Each halving divides the error by about four (4.14, then 4.07).

For log returns, $\ln1.03=0.029559$ against the differential 0.03, an error of −0.000441; $\ln0.90=-0.105361$ against −0.10, an error of −0.005361. The larger move has 12.1 times the error, close to $(10/3)^2=11.1$.

### When the linear approximation fails

The bound (2.10) names the two enemies: a large step $|x-a|$ and a large curvature $M$. Four situations defeat a tangent line.

1. **Large moves.** The error grows like the square of the move. A tangent fitted at today's yield or price is a local tool.
2. **Singularities.** Near a point where $g$ blows up, $M$ is huge and no tangent follows the curve. Leverage near the wipe-out level is the example below.
3. **Kinks and jumps.** At a corner, such as a call payoff at the strike, no tangent exists. Across a jump, such as a gap or a limit-locked session, no continuous model applies.
4. **Stationary points.** If $g'(a)=0$, the tangent is flat and says nothing about the direction of change. The second derivative decides, which is the business of [[LM4]] (optima) and [[LM3]] (convexity).

April 2025 shows the second case in real data. Trader B held one April contract on NT\$900,000 at the close of 2 April, with $f=4.732$. Equity reaches zero at $F^*=21{,}296-900{,}000/200=16{,}796$, where leverage has a vertical asymptote. [[ex:crash]] compares B's exact leverage with the linear approximation $f(F_0)+f'(F_0)(F-F_0)$ built on 2 April from [[eg:levfun]].

## Numerical Differentiation {lo=7}

Many pricing functions are code with no usable derivative formula: a callable bond on a tree, a Monte Carlo option value, an interpolated curve. Practitioners then **bump and reprice**: they evaluate the function at nearby inputs and form a difference quotient. Doing this well requires knowing two errors that pull in opposite directions.

### Forward and central differences

For a step $h>0$, the **forward**, **backward** and **central** difference quotients are
$$D_+(h)=\frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h},\qquad D_-(h)=\frac{g(x)-g(x-h)}{h},\qquad D_c(h)=\frac{g(x+h)-g(x-h)}{2h}. \tag{2.11}$$
The central quotient is the average of the other two.

**Truncation error of the forward quotient.** By the mean value theorem, $D_+(h)=g'(\xi)$ for some $\xi\in(x,x+h)$. Applying the theorem again to $g'$, $D_+(h)-g'(x)=g'(\xi)-g'(x)=g''(\zeta)\,(\xi-x)$. If $|g''|\le M_2$ near $x$, then $|D_+(h)-g'(x)|\le M_2\,h$: the error is $O(h)$.

**Truncation error of the central quotient.** If $|g'''|\le M_3$ near $x$, then $|D_c(h)-g'(x)|\le M_3\,h^2$: the error is $O(h^2)$. The odd symmetry of the central quotient cancels the first-order error term. The Deep Dive proves it with the mean value theorem alone.

> [!deepdive]- Why the central difference is second order
> Let $\Phi(t)=g(x+t)-g(x-t)$, so $\Phi(0)=0$ and $D_c(h)=\Phi(h)/(2h)$. By the mean value theorem, $\Phi(h)=h\,\Phi'(\tau)$ for some $\tau\in(0,h)$, and $\Phi'(\tau)=g'(x+\tau)+g'(x-\tau)$. Therefore
> $$D_c(h)-g'(x)=\tfrac12\Big[\big(g'(x+\tau)-g'(x)\big)-\big(g'(x)-g'(x-\tau)\big)\Big]=\tfrac{\tau}{2}\big[g''(\xi_1)-g''(\xi_2)\big]$$
> with $\xi_1\in(x,x+\tau)$ and $\xi_2\in(x-\tau,x)$, using the theorem on $g'$ twice. Once more, $|g''(\xi_1)-g''(\xi_2)|\le M_3\,|\xi_1-\xi_2|\le2\tau M_3$, hence $|D_c(h)-g'(x)|\le\tau^2M_3\le M_3h^2$. $\blacksquare$
>
> Taylor's theorem in [[LM3]] sharpens the constants: the forward error is $\tfrac12g''(x)\,h$ and the central error $\tfrac16g'''(x)\,h^2$, up to higher-order terms.

The orders are visible in the numbers. For $g=e^x$ at 0, the forward error divided by $h$ is 0.5171, 0.5084 and 0.5042 for $h=0.1$, 0.05 and 0.025, approaching $g''(0)/2=0.5$. The central error divided by $h^2$ is 0.16675, 0.16669 and 0.16667, approaching $g'''(0)/6=1/6$.

### Rounding error and the best step

A computer stores a number in double precision with about 16 significant digits. The gap between 1 and the next representable number is **machine epsilon**, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}=2.22\times10^{-16}$. Each computed value $g(x\pm h)$ carries an absolute rounding error of order $\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}|g(x)|$. Subtracting two nearly equal values keeps those errors while the true difference shrinks, and dividing by $h$ magnifies them.

The total error of the forward quotient is therefore about
$$E_+(h)\approx\tfrac12|g''|\,h+\frac{2\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}|g|}{h},\qquad\text{minimized at } h_+^*=2\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}|g|/|g''|},$$
and that of the central quotient about
$$E_c(h)\approx\tfrac16|g'''|\,h^2+\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}|g|}{h},\qquad\text{minimized at } h_c^*=\big(3\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}|g|/|g'''|\big)^{1/3}.$$
These are order-of-magnitude models, with the truncation constants taken from [[LM3]]. The lessons are robust: the best forward step scales like $\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}}=1.5\times10^{-8}$, the best central step like $\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}^{1/3}=6.1\times10^{-6}$, each times the scale of the problem.

For the bond, $|P|=100$, $P''(2\%)=9{,}399.5462$ and $P'''(2\%)=-108{,}918.1833$. The models give $h_+^*\approx3\times10^{-9}$ and $h_c^*\approx8\times10^{-7}$. [[ex:fderror]] scans $h$ from $10^{-16}$ to $10^{-1}$; the smallest errors occur at $h=10^{-8.25}$ (forward, relative error $2.4\times10^{-8}$) and $h=10^{-5.75}$ (central, relative error $6.1\times10^{-12}$).

## Derivatives in Python {lo=8}

Three habits make derivatives safe in code. Compare every numerical derivative with an exact one on a case where both exist. Bump with central differences and a step well above the rounding floor. And let a computer algebra system check hand-derived formulas.

The first block implements (2.11) and reproduces [[eg:bump]]:

```python
def bond_price(y, c=0.02, n=10, face=100.0):
    """Annual-coupon bond, eq. (2.6) setting; the 10-year 2% bond by default."""
    return sum(c * face / (1 + y) ** t for t in range(1, n + 1)) + face / (1 + y) ** n

def forward_diff(g, x, h):
    return (g(x + h) - g(x)) / h

def central_diff(g, x, h):
    return (g(x + h) - g(x - h)) / (2 * h)

y0, h = 0.02, 1e-4
P0 = bond_price(y0)
print(f"D_mod forward {-forward_diff(bond_price, y0, h) / P0:.6f}")   # 8.977887
print(f"D_mod central {-central_diff(bond_price, y0, h) / P0:.6f}")   # 8.982587
```

SymPy differentiates the same function symbolically and checks the leverage derivative of [[eg:levfun]]:

```python
import sympy as sp

y = sp.symbols("y", positive=True)
P = sum(sp.Integer(2) / (1 + y) ** t for t in range(1, 11)) + 100 / (1 + y) ** 10
dP = sp.diff(P, y)                                    # exact derivative
print(sp.N(dP.subs(y, sp.Rational(1, 50)), 10))       # -898.2585006
print(sp.N((-dP / P).subs(y, sp.Rational(1, 50)), 6)) # 8.98259

F, E0, F0, m = sp.symbols("F E_0 F_0 m", positive=True)
lev = m * F / (E0 + m * (F - F0))
print(sp.simplify(sp.diff(lev, F) + lev * (lev - 1) / F))   # 0, so f'(F) = -f(f-1)/F
```

## Summary {-}

- A limit describes where a function is heading. From $1+x\le e^x\le1/(1-x)$ and the squeeze theorem, $(e^h-1)/h\to1$ and $\ln(1+u)/u\to1$; these two limits drive every derivative of exponentials and logarithms.
- A function is continuous when its limit equals its value. Jumps (digital payoffs), infinite discontinuities (leverage at zero equity) and corners (call payoffs) are the financial failure modes. Differentiable implies continuous, not conversely.
- The derivative (2.4) is a rate with units: a slope, a marginal rate and a sensitivity. The power, exponential, logarithmic, product, quotient and chain rules, all proved from (2.4), differentiate any closed-form function.
- A futures position's delta is $nm$ NT\$ per point (TX 200, MTX 50, TMF 10). Modified duration is $-P'(y)/P$, and Macaulay duration emerges as the present-value-weighted time; the 10-year 2% par bond has $D_{\text{mod}}=8.9826$.
- Elasticity is $d\ln y/d\ln x$. A futures account's equity elasticity equals its leverage $f=nmF/E$, exactly for one mark-to-market period. On 11 June 2025, $f=7.3720$ turned a 1.03% futures move into a 7.60% account move.
- Leverage of a fixed position has elasticity $1-f$. It fell from 7.37 to 2.89 in the June–October 2025 rally and rose from 4.73 to 44.29 in three April sessions.
- A linear approximation has error $O((x-a)^2)$ and fails for large moves, near singularities, at kinks and jumps, and at stationary points.
- Numerical derivatives trade truncation error ($O(h)$ forward, $O(h^2)$ central) against rounding error ($\approx\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}/h$). Best steps are near $10^{-8}$ and $10^{-6}$ times the problem's scale; a 1 bp central bump prices duration to within 0.000002.

   $$P'(y)=100\Big[\frac{Nyv^{N+1}-(1-v^N)}{y}-Nv^{N+1}\Big]=-100\,\frac{1-v^N}{y}=-100\,a(y).$$
   Since $P(y)=100$ at par, $D_{\text{mod}}=-P'/P=a(y)$. For $y=2\%$ and $N=10$, $a=8.9826$, matching [[eg:duration]].

10. Exactly, $1.08\times0.95-1=+2.6000\%$. To first order, $8\%-5\%=+3.00\%$. The neglected term is the cross product $0.08\times(-0.05)=-0.4000\%$, which is second order; log returns add without it.

11. Forward: $(\ln2.01-\ln2)/0.01=0.498754$, error −0.0012458. Central: $(\ln2.01-\ln1.99)/0.02=0.5000042$, error $+4.167\times10^{-6}$. The exact value is $0.5$; the central estimate is about 300 times more accurate.

12. **C** is correct. The best central step scales like $\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}^{1/3}=6.1\times10^{-6}$. B is the best *forward* step, $\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}}$; A is machine epsilon itself, where rounding destroys the quotient.

13. $G'(f)=\dfrac{0.01}{1+0.02f}-\dfrac{0.005}{1-0.01f}$. Then $G'(0)=0.00500$, the arithmetic mean return; $G'(10)=0.002778$; $G'(25)=0$; $G'(40)=-0.002778$. Growth rises with $f$ up to $f=25$ and falls beyond it, so $f=25$ maximizes growth. [[LM4]] develops this first-order condition and explains why such a large $f$ should not be used as is.

14. By the quotient rule, $CY'=-C\,P'/P^2=(C/P)\cdot(-P'/P)=CY\cdot D_{\text{mod}}$. For the 10-year bond, $CY=2.0000\%$ and $CY'=0.02\times8.9826=0.17965$, so a 10 bp rise lifts the current yield by about $0.17965\times0.001=1.80$ bp. Exact repricing, $2/P(2.1\%)=2/99.1064$, gives 2.01803%, also +1.80 bp.

15. $F=22{,}470.10\,e^{(0.016-0.030)\times0.25}=22{,}391.6$. Since $F$ is proportional to $S$, $\eta_{F,S}=1$ exactly, although $dF/dS=e^{(r-q)T}=0.996506$. $dF/dq=-T\,F=-5{,}597.9$ per unit of $q$, so a rise of 0.001 in $q$ lowers $F$ by 5.60 points.

16. The scan reproduces [[ex:fdtable]]. The central error is smallest at $h=10^{-6}$ (relative error $6.9\times10^{-11}$). The forward error is about $5\times10^{-8}$ for every $h$ from $10^{-8}$ to $10^{-10}$. That flat floor is set by rounding, so its lowest grid point, $10^{-10}$ ($4.2\times10^{-8}$), is luck.

    Both minima agree in order of magnitude with $\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}}=1.5\times10^{-8}$ and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm m}^{1/3}=6.1\times10^{-6}$. A quarter-decade grid places them at $10^{-8.25}$ and $10^{-5.75}$ ([[ex:fderror]]).

## Glossary {-}

| Term | 中文 | Meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Limit | 極限 | Value approached by $g(x)$ as $x\to a$, regardless of $g(a)$. |
| One-sided limit | 單邊極限 | Limit as $x$ approaches $a$ from the left or from the right only. |
| Squeeze theorem | 夾擠定理 | A function trapped between two functions with a common limit shares that limit. |
| Continuity | 連續性 | $\lim_{x\to a}g(x)=g(a)$. |
| Difference quotient | 差商 | $[g(a+h)-g(a)]/h$, the slope of a secant line. |
| Derivative | 導數 | Limit of the difference quotient, (2.4); a rate with units. |
| Tangent line | 切線 | Line through $(a,g(a))$ with slope $g'(a)$. |
| Mean value theorem | 均值定理 | Some interior point has instantaneous rate equal to the average rate. |
| Chain rule | 連鎖律 | $(\psi\circ\varphi)'=\psi'(\varphi)\,\varphi'$, (2.5). |
| Sensitivity | 敏感度 | Change in value per unit change in one risk factor, others fixed. |
| Contract multiplier | 契約乘數 | NT\$ value of one index point for one futures contract. |
| Notional value | 名目價值（契約價值） | Multiplier × futures price × number of contracts. |
| Delta | Delta | Derivative of a position's value with respect to the underlying price. |
| Modified duration | 修正存續期間 | $-P'(y)/P$, (2.6). |
| Macaulay duration | 麥考利存續期間 | Present-value-weighted average time of cash flows; $(1+y)D_{\text{mod}}$. |
| DV01 | 基點價值 | Price change for a one-basis-point rise in yield. |
| Elasticity | 彈性 | $d\ln y/d\ln x$, the percentage change in $y$ per 1% change in $x$, (2.7). |
