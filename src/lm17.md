## Introduction

This module asks what happens to averages and sums of many independent random variables, such as a year's 246 daily returns. The law of large numbers says that an average settles at the expectation; the central limit theorem says that a standardized sum is approximately normal. The case shows what goes wrong when they are applied to the wrong quantity.

> [!case] Compounding the average day
> From 2 January 2003 to 8 October 2026 the TAIEX total-return index, with dividends reinvested and no costs, rose 25.2-fold over 5,849 trading days. Its average daily return was 0.06251%. Compounding that average 5,849 times gives 38.7 times the start, against the index's 25.2 ([[ex:case]]).
>
> Now apply a constant leverage $f$ to the same days, rebalanced daily with free borrowing (a hypothetical). Compounding the average day then promises more for every $f$: 2.2 million times the start at $f=4$. Realized wealth grows only up to $f=4.14$, to 2,147 times, and is back at the start at $f=8.04$. Which path does an investor live through, and why does the average day overstate it?

## Sums and Averages of Independent Variables {lo=1}

Take two independent trades of [[LM3]]'s bet, each returning +2% or −1% with equal probability. By (13.6) and (13.7) the outcomes $++$, $+-$, $-+$ and $--$ each have probability $\tfrac14$. The average return of the two trades is 2%, 0.5%, 0.5% or −1%. Its mean is 0.5%, as for one trade, and its variance, in %², is

$$\tfrac14\big(1.5^2+0^2+0^2+1.5^2\big)=1.125,$$

half of one trade's $\tfrac12(1.5^2+1.5^2)=2.25$. Averaging kept the mean and halved the variance.

Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ have a common mean $\mu_X$ and a common variance $\sigma_X^2<\infty$. They are **iid**, independent and identically distributed, when they are independent ((13.13), (16.1)) and share one distribution. Their sum and average are

$$S_n:=X_1+\dots+X_n,\qquad \bar X_n:=\frac{S_n}{n}.$$

By (14.3), $\E S_n=n\mu_X$. Independent draws with finite variance are uncorrelated ([[LM16]]), so the sum rule (16.3) gives $\Var S_n=n\sigma_X^2$, and (14.4) with $\eta=1/n$ divides it by $n^2$.

> [!result] Mean and variance of an average
> Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be iid with mean $\mu_X$ and variance $\sigma_X^2<\infty$, or uncorrelated with a common mean $\mu_X$ and a common variance $\sigma_X^2<\infty$. Then
> $$\E\bar X_n=\mu_X,\qquad \Var\bar X_n=\frac{\sigma_X^2}{n}. \tag{17.1}$$

The standard deviation $\sigma_X/\sqrt n$ of the average is its **standard error**. [[LM4]] used it with $s$ in place of $\sigma_X$, and [[LM20]] derives that estimate. A hundred times as many draws cut the standard error by a factor of ten.

> [!example] How many trades make the edge visible? {#avg}
> [[LM3]]'s bet has mean $\mu=0.5\%$ and standard deviation $\sigma=1.5\%$ per trade. Find the standard error of the average after 100 and after 10,000 independent trades, and the smallest $n$ at which it is at most 0.25%, half the edge.
>
> > [!solution]
> > By (17.1), the standard error is $1.5\%/\sqrt n$: 0.15% after 100 trades and 0.015% after 10,000. It is at most 0.25% when $\sqrt n\ge1.5/0.25=6$, that is, from $n=36$.

> [!pitfall] Correlated days do not average out
> Formula (17.1) needs uncorrelated draws. For identical copies, $X_i=X_1$ for every $i$, the average is $X_1$ itself and keeps the variance $\sigma_X^2$. In the sum rule (16.3) the $n(n-1)$ covariances all equal $\sigma_X^2$, so $\Var S_n=n^2\sigma_X^2$. Positively correlated days lie in between. Whether daily variances add up over months is a question about data, which [[LM19]] examines.

> [!kc]
> Four independent days each have a standard deviation of 1.2%. What is the standard deviation of their average return? What is it if the four days are identical copies of one day?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > By (17.1), $1.2\%/\sqrt4=0.6\%$. For identical copies the average is the single day's return, so its standard deviation stays 1.2%.

## The Law of Large Numbers {lo=2}

Toss a fair coin $n$ times, with $j$ heads. How fast does the frequency $j/n$ approach $\tfrac12$? The chance of a miss by 0.1 or more is an exact binomial sum, (15.1): 0.7539, 0.05689 and $2.7\times10^{-10}$ at $n$ = 10, 100 and 1,000.

Chebyshev's inequality (14.8) bounds the same chance without the pmf. By (17.1) the frequency has variance $\tfrac14/n$, so the bound is $(\tfrac14/n)/0.1^2=25/n$: 2.5, 0.25 and 0.025. The bound is crude, but it tends to zero.

> [!definition] Convergence in probability
> Random variables $\bar X_n$ converge **in probability** to a constant $c$ when $\Pr(\lvert\bar X_n-c\rvert\ge\varepsilon)\to0$ as $n\to\infty$, for every tolerance $\varepsilon>0$.

> [!theorem] Weak law of large numbers
> Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be iid with mean $\mu_X$ and variance $\sigma_X^2<\infty$, or uncorrelated with a common mean $\mu_X$ and a common variance $\sigma_X^2<\infty$. Then for every $\varepsilon>0$
> $$\Pr\big(\lvert\bar X_n-\mu_X\rvert\ge\varepsilon\big)\le\frac{\sigma_X^2}{n\varepsilon^2}, \tag{17.2}$$
> so $\bar X_n$ converges in probability to $\mu_X$.

*Proof.* If $\sigma_X=0$, the average equals $\mu_X$ with probability one. Otherwise, by (17.1) it has mean $\mu_X$ and standard deviation $\sigma_X/\sqrt n$. A miss of $\varepsilon$ is $\varepsilon\sqrt n/\sigma_X$ standard deviations, so by (14.8) its probability is at most $\sigma_X^2/(n\varepsilon^2)$. $\blacksquare$

Only (17.1) was used, so uncorrelated draws suffice (Durrett 2019, Theorem 2.2.3). For iid draws the weak law is Grinstead and Snell's (1997, §8.1, Theorem 8.2) and Wasserman's (2004, Theorem 5.6). For such draws a finite mean is even enough, with a longer proof (Durrett 2019, Theorem 2.2.14).

For independent trials that succeed with probability $p$, the indicator of the event $\mathcal A_i$ that trial $i$ succeeds, $\mathbf 1_{\mathcal A_i}$, has mean $p$ by (14.1) and variance $p(1-p)\le\tfrac14$ by (14.4). The count $j$ is their sum, so (17.2) gives

$$\Pr\Big(\Big\lvert\frac jn-p\Big\rvert\ge\varepsilon\Big)\le\frac{p(1-p)}{n\varepsilon^2}\le\frac1{4n\varepsilon^2}.$$

A frequency converges in probability to its probability: this is the $j/N\to p$ of [[LM4]]'s binary bet.

> [!example] How many trials pin down a frequency? {#chebyshev}
> By Chebyshev's bound, how many independent trials guarantee a frequency within one percentage point of $p$ with probability at least 95%, whatever $p$ is?
>
> > [!solution]
> > The worst case is $p=\tfrac12$, where $p(1-p)=\tfrac14$. The bound is at most 0.05 when $n\ge0.25/(0.01^2\times0.05)=50{,}000$. It is a property of a model of independent trials, not of trading days.

On TAIEX, 3,205 of TR23's 5,849 days rose and 2,644 fell ([[LM13]]), and exact arithmetic on the closes finds no unchanged day. [[ex:upfreq]] plots the running share of up days: 0.5163 after 246 days, 0.5378 after 1,231 and $\hat p=0.5480$ at the end.

The weak law speaks about each $n$ separately, so the weak law alone does not exclude a frequency that wanders away from $p$ again and again. The strong law speaks about a whole path.

> [!theorem] Strong law of large numbers
> Let $X_1,X_2,\dots$ be iid with $\E\lvert X_1\rvert<\infty$ and $\mu_X=\E X_1$. Then $\bar X_n\to\mu_X$ with probability one: the paths along which the averages converge to $\mu_X$ have probability one. Stated without proof (Wasserman 2004, Theorem 5.18; proved in Durrett 2019, Theorem 2.4.1).

Convergence **with probability one**, or **almost surely**, is stronger than convergence in probability, and the strong law needs only a finite mean.

> [!deepdive]- What "with probability one" adds
> Toss a fair coin forever. The all-heads path exists, yet its probability is at most $2^{-n}$ for every $n$, hence zero, an event of the kind [[LM13]] described. The strong law says that all paths along which $j/n$ fails to converge to $\tfrac12$ have probability zero together. The weak law, about one $n$ at a time, cannot say this. Grinstead and Snell (1997, §8.1, Exercise 16) give a sequence that obeys the weak law but not the strong.

[[LM3]]'s Deep Dive made a weak-law argument: the sum of squared increments has mean $\tau$ and variance $2\tau\Delta t\to0$, so by (14.8) it converges in probability to $\tau$. [[LM18]] simulates random paths; here every probability is exact or an analytic approximation.

> [!pitfall] The law does not correct runs
> After ten up days, independent days make a down day no more likely; believing otherwise is the **gambler's fallacy** (Blitzstein and Hwang 2019, §10.2). A frequency converges because early surpluses are diluted, not repaid: the surplus of heads $j-n/2$ even has a growing standard deviation, $\sqrt n/2$.

> [!zh] 大數法則：偏差被稀釋，不會被扳回
> 大數法則（law of large numbers）說的是：獨立（independent）抽樣的樣本平均數（sample mean）會靠近母體（population）的期望值（expectation）。弱大數法則（weak law of large numbers）由謝比雪夫不等式（Chebyshev's inequality）證明：平均數的變異數（variance）是 $\sigma_X^2/n$，偏離超過 $\varepsilon$ 的機率不超過 $\sigma_X^2/(n\varepsilon^2)$，這叫依機率收斂（convergence in probability）。平均數的標準差（standard deviation）$\sigma_X/\sqrt n$ 稱為標準誤（standard error），樣本加大一百倍，標準誤只縮小為十分之一。強大數法則（strong law of large numbers）講的是整條路徑：平均數不收斂的路徑，機率合起來是零，這叫幾乎必然收斂（almost sure convergence）。頻率會收斂，是因為早期的偏差被稀釋，不是因為之後會補回來；以為連漲之後比較容易跌，就是賭者謬誤（gambler's fallacy）。

> [!kc]
> For a fair coin, compute the standard deviations of the number and of the frequency of heads after 100 and after 10,000 tosses. Which one tends to zero?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > The count has $\sqrt n/2$: 5 and 50. The frequency has $0.5/\sqrt n$: 0.05 and 0.005, which tends to zero.

## Growth: the Mean Path and the Median Path {lo=3}

[[LM3]] warned that +100% and −50% leave wealth where it started. Play that bet ten times.

> [!example] Ten rounds of +100% or −50% {#doublingeg}
> Each round independently doubles or halves wealth with probability $\tfrac12$. Find $\E W_{10}$, the median of $W_{10}$, and the chances of ending above $\E W_{10}$ and below $W_0$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > With $j$ wins, $W_{10}/W_0=2^{2j-10}$, and $j\sim\operatorname{Bin}(10,\tfrac12)$. Each round multiplies expected wealth by $\tfrac12(2+0.5)=1.25$, and independent factors multiply in expectation, (16.1), so $\E W_{10}/W_0=1.25^{10}=9.3132$.
> >
> > Five wins return exactly to the start: $\Pr(W_{10}=W_0)=63/256=0.2461$. Wealth is at most $W_0$ with probability $638/1{,}024$, and at least $W_0$ with the same probability, so the median is $W_0$.
> >
> > Only $j\ge7$ ends above the mean, since $2^4=16>9.3132>2^2$. Those paths have probability $11/64=0.1719$ but carry 8.1875 of the 9.3132, 87.9% of the mean ([[ex:doubling]]). With $j\le4$ the investor loses: $\Pr(W_{10}<W_0)=193/512=0.3770$. After 100 rounds, $\E W_{100}/W_0=4.91\times10^{9}$, yet $\Pr(W_{100}>\E W_{100})=0.00044$ and $\Pr(W_{100}<W_0)=0.4602$.

Peters (2019) makes the same point with a coin paying +50% or −40%. Expected wealth grows by a factor 1.05 a round. Log wealth changes by $\tfrac12\ln0.9=-0.0527$ a round, a typical factor of $\sqrt{0.9}=0.949$.

### Realized growth: the median path

Over $m$ days, by (1.12),

$$\ln\frac{W_m}{W_0}=\sum_{t=1}^m\ln(1+R_t)=\sum_{t=1}^mr_t,$$

so the strong law applies to the average log return.

> [!theorem] Realized growth rate
> Let the daily returns $R_1,R_2,\dots$ be iid with $1+R_t>0$ and $\E\lvert\ln(1+R_t)\rvert<\infty$, and let $g=\E[\ln(1+R)]$. Then, with probability one,
> $$\frac1m\ln\frac{W_m}{W_0}\to g. \tag{17.3}$$

For [[LM4]]'s binary bet at a position $f$, the log return is $\ln(1+fb)$ with probability $p$ and $\ln(1-fa)$ with probability $q$, so (4.7) is the two-outcome case of (17.3).

For two positions with $g(f)>g(\tilde f)$ on the same trades, the average log growths differ in the limit by $g(f)-g(\tilde f)>0$. The ratio of their wealths thus grows without bound, with probability one (Breiman 1961; Thorp 2006). General bets, and what maximizing the median path assumes, are [[LM41]]'s and [[LM42]]'s subjects.

### Expected wealth: the mean path

When the $R_t$ are independent with mean $\mu$ and $\E\lvert R_t\rvert<\infty$, the factors multiply in expectation by (16.1):

$$\E W_m=W_0\prod_{t=1}^m\E(1+R_t)=W_0(1+\mu)^m.$$

Call $W_0(1+\mu)^m$ the **mean path** and $W_0e^{mg}$ the **median path**; Section 6 shows that the latter is approximately the median of $W_m$. By strict Jensen, (14.10) and (14.12), $g<\ln(1+\mu)$ unless $R$ is constant. The difference $\delta:=\ln(1+\mu)-g>0$ is [[LM14]]'s daily Jensen gap, and over $m$ days the mean path pulls ahead by the factor $e^{m\delta}$.

> [!theorem] The mean path runs away from the median path
> Let $R_1,\dots,R_m$ be independent with mean $\mu$ and $\E\lvert R_t\rvert<\infty$. Then $\E W_m=W_0(1+\mu)^m$. If, in addition, the $R_t$ are iid with $1+R_t>0$ and $0<\sigma_r<\infty$, then
> $$\Pr\big(W_m>\E W_m\big)\to0\qquad(m\to\infty). \tag{17.4}$$

*Proof of the limit.* The event $\{W_m>\E W_m\}$ says that the average log return $\frac1m\ln(W_m/W_0)$ exceeds its mean $g$ by more than $\delta$, which is positive because $\sigma_r>0$. By (17.2) its probability is at most a constant divided by $m$ (Problem 8). $\blacksquare$

Expected wealth is carried by rare paths far above the median, as in [[ex:doubling]]; the iid bootstrap of [[LM18]] has exactly this mean path.

> [!example] Resolving the case: compounding the average day {#casegap}
> TR23 has $\bar R=0.062505\%$ and $\bar r=0.055170\%$ a day. Explain the gap between the mean path's 38.66 and the realized 25.20. Then estimate the chance of ending above the mean path after $m=T$ days, in an iid model that draws days from the sample.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The mean path is $(1+\bar R)^{5849}=38.66$, and the realized wealth is $e^{T\bar r}=25.20$ by (1.12), the fitted median path. [[LM14]] found the daily Jensen gap $\delta=\ln(1+\bar R)-\bar r=0.7316$ bp, which needs six significant figures: $\bar R=0.0625051\%$, $\bar r=0.0551696\%$. Over the sample, $5{,}849\times0.7316$ bp $=0.4279$, and $e^{0.4279}=1.534=38.66/25.20$.
> >
> > With $\sigma_r=s_r=1.2107\%$, Section 6 justifies the normal approximation
> > $$\begin{aligned}\Pr(W_m>\E W_m)&\approx\Phi\big(-\sqrt m\,\delta/\sigma_r\big),\\ \sqrt{5849}\times\frac{0.7316\text{ bp}}{1.2107\%}&=0.462,\\ \Phi(-0.462)&=0.32.\end{aligned}$$
> > After 23.8 years the chance is still 0.32. A chance of 5% needs $\sqrt m\,\delta/\sigma_r\ge1.645$: about 74,000 days, or 300 years. Chebyshev's bound, $\sigma_r^2/(T\delta^2)=4.68$, is empty.

## The Central Limit Theorem {lo=4}

The central limit theorem says how an average is spread on its way to the mean. [[LM4]] held [[LM3]]'s +2%/−1% bet at its Kelly position $f^*=25$. Call this the Kelly bet: each trade multiplies wealth by 1.5 or 0.75 with equal probability, with growth $g^*=5.889\%$ per trade. After 100 trades the number of wins is $\operatorname{Bin}(100,\tfrac12)$. Its histogram in [[ex:binom]] is close to the normal density with mean 50 and standard deviation 5.

> [!exhibit] Wins in 100 trades of the Kelly bet: binomial and normal {#binom}
> <div data-chart="lm17-binom"></div>
>
> Note: $\Pr(j)$ for $j\sim\operatorname{Bin}(100,\tfrac12)$, each over $[j-\tfrac12,j+\tfrac12]$, and the density $\varphi\big((x-50)/5\big)/5$. At $f^*=25$ wealth falls below $W_0$ exactly when $j\le41$ (shaded): 0.04431 exactly, 0.04464 by the normal approximation, 0.04457 with the continuity correction ([[eg:kellycoin]]).

> [!definition] Convergence in distribution
> The standardized sums $Z_n:=(S_n-n\mu_X)/(\sigma_X\sqrt n)$ converge **in distribution** to a standard normal $Z$ when $\Pr(Z_n\le z)\to\Phi(z)$ for every $z$. In general, distribution functions must converge wherever the limit's is continuous.

> [!theorem] Central limit theorem
> Let $X_1,X_2,\dots$ be iid with mean $\mu_X$ and variance $0<\sigma_X^2<\infty$. Then, as $n\to\infty$, for every real $z$
> $$\Pr\Big(\frac{S_n-n\mu_X}{\sigma_X\sqrt n}\le z\Big)\to\Phi(z). \tag{17.5}$$
> Stated without proof (Wasserman 2004, Theorem 5.8; Grinstead and Snell 1997, §9.1–§9.3).

So for large $n$ the average is approximately $\mathcal N(\mu_X,\sigma_X^2/n)$. The theorem gives no error for a given $n$, and error bounds are not in this book.

> [!deepdive]- Why sums become normal
> Here $t$ is a real argument, and $M_X(t):=\E[e^{tX}]$ is the **moment generating function** (mgf), assumed finite near 0. For a centred draw $Y=X-\mu_X$, expanding $e^{tY}$ by (3.2) term by term (stated without proof) gives
> $$M_Y(t)=1+\tfrac12\sigma_X^2t^2+o(t^2)\qquad(t\to0),$$
> where $o(t^2)/t^2\to0$. Independent terms multiply their mgfs, by (16.1), so
> $$\begin{aligned}M_{Z_n}(t)&=\Big[M_Y\Big(\frac{t}{\sigma_X\sqrt n}\Big)\Big]^n\\&=\Big[1+\frac{t^2}{2n}+o\Big(\frac1n\Big)\Big]^n\to e^{t^2/2}.\end{aligned}$$
> The limit extends (1.6) to $x_n\to x$ by (3.3), and $e^{t^2/2}=\E[e^{tZ}]$ by (5.12). That converging mgfs give converging distribution functions is stated without proof (Grinstead and Snell 1997, §10.3; Blitzstein and Hwang 2019, Theorem 10.3.1).

A binomial count is a sum of $n$ iid indicators, so it has mean $np$ and variance $np(1-p)$, (15.2). For an integer $j_0$, (17.5) with the continuity correction gives

$$\Pr(j\le j_0)\approx\Phi\Big(\frac{j_0+\tfrac12-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\Big).$$

The $\tfrac12$ is the **continuity correction**: the bar of $j_0$ spans $[j_0-\tfrac12,j_0+\tfrac12]$, so the normal area should stop at $j_0+\tfrac12$.

> [!example] The chance of being behind at full Kelly {#kellycoin}
> Hold the Kelly bet ($f^*=25$) for $n=100$ trades. Find $\Pr(W_n<W_0)$ exactly, by the normal approximation of the log growth and with the continuity correction. Repeat for $n=10$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The log growth $j\ln1.5+(n-j)\ln0.75$ is negative exactly when $j<n\ln(4/3)/\ln2=0.4150375\,n$ ([[LM4]]'s 0.41504). At $n=100$ that means $j\le41$, with exact probability 0.04431.
> >
> > The per-trade log return has mean $g^*=0.0588915$ and standard deviation $\sigma_r=\ln2/2=0.3465736$. By (17.5),
> > $$\begin{aligned}\Pr(W_n<W_0)&\approx\Phi\big(-g^*\sqrt n/\sigma_r\big)\\&=\Phi(-1.69925)\\&=0.04464.\end{aligned}$$
> > The value $z=-1.69925$ keeps six significant figures because 0.04464 lies only $1.0\times10^{-6}$ above a rounding boundary. Corrected, $\Phi((41.5-50)/5)=\Phi(-1.7)=0.04457$.
> >
> > At $n=10$ the corrected normal, 0.37591, is close to the exact 0.37695, but the uncorrected one gives 0.29551.

> [!example] One percentage point with 95% {#onepoint}
> Redo [[eg:chebyshev]] with the normal approximation.
>
> > [!solution]
> > By (17.5), $j/n$ is approximately normal with standard deviation $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\le0.5/\sqrt n$. A miss beyond 0.01 has probability about 5% when $0.01=1.96\times0.5/\sqrt n$, with [[LM4]]'s 1.96. So $n\ge1.96^2\times0.25/0.01^2=9{,}604$, against Chebyshev's 50,000. This, too, is a property of a model, not an interval for a data frequency ([[LM20]]).

> [!pitfall] The theorem is about the centre, not the far tail
> Far in the tail a small absolute error can be a large relative one. After 400 trades of the Kelly bet the exact chance of being behind is about 0.0004. The continuity-corrected normal approximation is 2.6% too high, against 0.6% at 100 trades, and the uncorrected one is 14% too low (Problem 6 explains why). Check a normal approximation near $10^{-4}$ against an exact value or a simulation ([[LM18]]).

> [!zh] 中央極限定理：和會變成常態
> 中央極限定理（central limit theorem）說：獨立同分布（iid）的抽樣只要變異數（variance）有限，標準化之後的和會依分布收斂（convergence in distribution）到標準常態分布（standard normal distribution）。所以 $n$ 夠大時，和與平均數（mean）都「近似」常態；這是極限定理，並沒有說某個 $n$ 的誤差多大。二項分布（binomial distribution）的計數是 $n$ 個指示函數（indicator）的和，常態逼近（normal approximation）加上連續性校正（continuity correction），把面積算到 $j_0+\tfrac12$，通常相當準。在遠端尾部，絕對誤差雖小，相對誤差卻可能很大。Kelly 賭局 400 次後落後的機率約 0.0004：加上連續性校正的常態逼近高估 2.6%（100 次時只高估 0.6%），未校正的則低估 14%。

## Wealth Is Approximately Lognormal {lo=5}

By (17.5), the sum $\ln(W_m/W_0)$ of $m$ iid log returns is approximately $\mathcal N(mg,m\sigma_r^2)$, so $W_m/W_0$ is approximately lognormal, (15.7). The exponential is increasing, so the quantiles of $W_m$ are the exponentials of the normal quantiles of (15.6).

> [!result] Lognormal approximation of wealth
> Let the daily returns be iid with $1+R>0$ and $0<\sigma_r<\infty$, and let $m$ be large. Then
> $$\begin{gathered}\Pr(W_m<W_0)\approx\Phi\Big(-\frac{g\sqrt m}{\sigma_r}\Big),\\ F_{W_m}^{-1}(u)\approx W_0e^{mg+\Phi^{-1}(u)\,\sigma_r\sqrt m},\end{gathered} \tag{17.6}$$
> so the median is about $W_0e^{mg}$ and the 5% and 95% quantiles about $W_0e^{mg\mp1.645\,\sigma_r\sqrt m}$. Each "≈" is the error of (17.5), large in relative terms far in the tail. For iid normal log returns, (17.6) is exact.

Likewise $\{W_m>\E W_m\}$ is $\{\ln(W_m/W_0)-mg>m\delta\}$, so $\Pr(W_m>\E W_m)\approx\Phi(-\sqrt m\,\delta/\sigma_r)$, as used in [[eg:casegap]].

[[LM5]] assumed that 0050's annual log growth is normal, with median growth factor 1.2130 and mean 1.2358. The central limit theorem is why that can work: a year's log growth sums about 243 daily log returns, which should be iid with a finite variance. [[LM38]] derives the lognormal law exactly for geometric Brownian motion, into which more and smaller steps turn this random walk ([[LM36]]).

The lognormal mean $e^{m(g+\sigma_r^2/2)}$ of (5.12) can miss the exact $(1+\mu)^m$ badly, because a mean depends on the far tail.

## How Fast the Normal Arrives on TAIEX {lo=6}

A sum of fat-tailed iid days becomes normal only as $m$ grows. Excess kurtosis, $\operatorname{Kurt}-3$ by (14.5), measures the progress: it is 0 for a normal law by (14.6).

Let $X_1,\dots,X_m$ be iid with $\E X^4<\infty$ and $\sigma_X>0$, and let $Y_i=X_i-\mu_X$. In the expansion of $(Y_1+\dots+Y_m)^4$, a product in which some index appears once has expectation 0, by (16.1) and $\E Y_i=0$. The $m$ products $Y_i^4$ have expectation $\operatorname{Kurt}(X)\,\sigma_X^4$ each, by (14.5), and the $3m(m-1)$ products $Y_i^2Y_j^2$ with $i\ne j$ have $\sigma_X^4$ each (Problem 9). Dividing by $(\Var S_m)^2=m^2\sigma_X^4$,

$$\begin{gathered}\operatorname{Kurt}(S_m)=\frac{m\operatorname{Kurt}(X)+3m(m-1)}{m^2},\\ \operatorname{Kurt}(S_m)-3=\frac{\operatorname{Kurt}(X)-3}{m}.\end{gathered} \tag{17.7}$$

Sample kurtosis and its divisor are [[LM19]]'s subject; here the divisor is $T$, as in [[LM5]].

> [!example] TAIEX sums against the iid prediction {#kurtsum}
> TR23's daily log returns have excess kurtosis 5.428. What does (17.7) predict for sums of 5 and 20 days, and what do TR23's overlapping sums show?
>
> > [!solution]
> > The prediction is $5.428/5=1.09$ and $5.428/20=0.27$. TR23's sums over all windows of 5 and 20 consecutive days show 3.63 and 2.29 ([[ex:kurtm]]): the normal shape arrives far more slowly.
> >
> > The cause is dependence between squares. Next-day log returns correlate only 0.039, but their absolute values correlate 0.161. The pairs of absolute values twenty days apart still correlate 0.123. When large moves cluster, the terms $\E[Y_i^2Y_j^2]$ exceed $\sigma_X^4$ and raise the kurtosis of the sum. [[LM26]] models such changing volatility.

## When the Laws Fail {lo=7}

[[ex:failures]] lists what happens when a condition is missing, for the laws of [[LM5]]'s tails exhibit and for dependent draws.

> [!exhibit] What each law needs, and what happens without it {#failures}
> | Draws | Mean | Variance | Fourth moment | Law of large numbers | Normal limit with $\sigma_X/\sqrt n$ | Sample kurtosis settles |
> |:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
> | Normal | finite | finite | finite | yes | yes (exact) | yes |
> | Student $t_3$ | finite | finite | infinite | yes | yes | no |
> | Pareto, $\alpha=1.5$ | finite | infinite | infinite | yes (strong law) | no | no |
> | Cauchy ($t_1$) | none | none | none | no | no | no |
> | Identical copies $X_i=X_1$ | as $X_1$ | as $X_1$ | as $X_1$ | no | no | undefined |
>
> Note: iid draws in the first four rows. The law of large numbers needs a finite mean, (17.5) a finite variance, and a settling kurtosis a finite fourth moment. Identical copies give constant samples, with no kurtosis.

**No mean.** The standard Cauchy density $1/\big(\pi(1+x^2)\big)$ is the $t$ density (15.9) with $\nu=1$. Its tails fall like $x^{-2}$, so $\E\lvert X\rvert=\infty$ and the mean does not exist ([[LM5]]).

> [!example] Averages that never settle {#cauchy}
> Compare $\Pr(\lvert\bar X_n\rvert>1)$ for averages of $n$ independent standard Cauchy draws and of standard normal draws, at $n$ = 1, 4 and 25.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The average of $n$ independent standard Cauchy draws is again standard Cauchy (stated without proof; Grinstead and Snell 1997, §8.2, Example 8.8). Its distribution function is $\tfrac12+\tfrac1\pi\arctan x$, so $\Pr(\lvert\bar X_n\rvert>1)=1-\tfrac2\pi\arctan1=\tfrac12$ for every $n$. A normal average has standard deviation $1/\sqrt n$, so the chance is $2\Phi(-\sqrt n)$: 0.317, 0.0455 and $5.7\times10^{-7}$. Without a finite mean, iid averages converge with probability zero (Durrett 2019, Theorem 2.3.8).

**No variance.** A Pareto draw with $\alpha=1.5$, or a $t$ draw with $1<\nu\le2$, has a mean but no variance. The strong law holds, but sums need a larger scale than $\sqrt n$ and tend to **stable** laws (stated without proof; that theorem is not in this book). Mandelbrot (1963) modelled cotton prices with such laws; Cont (2001) puts the tail index of most return series between 2 and 5.

**No fourth moment.** With a tail index near 3, as [[LM5]] reported for index returns, the mean and the variance exist but the fourth moment does not. The running mean and variance settle, while the sample kurtosis estimates an infinite quantity. [[ex:runkurt]] shows TR23's running excess kurtosis, which 7 April 2025 alone moved from 4.25 to 4.94 ([[LM15]]).

A few days decide it. The ten largest of TR23's 5,849 terms supply 35.0% of $\sum_t(R_t-\bar R)^4$ but only 6.7% of $\sum_t(R_t-\bar R)^2$ ([[LM15]]). For 0050, [[LM5]] found 62.8% and 14.1%, and a jump of the excess kurtosis from 5.84 to 7.63 on the same day.

> <div data-chart="lm17-runkurt"></div>
>
> Note: running excess kurtosis of TR23's daily simple returns, divisor the days so far, from the 246th return (29 December 2003). The line marks [[LM14]]'s full-sample 5.27. The running annualized variance of $R$ (same divisor, times $A$) stays between 0.0326 (12 April 2024) and 0.0580 (23 June 2004), ending at 0.0359. **Dependence and change.** Identical copies are the extreme of dependence. Milder dependence, such as Section 7's clustering, slows the approach to the normal shape. Laws for dependent sequences, the ergodic theorems, are not in this book. A changing distribution also breaks iid: TR23's daily log returns had excess kurtosis 3.44 before 1 June 2015 and 8.41 after, when the daily limit was 10% instead of 7%.

> [!pitfall] A finite sample kurtosis shows no finite fourth moment
> Every sample has a finite kurtosis: TR23's excess kurtosis is 5.27 (kurtosis 8.27, [[LM14]]), whatever the population. What signals trouble is a running value that jumps with single days, as in [[ex:runkurt]]. Quote a sample kurtosis with its period, and prefer tail measures that need fewer moments ([[LM15]]).

> [!zh] 定理失效的時候
> 這些定理都有條件。沒有平均數（mean）時，例如 Cauchy 分布，平均數永遠不會收斂：$n$ 個獨立（independent）Cauchy 抽樣的平均仍是 Cauchy 分布。變異數（variance）無限大時，強大數法則（strong law of large numbers）仍然成立，但以 $\sqrt n$ 標準化的中央極限定理（central limit theorem）失效，極限是穩定分布（stable law）。四階動差（fourth moment）無限大時，樣本平均數（sample mean）與樣本變異數會收斂，樣本峰態（sample kurtosis）卻永遠穩定不下來；加權股價報酬指數的超額峰態（excess kurtosis）在 2025 年 4 月 7 日一天內從 4.25 跳到 4.94。獨立同分布（iid）的假設本身也可能不成立：大漲大跌會群聚，2015 年 6 月漲跌幅限制放寬以後，分布也變了。

> [!kc]
> Daily returns are iid with a power tail of index $\alpha=3$. As the sample grows, which running statistics settle: the mean, the variance, the kurtosis?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > The mean and the variance are finite, so by the strong law their running versions settle. The fourth moment is infinite, so the running kurtosis keeps jumping with extreme days. TR23's running excess kurtosis rose from 4.25 to 4.94 on 7 April 2025.

## The Laws in Python {lo=8}

Log returns enter every sum, simple returns the count of up days. The guard `R > 1e-12` keeps a return that is zero up to rounding out of the up days ([[LM5]]).

```python
import numpy as np
import polars as pl
from scipy import stats

tr = pl.read_csv("data/taiex_tr.csv")["price"].to_numpy()
R = tr[1:] / tr[:-1] - 1                  # T = 5,849 simple returns
r = np.log1p(R)                           # log returns, for every sum
n = np.arange(1, len(R) + 1)
up_share = np.cumsum(R > 1e-12) / n       # running share of up days
mean_r = np.cumsum(r) / n                 # running mean log return


def window_sums(x, m):                    # all len(x) - m + 1 sums of m days
    c = np.concatenate([[0.0], np.cumsum(x)])
    return c[m:] - c[:-m]


for m in (1, 5, 20, 60):                  # 5.43, 3.63, 2.29, 3.20
    print(m, stats.kurtosis(window_sums(r, m)))
print(stats.binom.cdf(41, 100, 0.5))      # 0.04431, exact
print(stats.norm.cdf((41.5 - 50) / 5))    # 0.04457, continuity-corrected
```

By default `stats.kurtosis` returns excess kurtosis with divisor $T$ (`fisher=True`, `bias=True`); Polars' `std` divides by $T-1$ unless given `ddof=0`.

## Summary {-}

- An average of $n$ uncorrelated draws keeps the mean and divides the variance by $n$, (17.1).
- Chebyshev's inequality gives the weak law (17.2); the strong law needs only a finite mean.
- Realized growth settles at $g$, (17.3); the mean path compounds faster, and the chance of beating it tends to zero, (17.4), slowly.
- Standardized sums are approximately normal when the variance is finite, (17.5), so wealth is approximately lognormal, (17.6).
- Excess kurtosis of iid sums falls like $1/m$, (17.7), but TAIEX's 20-day sums keep 2.29.
- Without a mean, a variance or a fourth moment, the matching law fails.

## Glossary {-}

| Term | 中文 | Meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|
| iid | 獨立同分布 | Independent and identically distributed: independent draws that share one distribution. |
| Standard error | 標準誤 | $\sigma_X/\sqrt n$, the standard deviation of an average of $n$ uncorrelated draws, (17.1). |
| Convergence in probability | 依機率收斂 | $\Pr(\lvert\bar X_n-c\rvert\ge\varepsilon)\to0$ for every $\varepsilon>0$. |
| Weak law of large numbers | 弱大數法則 | An average of uncorrelated draws with common mean and finite variance converges in probability to the mean, (17.2). |
| Convergence with probability one | 幾乎必然收斂 | Convergence along almost every path; the paths where it fails have probability zero together. Also "almost surely". |
| Strong law of large numbers | 強大數法則 | For iid draws with a finite mean, the average converges to the mean with probability one. |
| Gambler's fallacy | 賭者謬誤 | The belief that independent outcomes correct earlier runs. |
| Median path | 中位數路徑 | $W_0e^{mg}$, approximately the median of wealth, (17.3) and (17.6). |
| Mean path | 期望值路徑 | $W_0(1+\mu)^m=\E W_m$, (17.4); ahead of the median path by $e^{m\delta}$. |
| Convergence in distribution | 依分布收斂 | Distribution functions converge at every point where the limit's is continuous. |
| Central limit theorem | 中央極限定理 | For iid draws with finite positive variance, the standardized sum converges in distribution to $\mathcal N(0,1)$, (17.5). |
| Continuity correction | 連續性校正 | Cutting a normal approximation of $\Pr(j\le j_0)$ at $j_0+\tfrac12$. |
| Moment generating function | 動差母函數 | $M_X(t)=\E[e^{tX}]$. |
| Stable law | 穩定分布 | A limit of scaled sums of iid draws with infinite variance. |
