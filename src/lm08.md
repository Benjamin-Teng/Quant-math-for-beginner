## Introduction

A trading book holds several instruments at once. Its holdings, prices, weights and returns are therefore lists, and its P&L and return are sums over them. Linear algebra writes such sums as products of vectors. It also adds geometry: a length that measures exposure and an angle that measures how alike two return series are. This module builds those tools on one real book and one real week.

> [!case] 7 April 2025: two limit-downs and a bond that rose
> At the close of 2 April 2025 a trader with equity $W_0=$ NT\$10 million holds the book $\mathbf h=(-1,\ 24{,}000,\ 100{,}000)^{\top}$. It is short one April TX contract and long 24,000 shares of 0050 and 100,000 shares of 00679B, the US Treasury ETF of [[LM7]]. The short future roughly offsets the 0050 line: NT\$4.26 million of index exposure against NT\$4.22 million.
>
> Taiwan's markets were closed on 3 and 4 April and reopened on Monday 7 April, after the United States had announced new tariffs. TX and 0050 closed at their −10% limits, and 00679B rose ([[ex:case]]).
>
> How much did the book make on 7 April, and which line made it? Why did the "hedged" book still lose NT\$124,200 over the week? How alike are the series, over a week and over a decade?

## Vectors: Holdings, Prices, Weights and Returns {lo=1}

A **vector** is an ordered list of numbers, written as a column $\mathbf x=(x_1,\dots,x_N)^{\top}$. Entry $k$ always refers to the same thing, here the same asset, so the order is part of the vector. [[ex:arrow]] draws the weights $\mathbf w=(0.6,0.4)^{\top}$ of a mix of 0050 and 00679B as an arrow from the origin to the point $(0.6,0.4)$. The arrow is a sum of two scaled basis vectors:

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf w=\begin{pmatrix}0.6\\0.4\end{pmatrix}&=0.6\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}+0.4\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}\\&=0.6\,\mathbf e_1+0.4\,\mathbf e_2 .\end{aligned}$$

> [!exhibit] A weight vector as an arrow and as a list {#arrow}
> <div data-chart="lm08-arrow"></div>
>
> Note: $\mathbf w=(0.6,0.4)^{\top}$ holds 60% in 0050 and 40% in 00679B. The dashed arrows are its components $0.6\,\mathbf e_1$ and $0.4\,\mathbf e_2$, and the grey arrows the basis vectors.

**Shape, unit and index.** A vector from a market needs three labels besides its numbers. Its shape is the number of entries. Each entry has a unit, and an index says which asset each entry belongs to and, for prices and returns, on which date. In the case every vector lists (TX, 0050, 00679B) in that order:

$$\begin{gathered}\mathbf h=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\24{,}000\\100{,}000\end{pmatrix},\\ \mathbf P_{\text{2 Apr}}=\begin{pmatrix}200\times21{,}296\\176.00\\29.88\end{pmatrix},\\ \mathbf R_{\text{7 Apr}}=\begin{pmatrix}-0.0999718\\-0.1000000\\0.0180723\end{pmatrix}.\end{gathered}$$

**Addition and multiples.** Vectors of the same shape add entry by entry, and a number $a$ multiplies every entry: $(\mathbf x+\mathbf y)_k=x_k+y_k$ and $(a\mathbf x)_k=ax_k$. A sum such as $w_1\mathbf x_1+\dots+w_N\mathbf x_N$, with numbers $w_j$ and vectors $\mathbf x_j$, is a **linear combination**. Holding two books together adds their holdings, and Section 7 builds every portfolio's return series in the same way.

Two vectors can be added only when they agree in asset order, in the unit of each entry and, for prices and returns, in date. The sum $\mathbf h+\mathbf P_{\text{2 Apr}}$ has the right shape but no meaning.

## The Dot Product: P&L and Portfolio Return {lo=2}

Multiply two vectors entry by entry and add the products: the result is one number.

> [!definition] Dot product
> The **dot product** of two vectors of the same shape is
> $$\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y:=\sum_{k=1}^{N}x_ky_k=x_1y_1+\dots+x_Ny_N. \tag{8.1}$$
> The dot product is also written $\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf y$. The form $\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y$ reads as a row times a column, a matrix product (Section 8).

**P&L.** A book's P&L on date $t$ adds, over its lines, the holding times the price change:

$$\Delta W_t=\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_i\,\Delta P_{i,t}=\mathbf h^{\top}\Delta\mathbf P_t. \tag{8.2}$$

Each term is contracts times NT\$ per contract, or shares times NT\$ per share, so every term and the sum are in NT\$. The units must match line by line, which is why TX enters at $200F_t$ NT\$ per contract. Interest on cash and on margin deposits is ignored.

> [!example] The book's P&L on 7 April 2025, line by line {#lines}
> Use [[ex:case]] to compute $\mathbf h^{\top}\Delta\mathbf P$ for 7 April, and say which line made the money.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The price changes are $200\times(19{,}167-21{,}296)=-425{,}800$ NT\$ per TX contract, $158.40-176.00=-17.60$ and $30.42-29.88=0.54$ NT\$ per share. So
> > $$\begin{aligned}\mathbf h^{\top}\Delta\mathbf P&=(-1)(-425{,}800)\\&\quad+24{,}000\,(-17.60)\\&\quad+100{,}000\,(0.54)\\&=425{,}800-422{,}400+54{,}000\\&=57{,}400 .\end{aligned}$$
> > The book made NT\$57,400. The short future and the 0050 line nearly cancel, leaving NT\$3,400, so the bond line made almost all of the gain. The hedge is sized by notional value; [[LM23]] sizes hedges by regression, with the least-squares tools of [[LM12]].

**Portfolio return.** [[LM1]] (1.13) showed that simple returns add across assets with weights. Take a fully invested book, so that $W_0=\sum_ih_iP_{i,0}$, with weights $w_i:=h_iP_{i,0}/W_0$. Then

$$\begin{aligned}1+R_p&=\frac{\sum_ih_iP_{i,1}}{\sum_ih_iP_{i,0}}=\sum_i\frac{h_iP_{i,0}}{W_0}\,\frac{P_{i,1}}{P_{i,0}}\\&=\sum_iw_i(1+R_i)=1+\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf R,\end{aligned}$$

where the last step uses $\sum_iw_i=1$. Hence

$$R_p=\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf R. \tag{8.3}$$

Log returns do not combine this way: a portfolio's log return is $\ln(1+\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf R)$, not $\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf r$ ([[LM1]] (1.14)).

**Exposures.** A book with futures breaks the identity $W_0=\sum_ih_iP_{i,0}$, because a futures contract costs nothing to enter. Define each line's exposure as its position value over equity, $f_i:=h_iP_{i,0}/W_0$, the $f$ of [[LM2]]. Line by line, $h_i\Delta P_i=W_0f_iR_i$, because

$$f_iR_i=\frac{h_iP_{i,0}}{W_0}\cdot\frac{\Delta P_i}{P_{i,0}}=\frac{h_i\Delta P_i}{W_0}.$$

Summing over the lines gives

$$\Delta W=\mathbf h^{\top}\Delta\mathbf P=W_0\,\mathbf f^{\top}\mathbf R, \tag{8.4}$$

exactly, for one period and whatever the net exposure $\mathbf 1^{\top}\mathbf f$ is. For a fully invested book $\mathbf f=\mathbf w$, and (8.4) is (8.3) times $W_0$.

> [!example] The same P&L from exposures and returns {#expo}
> Compute the exposures $\mathbf f$ at the close of 2 April, and check (8.4) for 7 April.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The position values $h_iP_{i,0}$ are $-4{,}259{,}200$, $4{,}224{,}000$ and $2{,}988{,}000$ NT\$, so $\mathbf f=(-0.42592,\ 0.4224,\ 0.2988)^{\top}$. With the returns of [[ex:case]], the products $f_iR_i$ are $0.042580$, $-0.042240$ and $0.005400$. They sum to $\mathbf f^{\top}\mathbf R=0.005740$, a return of 0.574% on equity, and $W_0\,\mathbf f^{\top}\mathbf R=$ NT\$57,400. No rounding is involved: each product $f_iR_i$ equals $h_i\Delta P_i/W_0$ exactly.

Two rules make the dot product easy to use. It is symmetric, $\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y=\mathbf y^{\top}\mathbf x$, and it is linear in each vector:

$$(a\mathbf x+\mathbf y)^{\top}\Delta\mathbf P=a\,\mathbf x^{\top}\Delta\mathbf P+\mathbf y^{\top}\Delta\mathbf P .$$

So two books held together make the sum of their P&Ls. The P&L over several days is likewise the sum of the daily P&Ls, which [[ex:pnl-lines]] shows for the week.

> [!pitfall] A limit-locked P&L is a mark, not cash
> TX may move at most 10% from the previous regular session's settlement price, in ticks of one index point (TAIFEX). On 7 April that limit, $0.9\times21{,}296=19{,}166.4$, fell between two ticks. TX traded and settled at 19,167, a rounding that the data show and TAIFEX's pages do not state. The settlement price is the volume-weighted price of the last minute's trades, and TAIFEX states no special rule for a locked market.
>
> The April contract's regular-session volume fell from 66,423 contracts on 2 April to 2,736, so the settlement of 19,167 came from thin trading. On 10 April, when TX settled at its +10% limit, the volume was 2,382.
>
> In a market locked at its lower limit, sell orders queue while a buy order fills at once. A buy order would have closed the short TX line at 19,167 and realized its +425,800. A sell order for the long 0050 line, however, would only have joined the queue at 158.40, TWSE's 10% limit. The book could not be closed at its marks, so treat a limit day's P&L as a valuation, not as cash.

## Length, Distance and Exposure {lo=3}

The dot product of a vector with itself is a sum of squares, and its square root is the vector's length.

> [!definition] Norm, distance and unit vector
> The **Euclidean norm** (length) of $\mathbf x$ is
> $$\lVert\mathbf x\rVert:=\sqrt{\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf x}=\sqrt{x_1^2+\dots+x_N^2}, \tag{8.5}$$
> and the **distance** between $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf y$ is $\lVert\mathbf x-\mathbf y\rVert$. A **unit vector** has length 1, and for $\mathbf x\ne\mathbf 0$ the unit vector in the direction of $\mathbf x$ is $\mathbf u=\mathbf x/\lVert\mathbf x\rVert$. The **1-norm** is $\lVert\mathbf x\rVert_1:=\sum_k\lvert x_k\rvert$.

In two dimensions (8.5) is Pythagoras' theorem: the arrow of [[ex:arrow]] has length $\sqrt{0.6^2+0.4^2}=0.7211$. A norm scales with its vector, $\lVert a\mathbf x\rVert=\lvert a\rvert\,\lVert\mathbf x\rVert$, and it is zero only for $\mathbf x=\mathbf 0$.

**Net and gross exposure.** Two numbers summarize a book's exposures $\mathbf f$:

$$\begin{aligned}\text{net exposure}&=\mathbf 1^{\top}\mathbf f=\sum_if_i,\\ \text{gross exposure}&=\lVert\mathbf f\rVert_1=\sum_i\lvert f_i\rvert .\end{aligned}$$

Net exposure lets a short cancel a long, while gross exposure adds both. The two agree only when every position is long. [[ex:balls]] draws both norms for the case's TX and 0050 exposures.

> [!pitfall] Net exposure is not gross exposure
> A limit on net exposure lets a short offset more longs, while a limit on gross exposure counts the short as more exposure. The case book's net 0.295 hides a gross 1.147. Before comparing a book with a limit, check which of the two the limit names; [[LM7]] drew the same distinction for a leverage cap.

## Angles, the Cauchy–Schwarz Inequality and Orthogonality {lo=4}

In the plane, the law of cosines links the sides of the triangle formed by $\mathbf x$, $\mathbf y$ and $\mathbf x-\mathbf y$ to the angle $\theta$ between $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf y$. Expanding the squared length of $\mathbf x-\mathbf y$ with the dot product gives the same formula, with $\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y$ in place of $\lVert\mathbf x\rVert\lVert\mathbf y\rVert\cos\theta$:

$$\begin{aligned}\lVert\mathbf x-\mathbf y\rVert^2&=\lVert\mathbf x\rVert^2+\lVert\mathbf y\rVert^2-2\,\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y\\&=\lVert\mathbf x\rVert^2+\lVert\mathbf y\rVert^2-2\lVert\mathbf x\rVert\lVert\mathbf y\rVert\cos\theta .\end{aligned}$$

So in the plane $\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y=\lVert\mathbf x\rVert\lVert\mathbf y\rVert\cos\theta$. In $N$ dimensions this equation becomes the definition of the angle, once we know that the ratio of $\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y$ to $\lVert\mathbf x\rVert\lVert\mathbf y\rVert$ lies between −1 and 1.

> [!theorem] Cauchy–Schwarz inequality
> For any two vectors $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf y$ of the same shape,
> $$\lvert\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y\rvert\le\lVert\mathbf x\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf y\rVert, \tag{8.6}$$
> with equality exactly when one of the two vectors is a scalar multiple of the other (Strang 2016).

*Proof.* If $\mathbf y=\mathbf 0$, both sides are 0, and $\mathbf y=0\cdot\mathbf x$. Otherwise the squared length of $\mathbf x-a\mathbf y$ is non-negative for every number $a$, and the choice $a^*:=\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y/\lVert\mathbf y\rVert^2$ gives

$$\begin{aligned}0\le\lVert\mathbf x-a^*\mathbf y\rVert^2&=\lVert\mathbf x\rVert^2-2a^*\,\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y+a^{*2}\lVert\mathbf y\rVert^2\\&=\lVert\mathbf x\rVert^2-\frac{(\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y)^2}{\lVert\mathbf y\rVert^2}.\end{aligned}$$

Multiplying by $\lVert\mathbf y\rVert^2$ gives $(\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y)^2\le\lVert\mathbf x\rVert^2\lVert\mathbf y\rVert^2$, and square roots give (8.6). Equality holds exactly when $\lVert\mathbf x-a^*\mathbf y\rVert=0$, that is, when $\mathbf x=a^*\mathbf y$. Conversely, if $\mathbf x=a\mathbf y$ for some number $a$, both sides of (8.6) equal $\lvert a\rvert\,\lVert\mathbf y\rVert^2$. $\blacksquare$

The quadratic $\lVert\mathbf x\rVert^2-2a\,\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y+a^2\lVert\mathbf y\rVert^2$ is smallest where its derivative in $a$ is zero, at $a^*$. So $a^*\mathbf y$ is the multiple of $\mathbf y$ closest to $\mathbf x$, the projection of $\mathbf x$ on $\mathbf y$ ([[LM12]]). [[ex:cs-proof]] draws it: the leftover $\mathbf x-a^*\mathbf y$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf y$.

> [!definition] Angle and orthogonality
> For non-zero vectors $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf y$, the **angle** $\theta$ between them, from 0° to 180°, is defined by
> $$\cos\theta:=\frac{\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y}{\lVert\mathbf x\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf y\rVert}. \tag{8.7}$$
> Two vectors are **orthogonal**, $\mathbf x\perp\mathbf y$, when $\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y=0$.

By (8.6) the ratio in (8.7) lies between −1 and 1, so (8.7) defines an angle for every pair of non-zero vectors. The angle is 0° when $\mathbf y$ is a positive multiple of $\mathbf x$, 180° for a negative multiple and 90° for orthogonal vectors. A **dollar-neutral** book, with net exposure $\mathbf 1^{\top}\mathbf f=0$, is orthogonal to $\mathbf 1$.

**Triangle inequality.** The length of a sum is at most the sum of the lengths. Expand the square and apply (8.6) to the middle term:

$$\begin{aligned}\lVert\mathbf x+\mathbf y\rVert^2&=\lVert\mathbf x\rVert^2+2\,\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y+\lVert\mathbf y\rVert^2\\&\le\lVert\mathbf x\rVert^2+2\lVert\mathbf x\rVert\lVert\mathbf y\rVert+\lVert\mathbf y\rVert^2\\&=\big(\lVert\mathbf x\rVert+\lVert\mathbf y\rVert\big)^2,\end{aligned}$$

so that

$$\lVert\mathbf x+\mathbf y\rVert\le\lVert\mathbf x\rVert+\lVert\mathbf y\rVert. \tag{8.8}$$

> [!zh] 夾角與 Cauchy–Schwarz 不等式
> 在平面上，兩個箭頭的內積（dot product）等於兩個長度相乘、再乘上夾角（angle）的餘弦（cosine）。Cauchy–Schwarz 不等式（Cauchy–Schwarz inequality）保證內積的絕對值不超過兩個長度的乘積，所以在任何維度都能用 $\cos\theta=\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y/(\lVert\mathbf x\rVert\lVert\mathbf y\rVert)$ 定義夾角。證明只用一件事：$\mathbf x-a\mathbf y$ 的長度平方不會是負的，挑最好的 $a$ 就得到不等式；等號只在其中一個向量（vector）是另一個的倍數時成立。內積為零就是正交（orthogonal），淨曝險為零的多空部位與全為 1 的向量正交。三角不等式（triangle inequality）也是它的直接結果：和的長度不超過長度的和。

## Correlation as a Cosine {lo=5}

A return series over $T$ dates is a vector with one entry per date. Subtracting its mean from every entry **centres** it: $\tilde{\mathbf x}:=\mathbf x-\bar x\mathbf 1$, with $\bar x=\mathbf 1^{\top}\mathbf x/T$. With divisor $T$, the standard deviation is $s_x=\lVert\tilde{\mathbf x}\rVert/\sqrt T$, and the covariance of two series is $\tilde{\mathbf x}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf y}/T$, the average product of deviations in [[LM7]].

> [!result] A sample correlation is a cosine
> For two return series whose entries are not all equal, the sample correlation is
> $$\rho:=\frac{\tilde{\mathbf x}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf y}/T}{s_xs_y}=\frac{\tilde{\mathbf x}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf y}}{\lVert\tilde{\mathbf x}\rVert\,\lVert\tilde{\mathbf y}\rVert}=\cos\theta, \tag{8.9}$$
> where $\theta$ is the angle between the centred vectors. Hence $-1\le\rho\le1$, with $\rho=\pm1$ exactly when $\tilde{\mathbf y}=a\tilde{\mathbf x}$ for a number $a\ne0$.

The factors $T$ cancel in (8.9), and so would any other divisor, $T-1$ included. Annualization and units cancel too, because multiplying a vector by a positive number does not change its direction. The bound $\lvert\rho\rvert\le1$ is (8.6), with its equality case.

**Two series in their own plane.** Two vectors span a plane, even in 2,363 dimensions, and a drawing in that plane needs only their lengths and their angle. [[ex:plane]] draws the centred 0050 and 00679B vectors of [[LM7]], scaled so that each length is an annualized volatility $s\sqrt A$. The mix $0.423\,\tilde{\mathbf x}_{0050}+0.577\,\tilde{\mathbf x}_{00679B}$ is the centred return vector of the daily-rebalanced mix, and its length is that mix's volatility.

**Standardized series.** Dividing a centred vector by its standard deviation gives it length $\sqrt T$, and the squared distance between two such vectors depends only on $\rho$:

$$\Big\lVert\frac{\tilde{\mathbf x}}{s_x}-\frac{\tilde{\mathbf y}}{s_y}\Big\rVert^2=T+T-2\,\frac{\tilde{\mathbf x}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf y}}{s_xs_y}=2T(1-\rho). \tag{8.10}$$

Per date, the root-mean-square gap between two standardized series is $\sqrt{2(1-\rho)}$: 0 for identical series, $\sqrt2$ for uncorrelated ones and 2 for opposite ones. [[LM31]] groups assets by this distance.

**A bound on a portfolio's risk.** A daily-rebalanced portfolio's centred return vector is $\sum_iw_i\tilde{\mathbf x}_i$, so its standard deviation is a length divided by $\sqrt T$. For non-negative weights the triangle inequality (8.8), applied one term at a time, bounds that length:

$$\begin{aligned}s_p=\frac{\big\lVert\sum_iw_i\tilde{\mathbf x}_i\big\rVert}{\sqrt T}&\le\sum_iw_i\frac{\lVert\tilde{\mathbf x}_i\rVert}{\sqrt T}\\&=\sum_iw_is_i\qquad(w_i\ge0).\end{aligned} \tag{8.11}$$

Equality needs the centred vectors of all held assets to point the same way, that is, every correlation among them equal to 1. With short positions the bound becomes $\sum_i\lvert w_i\rvert s_i$.

## Matrices as Data {lo=6}

A **matrix** is a rectangular table of numbers. Stack the daily return vectors of $N$ assets over $T$ dates, one row per date, and you get the **data matrix**

$$\begin{aligned}X=\begin{pmatrix}x_{11}&\cdots&x_{1N}\\ \vdots&&\vdots\\ x_{T1}&\cdots&x_{TN}\end{pmatrix}&=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf R_1^{\top}\\ \vdots\\ \mathbf R_T^{\top}\end{pmatrix}\\&=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf x_1&\cdots&\mathbf x_N\end{pmatrix}.\end{aligned}$$

Entry $x_{tj}$ is the return of asset $j$ on date $t$: date first, asset second. Row $t$ is the vector $\mathbf R_t$ written as a row, and column $j$ is the return series $\mathbf x_j$ of asset $j$, a vector with $T$ entries. [[ex:week]] shows the $5\times3$ matrix of the case week.

> [!exhibit] The week as a data matrix: daily returns of TX, 0050 and 00679B, 7–11 April 2025 {#week}
> <div data-chart="lm08-week"></div>
>
> Note: rows are sessions and columns instruments; each cell is a simple daily return, TX from its daily settlement price. Row 1 runs from the close of 2 April to the close of 7 April, so it spans the closed days 3–6 April. Source: FinMind `TaiwanFuturesDaily` and `TaiwanStockPrice`, retrieved 10 October 2026.

The **transpose** $X^{\top}$ swaps rows and columns. It is $N\times T$, and its entry in row $j$ and column $t$ is $x_{tj}$. A column vector is an $N\times1$ matrix and its transpose a $1\times N$ row, which is why the dot product is written $\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y$.

**A portfolio's returns.** Multiply $X$ by a weight vector $\mathbf w$. Entry $t$ of the product is the dot product of row $t$ with $\mathbf w$, the portfolio's return on date $t$ by (8.3). Read by columns, the same product is a linear combination of the assets' return series:

$$X\mathbf w=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf R_1^{\top}\mathbf w\\ \vdots\\ \mathbf R_T^{\top}\mathbf w\end{pmatrix}=w_1\mathbf x_1+\dots+w_N\mathbf x_N. \tag{8.12}$$

In the words of [[LM7]], a matrix times a vector is the vector of each row's dot product with it. Every row uses the same $\mathbf w$, so $X\mathbf w$ is the return series of a portfolio that resets its weights to $\mathbf w$ at every close: it is **rebalanced daily**. [[LM3]] and [[LM7]] assumed daily rebalancing, and (8.12) shows where the assumption sits.

**A fixed book's P&L.** A book that does not trade keeps its holdings $\mathbf h$. Let $Y$ be the $T\times N$ matrix of price changes, with row $t$ equal to $\Delta\mathbf P_t^{\top}$. By (8.2), the book's daily P&Ls are

$$Y\mathbf h=\begin{pmatrix}\Delta\mathbf P_1^{\top}\mathbf h\\ \vdots\\ \Delta\mathbf P_T^{\top}\mathbf h\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\Delta W_1\\ \vdots\\ \Delta W_T\end{pmatrix}. \tag{8.13}$$

> [!example] A 60/40 mix over the week, rebalanced daily {#xw}
> Compute $X\mathbf w$ for $\mathbf w=(0,\ 0.6,\ 0.4)^{\top}$, that is 60% 0050, 40% 00679B and no TX, over 7–11 April. Compare the sum of the five returns with the return of the week.
>
> > [!solution]
> > Row 1 is $0.6\times(-10.000\%)+0.4\times1.807\%=-5.277\%$, and the five rows give, in percent,
> > $$X\mathbf w=\begin{pmatrix}-5.277\\-3.384\\-4.438\\7.145\\-0.026\end{pmatrix}.$$
> > Their sum is −5.98%. The mix compounds, so its return over the week is $\prod_t(1+R_{p,t})-1=-6.32\%$.

At the close of 7 April the book's equity was NT\$10,057,400, $W_0$ plus that day's NT\$57,400. > [!zh] 資料矩陣：一列一天、一行一資產
> 把每天的報酬向量（vector）一列一列疊起來，就是資料矩陣（data matrix）$X$。第 $t$ 列（row）是第 $t$ 天各資產的報酬，第 $j$ 行（column）是資產 $j$ 的整段報酬序列。矩陣乘向量有兩種讀法：按列看，每一列與權重做內積（dot product），得到當天的組合報酬；按行看，是各資產報酬序列的線性組合（linear combination）。每一天都用同一組權重，所以 $X\mathbf w$ 描述的是每天收盤都再平衡（rebalancing）回固定權重的組合。持股固定、不交易的帳戶要用價格變動矩陣 $Y$，$Y\mathbf h$ 是每天的損益，而它的曝險（exposure）會隨價格漂移。

## Matrix Multiplication, Transpose and Identity {lo=7,8}

Equation (8.12) multiplies a matrix by one vector. Multiplying by several vectors at once, stacked as the columns of a matrix, gives the general product.

> [!definition] Matrix product
> If $M$ is $n\times N$ and $K$ is $N\times m$, their **product** $MK$ is the $n\times m$ matrix with entries
> $$(MK)_{ij}:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}m_{i\ell}\,k_{\ell j}, \tag{8.14}$$
> the dot product of row $i$ of $M$ with column $j$ of $K$. The inner sizes must agree; otherwise the product is not defined.

Put the weights of $m$ portfolios side by side as the columns of an $N\times m$ matrix $K$. Column $j$ of $XK$ is $X$ times column $j$ of $K$, so each column of $XK$ is one portfolio's daily return series. [[ex:xk]] does this for three portfolios in the case week, with rows in the order (TX, 0050, 00679B):

$$K=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\1&0.6&0.5\\0&0.4&0.5\end{pmatrix}.$$

**Rules of the product.** The product is **associative**: $(MK)\mathbf x=M(K\mathbf x)$. By (8.14), entry $i$ of $(MK)\mathbf x$ is

$$\begin{aligned}\sum_j\Big(\sum_\ell m_{i\ell}k_{\ell j}\Big)x_j&=\sum_\ell m_{i\ell}\Big(\sum_jk_{\ell j}x_j\Big)\\&=\sum_\ell m_{i\ell}(K\mathbf x)_\ell,\end{aligned}$$

which is entry $i$ of $M(K\mathbf x)$. Applied to each column of a third matrix, the same identity makes a product of three matrices associative. Holding the three portfolios of [[ex:xk]] in proportions $\mathbf x$ therefore gives the returns of the combined weights $K\mathbf x$.

The product is **not commutative**. The product $KM$ may not exist, and when both products exist they usually differ:

$$\begin{gathered}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\\ \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.\end{gathered}$$

> [!proposition] Transpose of a product
> For an $n\times N$ matrix $M$ and an $N\times m$ matrix $K$,
> $$(MK)^{\top}=K^{\top}M^{\top}. \tag{8.15}$$

*Proof.* The entry in row $i$ and column $j$ of $(MK)^{\top}$ is entry $(j,i)$ of $MK$. By (8.14) it equals

$$\sum_\ell m_{j\ell}k_{\ell i}=\sum_\ell(K^{\top})_{i\ell}(M^{\top})_{\ell j},$$

which is entry $(i,j)$ of $K^{\top}M^{\top}$. $\blacksquare$

For a vector, (8.15) gives $(X\mathbf w)^{\top}=\mathbf w^{\top}X^{\top}$, a row.

**Identity.** The $N\times N$ **identity matrix** $I$ has ones on its diagonal and zeros elsewhere, so $MI=M$ and $IK=K$. It plays the role of 1. Dividing by a matrix means multiplying by its inverse, which exists only sometimes ([[LM9]]). The dot products of all centred columns form a square table $\tilde X^{\top}\tilde X$; [[LM10]] divides it by $T$ and calls it the sample covariance matrix.

**Summing over dates.** Multiplying a column by $\mathbf 1^{\top}$ adds its entries. For a fixed book the sum telescopes, because the daily price changes sum to the change over the period:

$$\mathbf 1^{\top}Y\mathbf h=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Delta\mathbf P_t^{\top}\mathbf h=(\mathbf P_T-\mathbf P_0)^{\top}\mathbf h .$$

This sum is the book's exact P&L over the period. For a daily-rebalanced portfolio, $\mathbf 1^{\top}X\mathbf w$ is only the sum of the daily returns, and that sum is no period return, because returns compound.

## Summary {-}

- A vector lists numbers in a fixed order, each with a unit and an index of asset and date; vectors add only when all three agree.
- The dot product (8.1) gives P&L, $\mathbf h^{\top}\Delta\mathbf P$ (8.2), and portfolio return, $\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf R$ (8.3), for simple returns only. On 7 April 2025 the case book made NT\$57,400, all but NT\$3,400 of it on the bond line.
- The P&L is exactly $W_0\mathbf f^{\top}\mathbf R$ (8.4). Net exposure 0.295 and gross exposure 1.147 describe the same book.
- The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality (8.6), with equality only for parallel vectors, defines angles (8.7) and gives the triangle inequality (8.8).
- A sample correlation is the cosine of the angle between centred vectors (8.9): 13.0° for 0050 and 00631L, 96.1° for 0050 and 00679B, 41.0° to 77.4° across the sector pairs.
- For long-only weights $s_p\le\sum_iw_is_i$ (8.11): 10.91% against 16.29% for the mix of [[LM7]].
- In a data matrix, $X\mathbf w$ (8.12) is a daily-rebalanced portfolio's return series and $Y\mathbf h$ (8.13) a fixed book's P&L. The case book lost NT\$124,200 over the week, and its unhedged bond line NT\$200,000.
- Matrix products (8.14) are associative but not commutative, and $(MK)^{\top}=K^{\top}M^{\top}$ (8.15). Summed daily returns are no period return.

## Solutions {-}

1. **B** is correct. Simple returns combine with weights, (8.3). A fails because log returns do not combine with weights: the portfolio's log return is $\ln(1+\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf R)$. C multiplies contracts and shares by pure numbers, so $\mathbf h^{\top}\mathbf R$ is neither a return nor a P&L; the P&L is $\mathbf h^{\top}\Delta\mathbf P$ (8.2).

2. The lines are $2\times200\times(18{,}169-19{,}167)=-399{,}200$ and $200{,}000\times(29.33-30.42)=-218{,}000$, so the book lost NT\$617,200 on 8 April.

3. The position values on 2 April are $2\times200\times21{,}296=8{,}518{,}400$, 0 and $200{,}000\times29.88=5{,}976{,}000$, so $\mathbf f=(0.85184,\ 0,\ 0.5976)^{\top}$. Net and gross exposure are both 1.44944, because every position is long. With the returns of [[ex:case]], $\mathbf f^{\top}\mathbf R=-0.085160+0.010800=-0.074360$, and $W_0\,\mathbf f^{\top}\mathbf R=-743{,}600$. Directly, $2\times(-425{,}800)+200{,}000\times0.54=-743{,}600$ NT\$.

4. The norms are $\sqrt{0.38}=0.6164$ and $\sqrt{0.34}=0.5831$. The difference is $(-0.1,\ 0,\ 0.1)^{\top}$, so the distance is 0.1414, and relative to the first norm it is $0.1414/0.6164=0.2294$. The 1-norm of the difference, 0.2, measures the trade's turnover. The trade sells 10% of capital in one asset and buys 10% in another, a two-way turnover of 20% (10% one way).

5. By the derivation of (8.8), equality needs $\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y=\lVert\mathbf x\rVert\lVert\mathbf y\rVert$. If $\mathbf y=\mathbf 0$, it holds, and $\mathbf y=0\cdot\mathbf x$. Otherwise equality in (8.6) makes $\mathbf x=a\mathbf y$ for a number $a$, and then $\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y=a\lVert\mathbf y\rVert^2$ equals $\lVert\mathbf x\rVert\lVert\mathbf y\rVert=\lvert a\rvert\lVert\mathbf y\rVert^2$ only when $a\ge0$. Conversely, if $\mathbf x=a\mathbf y$ with $a\ge0$, both sides equal $(1+a)\lVert\mathbf y\rVert$.

6. The dot product is $2+2+4=8$ and both lengths are 3, so $\cos\theta=8/9$ and $\theta=27.27^\circ$. The proof of (8.6) gives $a=\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y/\lVert\mathbf y\rVert^2=8/9$. Check: $\mathbf x-\tfrac89\mathbf y=(-\tfrac79,\ \tfrac{10}9,\ \tfrac29)^{\top}$, whose dot product with $\mathbf y$ is $(-14+10+4)/9=0$.

7. Both means are 2, so $\tilde{\mathbf x}=(0,\ 2,\ -2,\ 0)^{\top}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf y}=(-1,\ 1,\ -1,\ 1)^{\top}$. Then $\tilde{\mathbf x}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf y}=4$, $\lVert\tilde{\mathbf x}\rVert=\sqrt8$ and $\lVert\tilde{\mathbf y}\rVert=2$, so $\rho=4/(2\sqrt8)=0.7071$, an angle of 45°. With divisor 4, the covariance is 1 and the standard deviations are $\sqrt2$ and 1, so $\rho=1/\sqrt2$. With divisor 3, they are $4/3$, $\sqrt{8/3}$ and $\sqrt{4/3}$, and $\rho=(4/3)/\sqrt{32/9}=1/\sqrt2$ again.

8. **C** is correct. The angle is $\arccos(-0.106)=96.1^\circ$, a little more than a right angle. A, 83.9°, drops the sign of $\rho$. B treats a small correlation as zero.

9. By (8.10), the root-mean-square gap per date between the standardized series is $\sqrt{2(1-\rho)}$. For 0.7547, $\theta=\arccos0.7547=41.00^\circ$ and $\sqrt{2(1-0.7547)}=0.7004$. For 0.2175, $\theta=77.44^\circ$ and $\sqrt{2(1-0.2175)}=1.2510$. The root-mean-square daily gap is 0.70 standard deviations for the two electronics sectors and 1.25 for the least related pair.

10. **A** is correct. The daily P&Ls of a fixed book telescope to $\mathbf h^{\top}(\mathbf P_{\text{11 Apr}}-\mathbf P_{\text{2 Apr}})$. B ignores compounding: for the 60/40 mix the sum is −5.98% while the week's return is −6.32%. C ignores the drift of the exposures, so it is exact only on 7 April. On 8 April it gives a P&L of −22,628 against the book's −33,000, a miss of NT\$10,372.

## Glossary {-}

| Term | 中文 | Meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Vector | 向量 | Ordered list of numbers, a column here, with a unit per entry and an index of assets and dates. |
| Linear combination | 線性組合 | Sum of vectors times numbers, such as $\sum_jw_j\mathbf x_j$. |
| Dot product | 內積 | $\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y=\sum_kx_ky_k$, (8.1). |
| Exposure | 曝險 | Position value ÷ equity, $f_i=h_iP_{i,0}/W_0$. |
| Net exposure | 淨曝險 | $\mathbf 1^{\top}\mathbf f$: longs minus shorts. |
| Gross exposure | 總曝險 | $\lVert\mathbf f\rVert_1$: longs plus shorts. |
| Euclidean norm | 歐氏範數 | Length $\lVert\mathbf x\rVert=\sqrt{\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf x}$, (8.5). |
| Unit vector | 單位向量 | Vector of length 1. |
| Cauchy–Schwarz inequality | Cauchy–Schwarz 不等式 | $\lvert\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y\rvert\le\lVert\mathbf x\rVert\lVert\mathbf y\rVert$, (8.6). |
| Angle | 夾角 | $\cos\theta=\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf y/(\lVert\mathbf x\rVert\lVert\mathbf y\rVert)$, (8.7). |
| Orthogonal | 正交 | With dot product zero. |
| Triangle inequality | 三角不等式 | $\lVert\mathbf x+\mathbf y\rVert\le\lVert\mathbf x\rVert+\lVert\mathbf y\rVert$, (8.8). |
| Centred vector | 中心化向量 | A series minus its mean, $\tilde{\mathbf x}=\mathbf x-\bar x\mathbf 1$. |
| Correlation | 相關係數 | Cosine of the angle between centred vectors, (8.9). |
| Matrix | 矩陣 | Rectangular table of numbers. |
| Data matrix | 資料矩陣 | $T\times N$ matrix of returns, a row per date and a column per asset. |
| Transpose | 轉置 | Matrix with rows and columns swapped. |
| Daily rebalancing | 每日再平衡 | Resetting the weights at every close; its returns are $X\mathbf w$, (8.12). |
| Matrix product | 矩陣乘積 | $(MK)_{ij}=\sum_\ell m_{i\ell}k_{\ell j}$, (8.14). |
| Identity matrix | 單位矩陣 | Ones on the diagonal, zeros elsewhere; $MI=M$. |
