## Introduction

[[LM12]] fitted lines by least squares and read their coefficients as geometry. Here the same coefficients are estimates, which another sample would change. Each gets a standard error, which depends on what the model's errors do.

> [!case] Is Semiconductor's alpha real?
> From 1 December 2008 to 8 October 2026 the TWSE Semiconductor price index rose 38.1-fold, against 10.9-fold for the TAIEX price index. Regress Semiconductor's 4,382 daily returns on the TAIEX's. The slope, the sector's beta, is 1.207. The intercept is 2.10 bp a day, 5.16% a year, with a classical t-stat of 2.10. That clears the familiar 1.96 ([[ex:case]]).
>
> Before calling it skill, answer three questions. How sure is the beta? Its standard error is 0.0088 by the textbook formula, 0.0129 by White's and 0.0185 by Newey–West's. The beta was 1.089 in the sample's first half and 1.294 in the second (Newey–West standard errors 0.023 and 0.029). What does the intercept measure, when price indices omit dividends and the regression omits interest? And how many sectors were tried? Of 28, one intercept clears 1.96; chance alone predicts 1.4.

## The Linear Model and Its Assumptions {lo=1}

[[LM12]]'s four points, regressor values 0, 1, 2, 3 and responses $\mathbf y=(1,3,2,6)^{\top}$, gave the line $\hat\alpha=0.9$, $\hat\beta=1.4$. Four other points from the same process would give another line. How far the line can move is a question about a model of the data.

Write the model and its least-squares estimate as

$$\mathbf y=X\boldsymbol\beta+\boldsymbol\varepsilon,\qquad \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}\mathbf y. \tag{23.1}$$

Here $X$ is the $T\times d$ design with first column $\mathbf 1$, and $\boldsymbol\varepsilon$ is the unobserved error. The estimate is [[LM12]]'s solution of (12.1), the **ordinary least squares** (OLS) estimator. It is unique when $X$ has full column rank, so that $X^{\top}X$ is invertible ([[LM10]]).

> [!theorem] Mean and variance of OLS
> Let $\mathbf y=X\boldsymbol\beta+\boldsymbol\varepsilon$, where
> 1. the design has full column rank $d$, and
> 2. the errors have conditional mean zero, $\E[\boldsymbol\varepsilon\mid X]=\mathbf 0$.
>
> Then $\E[\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\mid X]=\boldsymbol\beta$. If also
> 3. the errors are homoskedastic and uncorrelated, $\Var(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\mid X)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2I$,
>
> then
> $$\begin{gathered}\E[\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\mid X]=\boldsymbol\beta,\\ \Var(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\mid X)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2(X^{\top}X)^{-1}.\end{gathered} \tag{23.2}$$

*Proof.* Substituting the model gives $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}-\boldsymbol\beta=(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}\boldsymbol\varepsilon$, a matrix fixed given $X$ times $\boldsymbol\varepsilon$. Its conditional expectation is $\mathbf 0$ by condition 2, so $\E[\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\mid X]=\boldsymbol\beta$. By (16.4) and condition 3,

$$\begin{aligned}\Var(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\mid X)&=(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}(\sigma_\varepsilon^2I)X(X^{\top}X)^{-1}\\&=\sigma_\varepsilon^2(X^{\top}X)^{-1}.\qquad\blacksquare\end{aligned}$$

The model itself is the linearity assumption; normal errors are an optional fourth. In a simple regression, $d=2$, and the slope's entry of $(X^{\top}X)^{-1}$ is $1/(Ts_x^2)$, so $\Var(\hat\beta\mid X)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2/(Ts_x^2)$. The variance falls like $1/T$, the rate [[LM18]] assumed for a slope's batch errors.

The error variance is unknown. Its estimate divides the residual sum of squares by $T-d$:

$$s_\varepsilon^2:=\frac{\text{RSS}}{T-d},\qquad \E[s_\varepsilon^2\mid X]=\sigma_\varepsilon^2. \tag{23.3}$$

The residual is $\hat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}=(I-\Pi)\mathbf y=(I-\Pi)\boldsymbol\varepsilon$, because $\Pi X=X$. Since $I-\Pi$ is symmetric and idempotent, $\text{RSS}=\boldsymbol\varepsilon^{\top}(I-\Pi)\boldsymbol\varepsilon$. Its expectation sums each entry of $I-\Pi$ times the matching entry of $\Var(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\mid X)$. By condition 3 those entries are $\sigma_\varepsilon^2$ on the diagonal and 0 off it, which gives $\sigma_\varepsilon^2\operatorname{tr}(I-\Pi)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2(T-d)$ by (12.2). For normal errors the maximum-likelihood estimate divides RSS by $T$, as [[LM20]]'s normal MLE (20.5) does.

> [!example] Four points with an error bar {#fourpts}
> For [[LM12]]'s four points ($\hat\alpha=0.9$, $\hat\beta=1.4$, $\text{RSS}=4.2$), compute $s_\varepsilon^2$, the standard errors of both coefficients and a 95% interval for $\beta$ under normal errors.
>
> > [!solution]
> > Here $T-d=2$, so $s_\varepsilon^2=4.2/2=2.1$. The regressor has $\bar x=1.5$ and $Ts_x^2=5$. By (23.2), $\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta)=\sqrt{2.1/5}=0.648$ and $\operatorname{SE}(\hat\alpha)=\sqrt{2.1\,(1/4+1.5^2/5)}=1.212$. The t-stat of $\hat\beta$ is $1.4/0.648=2.16$.
> >
> > With normal errors the t-stat follows $t_2$ (Section 3), whose 97.5% quantile is 4.303. The interval $1.4\pm4.303\times0.648$ runs from −1.39 to 4.19 and contains 0. The normal quantile 1.96 would give 0.13 to 2.67 and exclude 0. With two residual degrees of freedom, the normal reference misleads.

> [!theorem] Gauss–Markov
> Under the three conditions of (23.2), let $M\mathbf y$ be any linear estimator of $\boldsymbol\beta$ that is unbiased for every $\boldsymbol\beta$, with $M$ a $d\times T$ matrix fixed given $X$. Then $\Var(M\mathbf y\mid X)-\Var(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\mid X)$ is positive semi-definite: OLS is the **best linear unbiased estimator** (Greene 2018, ch. 4; proof in the Deep Dive).

No linear unbiased estimator gives any combination $\mathbf a^{\top}\boldsymbol\beta$ a smaller variance. The theorem says nothing about nonlinear estimators, nor about errors that break condition 3, as daily returns do (Section 4).

> [!deepdive]- Why OLS is best among linear unbiased estimators
> Since $\E[M\mathbf y\mid X]=MX\boldsymbol\beta$, unbiasedness for every $\boldsymbol\beta$ requires $MX=I$. Write $M=(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}+K$; then $KX=0$. By (16.4),
> $$\begin{aligned}\Var(M\mathbf y\mid X)&=\sigma_\varepsilon^2MM^{\top}\\&=\sigma_\varepsilon^2(X^{\top}X)^{-1}+\sigma_\varepsilon^2KK^{\top},\end{aligned}$$
> because the cross terms $(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}K^{\top}$ vanish with $KX=0$. The extra term is positive semi-definite, since for every $\mathbf a$
> $$\mathbf a^{\top}KK^{\top}\mathbf a=\lVert K^{\top}\mathbf a\rVert^2\ge0.\qquad\blacksquare$$

> [!pitfall] Regress returns, not prices
> Regressing one trending price series on another can show a strong fit with no relation at all. [[LM25]] treats this spurious regression; this module regresses returns.

> [!zh] 迴歸是帶誤差的量測
> 迴歸（regression）把資料寫成 $\mathbf y=X\boldsymbol\beta+\boldsymbol\varepsilon$，其中係數（coefficient）是要估計的常數，誤差（error）是看不到的隨機部分。普通最小平方法（ordinary least squares）的估計量（estimator）就是 [[LM12]] 用投影（projection）求得的解；只要誤差在給定 $X$ 時平均為零，它就是不偏估計量（unbiased estimator）。殘差（residual）只剩 $T-d$ 個維度（dimension），所以誤差的變異數（variance）要用 $\text{RSS}/(T-d)$ 估計。誤差若變異數齊一（homoskedastic）且互不相關（uncorrelated），高斯-馬可夫定理（Gauss–Markov theorem）保證 OLS 是最佳線性不偏估計量（best linear unbiased estimator）。這個保證只比較線性不偏的估計量，而且誤差變異數一旦逐日不同就不再成立。

> [!kc]
> [[LM12]] fitted TAIEX's 22 daily returns of December 2008 on the 28 sectors with an intercept: 29 columns of rank 22, an exact fit. What is $s_\varepsilon$?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > The estimate $s_\varepsilon$ does not exist. The fit is exact, so $\text{RSS}=0$, and the rank leaves $22-22=0$ residual degrees of freedom, so (23.3) reads $0/0$. An exact fit has no error estimate, hence no standard errors: it is no evidence.

## Standard Errors, Intervals and Hat Values {lo=2}

Replace $\sigma_\varepsilon^2$ by $s_\varepsilon^2$ in (23.2): the square roots of the diagonal of $s_\varepsilon^2(X^{\top}X)^{-1}$ are the **classical standard errors**. A coefficient divided by its standard error is a t-stat, as in [[LM21]]'s test of a mean (21.1).

> [!result] t-stats and intervals
> Under the conditions of (23.2), test $H_0:\beta_j=\beta_{j,\text{null}}$ with
> $$\begin{gathered}\text{t-stat}(\hat\beta_j)=\frac{\hat\beta_j-\beta_{j,\text{null}}}{\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_j)},\\ \text{interval }\ \hat\beta_j\pm1.96\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_j).\end{gathered} \tag{23.4}$$
> With normal errors the t-stat follows $t_{T-d}$ under $H_0$ (stated without proof; it is the regression form of [[LM20]]'s $t_{T-1}$ law). For large $T$, with finite fourth moments, it is approximately $\mathcal N(0,1)$ without normality (stated without proof; (17.5) is the reason). The interval then covers $\beta_j$ with probability close to 95%.

Later t-stats and p-values are two-sided with the normal reference. Daily returns may lack a finite fourth moment ([[LM19]]), so these normal references are approximations. Every later t-stat comes from a fit with $T-d\ge183$ (the widget's 2026 window). The 97.5% quantile of $t_{183}$, 1.973, exceeds the normal 1.960 by 0.7%. Each t-stat names its standard error: classical, White or Newey–West.

> [!example] The case by hand {#semi}
> Compute Semiconductor's classical standard error of beta from $s_\varepsilon$ and $s_x$, the 95% intervals with the classical and Newey–West errors, and the intercept's p-value.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The residual standard deviation is $s_\varepsilon=0.66239\%$ with divisor $T-2$ (0.66224% with divisor $T$), and the TAIEX's returns have $s_x=1.137\%$. So
> > $$\begin{aligned}\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta)&=\frac{s_\varepsilon}{\sqrt T\,s_x}\\&=\frac{0.66239\%}{\sqrt{4382}\times1.137\%}=0.0088.\end{aligned}$$
> > The classical interval $1.2073\pm1.96\times0.0088$ runs from 1.190 to 1.225. With the Newey–West error 0.0185 it runs from 1.171 to 1.244 ([[ex:betas]]).
> >
> > The intercept's classical t-stat is 2.10, with p-value $2\Phi(-2.10)=0.036$ by (21.2). It is one test of 28 on the same data, which Section 7 counts.

R-squared, (12.4), is 0.8112 here. As a statistic it has its own sampling error, and it never falls when a column is added. **Adjusted R-squared**, $1-(1-\text{R-squared})(T-1)/(T-d)$, charges for columns: 0.81119 against 0.81124 (from unrounded values). The **F statistic** tests that all coefficients but the intercept are zero; with one regressor it is the squared t-stat, $137.20^2=18{,}824$. R-squared describes a fit and tests nothing: [[LM12]]'s 0.99912 for TAIEX on its own sectors was an accounting identity.

A new observation $y_0=\mathbf x_0^{\top}\boldsymbol\beta+\varepsilon_0$ has an error of variance $\sigma_\varepsilon^2$, independent of the sample. By (16.4), the fitted value $\mathbf x_0^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ and the prediction error have

$$\begin{gathered}\Var(\mathbf x_0^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\mid X)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2h_0,\\ h_0:=\mathbf x_0^{\top}(X^{\top}X)^{-1}\mathbf x_0,\\ \Var(y_0-\mathbf x_0^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\mid X)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2(1+h_0).\end{gathered} \tag{23.5}$$

Under (23.2)'s conditions, with $s_\varepsilon$ in place of $\sigma_\varepsilon$, ±1.96 standard errors give a **confidence interval** for the fitted line, approximately for large $T$. The **prediction interval** for a new day also needs normal errors, because one day's error does not average out.

At a sample point, $h_t:=\mathbf x_t^{\top}(X^{\top}X)^{-1}\mathbf x_t$ is the diagonal entry of $\Pi$ in row $t$, the **hat value** of date $t$. It is the weight of $y_t$ in its own fitted value $\hat y_t$, a measure of influence. The hat values sum to $\operatorname{tr}\Pi=d$, so their average is $d/T$. Leverage keeps the book's meaning, $f>1$.

> [!zh] 標準誤、信賴區間與 hat value
> 係數（coefficient）除以它的標準誤（standard error）就是 t 統計量（t-statistic）；誤差（error）為常態分布（normal distribution）時它服從 $t_{T-d}$，樣本大時近似標準常態分布。估計值（estimate）加減 1.96 個標準誤，就是 95% 信賴區間（confidence interval）。判定係數（R-squared）只描述配適（fit），本身也有抽樣誤差（sampling error），不是檢定（test）。預測新的一天時，除了係數的誤差，還要加上當天自己的誤差，所以預測區間（prediction interval）寬得多。hat value 衡量一個點對自己配適值（fitted value）的影響力（influence）：2025 年 4 月 7 日的 hat value 是當年平均的 21 倍，這一天就讓全年的 beta 移動 2.8 個標準誤。

## Heteroskedasticity and White's Standard Errors {lo=3}

The classical standard errors rest on condition 3 of (23.2): every day's error has the same variance. Daily returns break it: large errors come on days of large index moves, far from the regressor's mean, where they move the slope most.

Keep conditions 1 and 2, and let $\Sigma_\varepsilon:=\Var(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\mid X)$ be any covariance matrix. Applying (16.4) to $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}-\boldsymbol\beta=(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}\boldsymbol\varepsilon$ gives the **sandwich**

$$\Var(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\mid X)=(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}\Sigma_\varepsilon X(X^{\top}X)^{-1}. \tag{23.6}$$

The outer factors are the bread and the middle the filling. When the errors are uncorrelated, $\Sigma_\varepsilon$ is diagonal and the filling is $\sum_t\Var(\varepsilon_t\mid X)\,\mathbf x_t\mathbf x_t^{\top}$.

**White's estimator** replaces each unknown variance by the squared residual, so the filling becomes $\sum_t\hat\varepsilon_t^2\mathbf x_t\mathbf x_t^{\top}$ (statsmodels' HC0). One squared residual estimates its variance badly; the sum over $T$ days averages the errors out. White (1980) shows that the estimate is consistent for independent rows with finite moments as $T$ grows (stated without proof). Variance that changes from day to day is what [[LM26]] models; White's standard errors need no model of it.

> [!example] LM18's slope on 10,000 paths {#lm18slope}
> [[LM18]] simulated 10,000 five-year paths of iid normal log returns ($m=1{,}231$, `default_rng([3, 20261011])`) and regressed the growth factor $G$ on $\ln G$. It reported $\hat\beta=2.18$ with batch standard error 0.01. Compare the classical, White and batch standard errors with the model's value.
>
> > [!solution]
> > OLS gives $\hat\beta=2.1759$, with classical standard error 0.0067 and White's 0.0152. LM18's batch value from 20 batches of 500 is 0.0113. In the model $\ln G$ is exactly normal, so (23.6) with the model's moments in place of the sample's, for 10,000 paths, is an integral over a normal law. Quadrature gives 0.0157 around the true slope $\E G=2.1584$.
> >
> > The classical formula gives 43% of that value, and White's 0.97 of it. The batch value is 0.72 of it. By [[LM18]]'s rule, a standard deviation of 20 values errs by about $1/\sqrt{40}=16\%$, so the batch value misses by under two such errors.
> >
> > Since $G=e^{\ln G}$ is convex, the line misses most at both ends of $\ln G$, where the regressor is farthest from its mean. The residuals' root mean square is 0.260, 0.179 and 0.378 in the lower, middle and upper thirds of $\ln G$ (cut at $\hat F^{-1}(1/3)$ and $\hat F^{-1}(2/3)$). The classical formula ignores where the large errors sit.

Across U28's 28 market models, White's standard error of beta is 1.19 to 1.91 times the classical one. In FinancialInsurance's 2025 fit ([[eg:band]]) it is 0.106, three times the classical 0.0350, and 0.097 without 7 April 2025. That year's errors are largest on its few days of large index moves.

So [[eg:band]]'s bands are too narrow at −5%: White's standard error of the fitted value there is 0.557 points, not 0.1865. The two 2025 days with the TAIEX at or below −5% have residuals of −5.7 and −3.0 $s_\varepsilon$, outside the prediction band.

## Overlapping Returns and Newey–West {lo=3}

A regression of 20-day returns can start a sum on every day: days 1–20, 2–21 and so on. Neighbouring sums share 19 days, so their errors are autocorrelated, and condition 3 fails again. Semiconductor's 4,363 overlapping log-return windows leave residuals with sample autocorrelation (19.5) $\hat\rho_1=0.953$, close to the 19/20 of iid days.

With autocorrelated rows the filling of (23.6) also collects covariances between days. Divided by $T$, it tends to the **long-run variance** of the products $\varepsilon_t\mathbf x_t$,

$$\begin{gathered}\Gamma_0+\sum_{k\ge1}\big(\Gamma_k+\Gamma_k^{\top}\big),\\ \Gamma_k:=\E\big[\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_{t-k}\mathbf x_t\mathbf x_{t-k}^{\top}\big],\end{gathered}$$

the matrix form of (19.7)'s limit. Newey and West (1987) estimate it with declining weights:

$$\begin{gathered}\hat\Gamma_0+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\Big(1-\frac{k}{\ell+1}\Big)\big(\hat\Gamma_k+\hat\Gamma_k^{\top}\big),\\ \hat\Gamma_k:=\frac1T\sum_{t=k+1}^{T}\hat\varepsilon_t\hat\varepsilon_{t-k}\mathbf x_t\mathbf x_{t-k}^{\top},\end{gathered} \tag{23.7}$$

which replaces $X^{\top}\Sigma_\varepsilon X/T$ in (23.6). statsmodels calls $1-k/(\ell+1)$ Bartlett weights. With them the estimate is positive semi-definite. It is consistent when the products' dependence fades fast enough, their moments are bounded and $\ell$ grows more slowly than $T^{1/4}$ (both stated without proof; Newey and West 1987).

The book fixes the lag in advance as $\ell=\lfloor4(T/100)^{2/9}\rfloor$, whose exponent 2/9 is below 1/4: 9 for 4,382 returns, 7 for a half, 4 for a year. It is statsmodels' default HAC lag and the pilot lag of Newey and West (1994), not the data-based lag they recommend. A fixed rule avoids choosing the lag that gives the wanted answer.

> [!deepdive]- Why overlapping sums need a long lag
> Let the daily errors and the regressor's daily returns be iid with mean zero and variances $\sigma_\varepsilon^2$ and $\sigma_x^2$, the two series independent. Two sums of $m$ days that end $k<m$ days apart share $m-k$ days. By (16.4) the error sums then have covariance $(m-k)\sigma_\varepsilon^2$, and the regressor sums $(m-k)\sigma_x^2$. By (16.1) the slope's entry of $\Gamma_k$ is their product:
> $$\begin{gathered}(m-k)^2\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sigma_x^2\quad(k<m),\\ 0\quad(k\ge m).\end{gathered}$$
> The classical formula keeps $\Gamma_0$ alone, $m^2\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sigma_x^2$. The long-run variance adds the other lags,
> $$\begin{aligned}&2\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}(m-k)^2\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sigma_x^2\\&\quad=\frac{(m-1)m(2m-1)}{3}\,\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sigma_x^2,\end{aligned}$$
> so the true variance is $(2m^2+1)/(3m)$ times the classical one. At $m=20$ that is 13.35, a factor 3.65 in the standard error. A lag below $m-1$ drops terms, and Bartlett weights shrink the rest: in large samples $\ell=19$ recovers a factor 3.17, and $\ell=38$ a factor 3.41.

The lag also diagnoses a drifting beta ([[ex:haclag]]). With the full-sample residuals, Semiconductor's daily Newey–West standard error keeps growing: 0.0129 at $\ell=0$ (White's), 0.0185 at 9, 0.0275 at 50 and 0.0390 at 200. With a drifting beta, the products $\hat\varepsilon_t\mathbf x_t$ of the full-sample fit keep their sign for long stretches. Residuals from each calendar year's own line give 0.0152, 0.0160 and 0.0123 at lags 9, 50 and 200 instead.

> [!pitfall] A standard error that grows with the lag
> When a Newey–West standard error keeps growing with $\ell$, the constant coefficient itself is in doubt: the full-sample beta averages a moving target (Section 6). On overlapping sums, use $\ell\ge m-1$ or non-overlapping sums, and report both.

[[LM22]]'s block bootstrap is the resampling alternative to (23.7), and [[LM33]] applies the long-run variance to a Sharpe ratio.

## The Market Model: Beta, Alpha and Hedging {lo=4,5}

The **market model** regresses a sector's return on the market's:

$$R_{i,t}=\alpha+\beta R_{\text{mkt},t}+\varepsilon_t.$$

The market model's slope estimates the sector's beta, the population slope of (16.8). The model describes co-movement and makes no claim about expected returns.

The capital asset pricing model (CAPM) makes one. With total returns and a risk-free rate $r_f$, it states $\E[R_i]-r_f=\beta_i(\E[R_{\text{mkt}}]-r_f)$ for every asset (Campbell, Lo and MacKinlay 1997, ch. 5; Sharpe 1964 and Lintner 1965 are its origins). **Jensen's alpha** $\alpha_{\mathrm J}$ is the market model's intercept on total excess returns (Jensen 1968), zero under the CAPM.

A total return adds the dividend yield, $q_i/A$ a day if spread evenly, so Jensen's regression reads

$$\begin{aligned}R_{i,t}+\frac{q_i-r_f}{A}=\alpha_{\mathrm J}&+\beta\Big(R_{\text{mkt},t}+\frac{q_{\text{mkt}}-r_f}{A}\Big)\\&+\varepsilon_t.\end{aligned}$$

Collecting constants gives U28's raw intercept

$$\alpha=\alpha_{\mathrm J}+\frac{(1-\beta)\,r_f}{A}-\frac{q_i-\beta q_{\text{mkt}}}{A} \tag{23.8}$$

per day, with $r_f$, $q_i$ and $q_{\text{mkt}}$ simple rates a year.

Over U28's window the TAIEX total-return index rose 20.23-fold and the price index 10.91-fold. Their mean daily return gap, times $A$, gives $q_{\text{mkt}}=3.46\%$ a year. For Semiconductor ($\beta=1.207$), a 2% risk-free rate, [[LM6]]'s proxy, adds 0.17 bp a day to Jensen's alpha, and equal dividend yields subtract 0.29 bp. Jensen's alpha is then 1.98 bp. Each point of sector yield below the index's subtracts another 0.41 bp; the sectors' own yields are not in the data.

A beta is an average over the sample. [[ex:betas]] sets the 28 full-sample betas beside the halves'. With Newey–West errors ($\ell=7$ in each half), the change between the halves in standard errors,

$$z=\frac{\hat\beta_{\text{second}}-\hat\beta_{\text{first}}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{SE}_{\text{first}}^2+\operatorname{SE}_{\text{second}}^2}},$$

exceeds 1.96 in absolute value for 25 of 28 sectors. FinancialInsurance fell from 1.156 to 0.578 and Cement from 0.982 to 0.463; Semiconductor rose from 1.089 to 1.294. The halves' betas correlate 0.653.

## A Factor Named in Advance {lo=7}

[[LM11]] found principal components and warned that nothing makes its second component "electronics". An economic factor model names its factors before looking. Here the factor is the Electronic index's return minus the TAIEX's, constructed by this module; Electronic aggregates the electronics sectors, Semiconductor among them.

$$\begin{aligned}R_{i,t}=\alpha&+\beta_{\text{mkt}}R_{\text{mkt},t}\\&+\beta_{\text{el}}\big(R_{\text{el},t}-R_{\text{mkt},t}\big)+\varepsilon_t\end{aligned}$$

Why not regress on the TAIEX and Electronic directly? Their returns correlate 0.9696. With two regressors of sample correlation $\hat\rho$, each slope's variance is its value with uncorrelated regressors times the **variance inflation factor**

$$\operatorname{VIF}=\frac{1}{1-\hat\rho^2}. \tag{23.9}$$

The factor comes from (23.2): the slopes' block of $(X^{\top}X)^{-1}$ is $(T\hat\Sigma)^{-1}$ by (12.3), and inverting a 2×2 correlation matrix gives diagonal entries $1/(1-\hat\rho^2)$. For the raw pair the VIF is 16.7, and the condition number of their correlation matrix, $(1+\hat\rho)/(1-\hat\rho)$, is 65; (12.6) relates it to the data's. The factor correlates only 0.365 with the TAIEX: VIF 1.15, condition number 2.15.

The raw pair and the factor pair give the same fitted values and residuals (Problem 10).

## Regression in Python {lo=8}

NumPy computes every number here. The checks use statsmodels with every option passed, because its defaults differ from the book's.

```python
import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from statsmodels.stats.multitest import multipletests

# y: a sector's returns, x: the TAIEX's (U28, T = 4,382)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(x)), x])
b = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]
e = y - X @ b
s2 = e @ e / (len(y) - X.shape[1])          # divisor T - d
XtXi = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
se_classical = np.sqrt(s2 * np.diag(XtXi))
u = X * e[:, None]
se_white = np.sqrt(np.diag(XtXi @ (u.T @ u) @ XtXi))   # HC0

white = sm.OLS(y, X).fit(cov_type="HC0")
nw = sm.OLS(y, X).fit(cov_type="HAC",
                      cov_kwds={"maxlags": 9, "use_correction": False})
print(nw.bse, nw.use_t)                     # use_t False: normal p-values
# pvals: the 28 intercepts' two-sided p-values (Problem 12)
holm = multipletests(pvals, alpha=0.05, method="holm")[0]
```

`get_robustcov_results()` without arguments returns HC1, which multiplies HC0's variance by $T/(T-d)$. In `OLS.fit` the HAC option needs `maxlags` and leaves `use_correction` False; `cov_hac` defaults to True. `multipletests` defaults to Holm–Šidák (`"hs"`), so name the method.

## Summary {-}

- OLS is unbiased when the errors have mean zero given $X$, (23.2); $s_\varepsilon^2$ divides RSS by $T-d$, (23.3).
- Classical standard errors (23.4) assume homoskedastic, uncorrelated errors; hat values measure influence, (23.5).
- White's sandwich (23.6) allows heteroskedasticity, and Newey–West (23.7) autocorrelation too.
- A Newey–West standard error that grows with the lag signals a drifting coefficient.
- A raw intercept on price indices is not Jensen's alpha, (23.8), and betas move.
- One significant intercept among 28 is what chance gives.
- A factor named in advance avoids collinearity, measured by (23.9).

## Solutions {-}

1. **B** is correct: $s_\varepsilon=\sqrt{0.0240/(12-2)}=4.90\%$ by (23.3). A divides by $T$, which ignores the two fitted coefficients. C divides by $T-1$, [[LM20]]'s $s_{\mathrm u}$, which allows for one.

2. **A** is correct: $0.01/(0.012\sqrt{1000})=0.0264$ by (23.2). B omits $\sqrt T$. C divides by $T$, not $\sqrt T$.

3. **C** is correct: $(1.2073-1)/0.0185=11.2$. A tests $\beta=0$, $1.2073/0.0185=65.3$. B uses the classical error, $0.2073/0.0088=23.6$.

4. **A** is correct: by the Deep Dive of Section 5 the factor is
   $$\sqrt{\frac{2m^2+1}{3m}}=\sqrt{\frac{801}{60}}=3.65.$$
   B ignores the overlap. C, $\sqrt{20}=4.47$, is the non-overlapping sums' standard error relative to the overlapping classical one; overlap keeps more information, so the true factor is smaller.

5. **C** is correct. With $q_i=q_{\text{mkt}}=0$, (23.8) gives
   $$\begin{aligned}\alpha_{\mathrm J}&=\alpha-(1-\beta)r_f/A\\&=1.0-0.4\times0.02/245.48\times10^4\\&=1.0-0.326=0.674\text{ bp}.\end{aligned}$$
   A adds the term instead. B subtracts $r_f/A=0.815$ bp, ignoring $1-\beta$.

6. **B** is correct. Holm compares the ordered p-values with $0.05/5=0.01$, $0.05/4=0.0125$ and $0.05/3=0.0167$: 0.004 and 0.011 pass, 0.019 fails, and Holm stops. A is Bonferroni's count (only 0.004 is below 0.01). C is Benjamini–Hochberg's at a 5% false discovery rate: 0.030 is below $4\times0.05/5=0.04$. statsmodels' default, Holm–Šidák, also gives 2 here, but its thresholds differ, so name the method.

7. Substituting $\mathbf y$ gives $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}-\boldsymbol\beta=(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}\boldsymbol\varepsilon$. By (16.4) and condition 3,
   $$\begin{aligned}&\Var(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\mid X)\\&\quad=(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}(\sigma_\varepsilon^2I)X(X^{\top}X)^{-1}\\&\quad=\sigma_\varepsilon^2(X^{\top}X)^{-1},\end{aligned}$$
   which is (23.2). Next, RSS is a scalar, so it equals its trace:
   $$\text{RSS}=\boldsymbol\varepsilon^{\top}(I-\Pi)\boldsymbol\varepsilon=\operatorname{tr}\big((I-\Pi)\boldsymbol\varepsilon\boldsymbol\varepsilon^{\top}\big).$$
   Taking expectations given $X$, condition 3 gives
   $$\begin{aligned}\E[\text{RSS}\mid X]&=\operatorname{tr}\big((I-\Pi)\sigma_\varepsilon^2I\big)\\&=(T-d)\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\end{aligned}$$

8. With $\mathbf x_t=(1,x_t)^{\top}$, the matrix $X^{\top}X$ has entries $T$, $T\bar x$, $T\bar x$ and $T(s_x^2+\bar x^2)$, with determinant $T^2s_x^2$. Hence
   $$\begin{aligned}h_t&=\frac{T(s_x^2+\bar x^2)-2T\bar xx_t+Tx_t^2}{T^2s_x^2}\\&=\frac1T+\frac{(x_t-\bar x)^2}{Ts_x^2}.\end{aligned}$$
   Summing over $t$ gives $1+Ts_x^2/(Ts_x^2)=2=d$. For 7 April 2025,
   $$h_t=\frac1{243}+\frac{9.8059^2}{243\times1.5405^2}=0.1709,$$
   21 times the average $2/243$. The lab computes all 243 from $\Pi$.

9. The contracts are $0.75\times50{,}000{,}000/(200\times20{,}000)=9.375$, about 9, against $12.50$ for the notional hedge. The hedged variance is $1-0.5$ of the unhedged, so the standard deviation is $\sqrt{0.5}=0.7071$ of it. In-sample, the fitted beta is the best ratio by construction; a moving beta, as in [[eg:hedge]], cuts less out of sample.

10. Each design's columns are combinations of the other's, since $R_{\text{el}}=(R_{\text{el}}-R_{\text{mkt}})+R_{\text{mkt}}$. So both span the same space, and $\Pi$, the fitted values and the residuals agree. Equating the fits day by day,
    $$\begin{aligned}&\beta_{\text{mkt}}^{\text{raw}}R_{\text{mkt}}+\beta_{\text{el}}^{\text{raw}}R_{\text{el}}\\&\quad=\beta_{\text{mkt}}R_{\text{mkt}}+\beta_{\text{el}}(R_{\text{el}}-R_{\text{mkt}}),\end{aligned}$$
    gives $\beta_{\text{el}}=\beta_{\text{el}}^{\text{raw}}$ and $\beta_{\text{mkt}}=\beta_{\text{mkt}}^{\text{raw}}+\beta_{\text{el}}^{\text{raw}}$. For Semiconductor, $-0.515+1.552=1.037$. The VIFs are $1/(1-0.9696^2)=16.7$ and $1/(1-0.365^2)=1.15$.

11. Both give $\hat\beta=0.8234$, with standard errors 0.0103 classical, 0.0183 HC0 and 0.0288 HAC; NumPy and statsmodels agree to 12 digits. With `use_correction=True` the HAC value is 0.02885. `get_robustcov_results()` defaults to HC1, which scales HC0's variance by $T/(T-2)$.

12. The t-stats run from −1.935 (Optoelectronic) to 2.097 (Semiconductor); the smallest p-value is 0.036. Bonferroni, Holm and Benjamini–Hochberg reject none. Holm's smallest adjusted p-value is 1.000 and Benjamini–Hochberg's 0.742. Experiment 1 gives $\Pr(\text{at least one})=0.696$ with Monte Carlo SE 0.0015, against 0.7622 for independent tests.

## Glossary {-}

| Term | 中文 | Meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Regression | 迴歸 | A model $\mathbf y=X\boldsymbol\beta+\boldsymbol\varepsilon$ of a response on regressors, (23.1). |
| Ordinary least squares (OLS) | 普通最小平方法 | The estimate $(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}\mathbf y$, which minimizes RSS. |
| Residual | 殘差 | $\hat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}=\mathbf y-X\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$. |
| Unbiased estimator | 不偏估計量 | An estimator whose expectation is the parameter, (23.2), (23.3). |
| Gauss–Markov theorem | 高斯-馬可夫定理 | Under (23.2)'s conditions, OLS has the smallest variance among linear unbiased estimators. |
| Best linear unbiased estimator | 最佳線性不偏估計量 | OLS under the Gauss–Markov conditions. |
| Standard error | 標準誤 | The estimated standard deviation of an estimator. |
| Confidence interval | 信賴區間 | $\hat\beta_j\pm1.96\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_j)$, (23.4). |
| R-squared | 判定係數 | The share of the centred response's squared length that the fit reproduces, (12.4). |
| Hat value | — | $h_t$, the diagonal entry of $\Pi$ in row $t$: a point's influence on its own fit, (23.5). |
| Heteroskedasticity | 變異數非齊一性 | Error variances that differ across observations. |
| Robust standard error | 穩健標準誤 | A standard error from the sandwich (23.6), such as White's (HC0). |
| HAC estimator | 變異數非齊一與自我相關一致估計量 | Newey–West, (23.7): robust to heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation. |
| Autocorrelation | 自我相關 | Correlation of a series with its own past. |
| Long-run variance | — | The limit of the filling of (23.6) over $T$ for autocorrelated rows, which (23.7) estimates. |
| Market model | 市場模型 | $R_{i,t}=\alpha+\beta R_{\text{mkt},t}+\varepsilon_t$. |
| Jensen's alpha | — | The market model's intercept on total excess returns; (23.8) links it to a raw intercept. |
| Multiple testing | 多重假設檢定 | Running many tests on the same data. |
| Family-wise error rate | 族系誤差率 | The probability of at least one false rejection. |
| False discovery rate | 偽發現率 | The expected share of false rejections among all rejections. |
| Bonferroni correction | Bonferroni 校正 | Each test of a family at the family's level divided by the number of tests, (21.6). |
| Data snooping | 資料偵伺 | Searching many models or samples and keeping the best. |
| Collinearity | 共線性 | Highly correlated regressors; it inflates the slopes' variances by (23.9). |
