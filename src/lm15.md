## Introduction

[[LM5]] fitted a normal model to 0050's daily returns and found its tail far too thin. The model expected 0.058 days at or below −5% in twelve years, and 9 occurred. [[LM13]] and [[LM14]] built the language of random variables, distribution functions and moments. This module applies it to the distributions that finance uses most. Each has one to three parameters and answers some questions well and others badly; the data decide which.

> [!case] Which model for which question?
> Take TAIEX's 5,849 daily total returns from 3 January 2003 to 8 October 2026. A normal model with their mean, 0.0625%, and standard deviation, 1.2084%, expects **0.08** days at or below −5%. TAIEX had **15** ([[LM13]]'s count). A Student-t fitted by maximum likelihood, with $\nu=3.065$ degrees of freedom, expects **19.6** ([[ex:case]]).
>
> Now count the days with a move of 3% or more, either way, month by month. They average **0.612** a month, but their variance is **1.846**, three times the mean. Three months had eight: September and October 2008, and March 2020.
>
> Which model should answer which question? And what does the crowding of large moves into a few months say about independence?

## Counting Successes: Bernoulli and Binomial {lo=1}

TR23's first 20 returns, 3 January to 7 February 2003, contain 12 up days. Is 12 many? The answer needs a model for the count.

A **Bernoulli trial** has two outcomes: a success with probability $p$ and a failure with probability $1-p$. Its indicator $X=\mathbf 1\{\text{success}\}$ takes the values 1 and 0, so (14.1) gives $\E[X]=p$. Because $X^2=X$, also $\E[X^2]=p$, and (14.4) gives $\Var(X)=p-p^2=p(1-p)$.

> [!definition] Binomial distribution
> Let $n$ independent trials ((13.13) of [[LM13]]) each succeed with the same probability $p$, $0<p<1$. The number of successes $X$ has the **binomial distribution** $\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)$:
> $$\begin{gathered}\Pr(X=j)=\binom nj p^j(1-p)^{n-j},\\ j=0,1,\dots,n.\end{gathered} \tag{15.1}$$

By (13.7), one given sequence with $j$ successes has probability $p^j(1-p)^{n-j}$. There are $\binom nj$ such sequences, one for each choice of the positions of the successes, and they are disjoint, so their probabilities add. By the binomial theorem of [[LM1]]'s Deep Dive on $e$, the probabilities (15.1) sum to $(p+1-p)^n=1$.

For the moments, use $j\binom nj=n\binom{n-1}{j-1}$ and $j(j-1)\binom nj=n(n-1)\binom{n-2}{j-2}$, and then the binomial theorem again:

$$\begin{gathered}\E[X]=np\sum_{j=1}^{n}\binom{n-1}{j-1}p^{j-1}(1-p)^{n-j}=np,\\ \E[X(X-1)]=n(n-1)p^2 .\end{gathered}$$

Since $\Var(X)=\E[X(X-1)]+\E[X]-(\E[X])^2$ by (14.4),

$$\begin{aligned}\E[X]&=np,\\ \Var(X)&=n(n-1)p^2+np-n^2p^2\\&=np(1-p).\end{aligned} \tag{15.2}$$

[[LM16]] reaches this variance in one line, adding the variances of independent indicators. [[LM17]] shows why a sum of many independent trials is close to normal.

> [!example] Up days in a block {#updays}
> TR23's first 5,840 returns form 292 blocks of 20 sessions; the last 9 returns, 24 September – 8 October 2026, are excluded. The blocks contain 3,200 up days (no return is exactly 0), so their up-day frequency is $\hat p=3{,}200/5{,}840=0.548$. Model each block's count as $\operatorname{Bin}(20,0.548)$. Compute its mean and variance and the expected numbers of blocks with at most 5 and at least 16 up days.
>
> > [!solution]
> > By (15.2), the mean is $20\times0.548=10.96$ and the variance $20\times0.548\times0.452=4.95$. The first block's 12 up days lie within one standard deviation, 2.23, of the mean 10.96. The 292 blocks have mean 10.96, by construction, and variance 4.72 (divisor 292).
> >
> > By (15.1), $\Pr(X\le5)=0.00677$, or $292\times0.00677=1.98$ blocks, against 2 observed. Similarly $\Pr(X\ge16)=0.01807$, or 5.28 blocks, against 3. [[ex:binom]] compares every count.

> [!pitfall] Independent trials with one $p$
> A variance near $np(1-p)$ is consistent with independent days; it does not prove independence. In [[LM13]]'s 5,848 pairs of adjacent days, the second day fell with frequency 0.4521, and with 0.4639 after a fall. Let $X_1$, $X_2$ indicate falls on adjacent days, each with frequency 0.4521. Their covariance $\E[X_1X_2]-\E[X_1]\E[X_2]$ ([[LM16]]) is $0.4521\times(0.4639-0.4521)$ and each variance $0.4521\times(1-0.4521)$, so their correlation is $\rho_1=(0.4639-0.4521)/(1-0.4521)=0.0215$ (0.0214 from the unrounded frequencies), as for up-day indicators.
>
> If only adjacent days are correlated, [[LM16]]'s variance of a sum carries $\rho_1$ into a block of 20:
> $$\begin{gathered}20\times0.548\times0.452\\ \times\Big(1+2\cdot\tfrac{19}{20}\cdot0.0215\Big)=5.16.\end{gathered}$$
> The blocks give 4.72, below both 5.16 and 4.95: 292 blocks cannot see a dependence this small either way ([[LM21]] tests such gaps).

> [!kc]
> For $n=20$ trials, which $p$ maximizes the binomial variance? Compare the blocks' 4.95 with that maximum.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > The variance $np(1-p)$ is a downward parabola in $p$ with derivative $n(1-2p)$, zero at $p=\tfrac12$. Its maximum is $n/4=5$. The blocks' $\hat p=0.548$ lies near $\tfrac12$, so 4.95 lies near 5: there the variance hardly depends on $p$.

## Rare Events: the Poisson Distribution {lo=2}

TR23 has 175 days with $\lvert R\rvert\ge3\%$ among 5,849 ([[LM14]]); set $p$ to the frequency $175/5{,}849=0.02992$ a session. A month has about 20 sessions, so its count of large moves looks like a binomial with many trials and a small $p$. What matters is the mean $\lambda=np$. Fix $\lambda$ and let $n$ grow. At $j=0$, (1.6) with $-\lambda$ in place of its variable gives

$$\Pr(X=0)=\Big(1-\frac\lambda n\Big)^n\to e^{-\lambda}.$$

> [!definition] Poisson distribution
> A count $X$ has the **Poisson distribution** $\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)$, with $\lambda>0$, when
> $$\Pr(X=j)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^j}{j!},\qquad j=0,1,2,\dots \tag{15.3}$$

> [!theorem] Poisson limit of the binomial
> Fix $\lambda>0$ and a count $j\ge0$. Then, as $n\to\infty$,
> $$\binom nj\Big(\frac\lambda n\Big)^j\Big(1-\frac\lambda n\Big)^{n-j}\to\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^j}{j!}. \tag{15.4}$$

Deep Dive 1 proves (15.4) for every $j$. By (3.2), the probabilities (15.3) sum to $e^{-\lambda}e^{\lambda}=1$. The mean comes from the same series, after the term $j=0$ is dropped and the index shifted:

$$\E[X]=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j\,\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^j}{j!}=\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}=\lambda.$$

The same shift gives $\E[X(X-1)]=\lambda^2$ (Problem 4), so

$$\E[X]=\Var(X)=\lambda. \tag{15.5}$$

A Poisson count has a variance equal to its mean, the property that monthly counts of large moves fail in [[eg:bigmoves]].

> [!deepdive]- Deep Dive 1: why the binomial tends to the Poisson
> Fix $j$ and take $n>\lambda$. Split the binomial probability into four factors:
> $$\begin{aligned}&\binom nj\Big(\frac\lambda n\Big)^j\Big(1-\frac\lambda n\Big)^{n-j}\\&\quad=\frac{\lambda^j}{j!}\cdot\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-j+1)}{n^j}\\&\qquad\cdot\Big(1-\frac\lambda n\Big)^{n}\cdot\Big(1-\frac\lambda n\Big)^{-j}.\end{aligned}$$
> The second factor is a product of $j$ terms $(n-l)/n$, $l<j$, each tending to 1, as in [[LM1]]'s Deep Dive on $e$. The third tends to $e^{-\lambda}$ by (1.6). The fourth is a fixed power of a term that tends to 1. So the product tends to $e^{-\lambda}\lambda^j/j!$. $\blacksquare$

> [!example] From a rare binomial to the Poisson {#rare}
> A 20-session month in which each session has a large move with probability 0.02992 has a count $X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(20,0.02992)$, with mean $\lambda=0.5984$. Compare its pmf with that of $\operatorname{Poisson}(0.5984)$ at $j=0$, 1 and 2. Then take the case's monthly mean, 0.612 ($\hat\lambda$ of [[eg:bigmoves]]), and compute $(1-0.612/n)^n$ for $n=20$, 250 and 10,000, against $e^{-0.612}$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > By (15.1) and (15.3):
> >
> > | $j$ | $\operatorname{Bin}(20,0.02992)$ | $\operatorname{Poisson}(0.5984)$ |
> > |--:|--:|--:|
> > | 0 | 0.5447 | 0.5497 |
> > | 1 | 0.3360 | 0.3289 |
> > | 2 | 0.09845 | 0.09842 |
> >
> > The two pmfs differ by at most 0.0071 here. The limit at $j=0$ is slow: $(1-0.612/n)^n$ is 0.53711, 0.54186 and 0.54226 for $n=20$, 250 and 10,000, against $e^{-0.612}=0.54227$. The gap, 0.0052 at $n=20$ and 0.0004 at $n=250$, is close to $e^{-\lambda}\lambda^2/(2n)$ with $\lambda=0.612$ (0.0051 and 0.0004). The reason is that, by (3.3),
> > $$n\ln\Big(1-\frac\lambda n\Big)=-\lambda-\frac{\lambda^2}{2n}-\dots,$$
> > and the error of the approximation is of order $1/n^2$.

> [!example] Do large moves arrive like a Poisson count? {#bigmoves}
> TR23's 286 calendar months, January 2003 (18 sessions) to October 2026 (6 sessions), contain the 175 days with $\lvert R\rvert\ge3\%$. Fit a Poisson by the months' mean count, and compare expected and observed months.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The fitted mean is $\hat\lambda=175/286=0.612$. By (15.3), 155.1 of the 286 months should have no large move, and 210 had none. One large move: 94.9 expected, 38 observed. Five or more: 0.123 against 9. Eight or more: $8.1\times10^{-5}$ against 3 ([[ex:poisson]]).
> >
> > The counts have variance 1.846 (divisor 286), 3.02 times their mean, where (15.5) requires 1: they are **overdispersed**. Months of unequal length (6 to 24 sessions) do not explain it: with one $p$ for every session, [[LM16]]'s law of total variance gives only 0.60. Each limit regime alone has a ratio near 3: 2.95 before June 2015, 3.01 after.

> [!pitfall] Rare is not independent
> A Poisson count needs rare events that arrive independently, at a constant rate. Large moves are rare, but they cluster. The fitted Poisson matches the average month and misses the bad ones: 9 months with five or more large moves against 0.123 expected. Check a count's variance before modelling it as a Poisson.

> [!zh] 二項分布與卜瓦松分布：計數的模型
> 一個交易日漲或不漲，可以用柏努利分布（Bernoulli distribution）描述：成功機率 $p$，平均數（mean）$p$，變異數（variance）$p(1-p)$。$n$ 次獨立（independent）而成功機率相同的試驗，成功次數服從二項分布（binomial distribution），平均數 $np$、變異數 $np(1-p)$。台股每 20 個交易日一組，上漲天數的變異數是 4.72，與二項分布的 4.95 相近，但這只表示資料與獨立相容，並不是證明。當 $p$ 很小、$n$ 很大而 $np=\lambda$ 固定時，二項分布趨近卜瓦松分布（Poisson distribution），它的平均數與變異數都等於 $\lambda$。台股每月單日漲跌 3% 以上的天數平均 0.612 天，變異數卻是 1.846，約為平均數的三倍，這叫過度離散（overdispersion）：大波動會群聚，不像獨立同分布（iid）的稀有事件（rare event）。

> [!kc]
> Why not model the up days of a 20-session block with a Poisson?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > The Poisson is the limit of a binomial with a small $p$, and up days have $\hat p=0.548$. A Poisson's variance equals its mean, while a binomial's is $1-p$ times its mean: 4.95 against 10.96 here.

## The Normal Distribution: Quantiles and QQ Plots {lo=3}

[[LM5]] computed normal probabilities, $\Pr(R\le-x)=\Phi\big((-x-\mu)/\sigma\big)$. Risk questions often run the other way: which loss is reached on 1% of days? That loss is a quantile. [[LM14]]'s quantile function $F_X^{-1}(u)$ is the smallest value at which $F_X$ reaches $u$. Because $\Phi$ is continuous and strictly increasing, exactly one $z$ solves $\Phi(z)=u$ for each $u$ in $(0,1)$; that $z$ is $\Phi^{-1}(u)$.

> [!proposition] Normal quantiles
> Let $R\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ with $\sigma>0$, and let $0<u<1$. Then
> $$\begin{gathered}F_R^{-1}(u)=\mu+\sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(u),\\ \Phi^{-1}(1-u)=-\Phi^{-1}(u).\end{gathered} \tag{15.6}$$

*Proof.* By (5.5), $\Pr\big(R\le\mu+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(u)\big)=\Phi\big(\Phi^{-1}(u)\big)=u$, and $F_R$ is strictly increasing. The symmetry $\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)$ of [[LM5]] gives $\Phi\big(-\Phi^{-1}(u)\big)=1-u$. $\blacksquare$

[[LM4]] used 1.96, without proof, as the value that a standard normal variable exceeds with probability 2.5%. By (15.6) it is $\Phi^{-1}(0.975)=-\Phi^{-1}(0.025)=1.9600$. With its sign reversed, the 1% quantile of a daily return is the one-day value at risk at 99% ([[LM32]]).

> [!example] Normal quantiles against TAIEX's {#normq}
> SciPy's `norm.ppf` gives $\Phi^{-1}$ at 0.95, 0.975, 0.99 and 0.999 as 1.6449, 1.9600, 2.3263 and 3.0902. With $\bar R=0.0625\%$ and $s=1.2084\%$, compute the normal's 5%, 2.5%, 1% and 0.1% quantiles. Compare them with TAIEX's empirical quantiles $\hat F^{-1}(u)=R_{(\lceil uT\rceil)}$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > By (15.6) and symmetry, the normal's 5% quantile is $0.0625\%-1.2084\%\times1.6449=-1.925\%$; the other levels follow in the same way (three decimals):
> >
> > | Level $u$ | Rank $\lceil uT\rceil$ | Normal (%) | TAIEX (%) |
> > |--:|--:|--:|--:|
> > | 5% | 293 | −1.925 | −1.862 |
> > | 2.5% | 147 | −2.306 | −2.648 |
> > | 1% | 59 | −2.749 | −3.567 |
> > | 0.1% | 6 | −3.672 | −6.313 |
> >
> > At 5% the normal is slightly too deep. From 2.5% on it is too shallow, and the gap widens to 0.82 points at 1% and 2.64 points at 0.1%. TAIEX's 0.1% quantile is its sixth-worst day, 11 October 2018.

> [!definition] QQ plot
> Sort the $T$ returns, $R_{(1)}\le\dots\le R_{(T)}$. The **QQ plot** (quantile–quantile plot) of the returns against a fitted normal places rank $j$ at the point
> $$\begin{gathered}\Big(\Phi^{-1}\Big(\frac{j-\frac12}{T}\Big),\ \frac{R_{(j)}-\bar R}{s}\Big),\\ j=1,\dots,T.\end{gathered}$$
> The coordinates are the normal quantile and the standardized return; normal returns lie near the 45° line.

> [!pitfall] Which level does a QQ point stand for?
> Rank $j$ sits at the level $(j-\tfrac12)/T$, the middle of the step of $\hat F$ from $(j-1)/T$ to $j/T$; at $j/T$ the best day would sit at $\Phi^{-1}(1)=+\infty$. A quantile quoted at a stated level keeps [[LM14]]'s rule $R_{(\lceil uT\rceil)}$: the QQ convention is for drawing, not for quoting.

> [!zh] 常態分位數與分位數-分位數點圖
> 機率是給定門檻求面積，分位數（quantile）反過來，是給定機率求門檻。常態分布（normal distribution）的分位數函數（quantile function）是 $\mu+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(u)$，[[LM4]] 用到的 1.96 就是 $\Phi^{-1}(0.975)$。在 1% 的水準，常態模型給 −2.749%，台股經驗分布（empirical distribution）的 1% 分位數卻是 −3.567%，相差 0.82 個百分點，而且越往尾端差得越多。分位數-分位數點圖（QQ plot）把排序後的標準化報酬對常態分位數作圖：資料若接近常態，點會落在 45 度線附近；台股的點左端低於直線、右端高於直線，呈 S 形，表示兩側尾巴都比常態厚，也就是厚尾（heavy tail）。

> [!kc]
> Which standard deviation $\sigma$ would make a normal with mean 0.0625% expect 15 days at or below −5% in 5,849? How many days at or below −2% would it then expect?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > The model needs $5{,}849\,\Phi\big((-5\%-0.0625\%)/\sigma\big)=15$, so $(-5.0625\%)/\sigma=\Phi^{-1}(15/5{,}849)=-2.7988$ and $\sigma=1.809\%$. That normal expects $5{,}849\,\Phi(-2.0625/1.809)=743$ days at or below −2%, against 252. A wider bell moves the whole curve; the data need a different shape.

## The Lognormal Distribution {lo=4}

A year's growth factor $G$ is positive, a product of many daily factors, so its logarithm $X=\ln G$ is a sum. [[LM17]] explains why a product of many independent factors is close to lognormal; [[LM38]] builds lognormal prices.

> [!definition] Lognormal distribution
> Let $X\sim\mathcal N(\mu_X,\sigma_X^2)$ with $\sigma_X>0$. Then $G=e^X$ has the **lognormal distribution** with parameters $\mu_X$ and $\sigma_X$.

> [!proposition] Density, quantiles and moments of a lognormal
> Let $G=e^X$ with $X\sim\mathcal N(\mu_X,\sigma_X^2)$ and $\sigma_X>0$. Then, for $y>0$ and $0<u<1$,
> $$\begin{gathered}f_G(y)=\frac{1}{\sigma_Xy}\,\varphi\Big(\frac{\ln y-\mu_X}{\sigma_X}\Big),\\ F_G^{-1}(u)=e^{\mu_X+\sigma_X\Phi^{-1}(u)},\\ \E[G]=e^{\mu_X+\sigma_X^2/2},\\ \Var(G)=\big(e^{\sigma_X^2}-1\big)\,e^{2\mu_X+\sigma_X^2}.\end{gathered} \tag{15.7}$$
> In particular the median is $e^{\mu_X}$, below the mean.

*Proof.* The exponential is increasing, so $F_G(y)=\Pr(X\le\ln y)=\Phi\big((\ln y-\mu_X)/\sigma_X\big)$ for $y>0$. The chain rule (2.5) and $\Phi'=\varphi$ ([[LM5]]) give the density. The same monotonicity and (15.6) give the quantiles; the median, at $u=\tfrac12$, is $e^{\mu_X}$. The mean is (5.12). For the variance, $2X\sim\mathcal N(2\mu_X,4\sigma_X^2)$, so (5.12) gives $\E[G^2]=e^{2\mu_X+2\sigma_X^2}$; subtract $(\E[G])^2=e^{2\mu_X+\sigma_X^2}$, by (14.4). $\blacksquare$

> [!example] A lognormal for annual growth {#annual}
> TR23 has 23 calendar-year growth factors $G$, 2003–2025, with 2003 measured from its first close (2 January 2003). Their logarithms have mean $\mu_X=0.11646$ and standard deviation $\sigma_X=0.22207$ (divisor 23). [[LM14]] found an arithmetic mean return of 14.97% and a compound rate of 12.35%. Fit a lognormal with these parameters. Compute its median, mean and standard deviation, the probability of a losing year, and the 5% and 95% quantiles.
>
> > [!solution]
> > By (15.7), the median is $e^{0.11646}=1.1235$, or +12.35%: the compound rate, because $e^{\mu_X}$ is the geometric mean of the factors. The mean is $e^{0.11646+0.22207^2/2}=1.1516$, +15.16% against the sample's 14.97%. The standard deviation is 0.2589.
> >
> > A losing year has probability $\Pr(G<1)=\Phi(-0.11646/0.22207)=\Phi(-0.5244)=30.0\%$: 6.90 of 23 years, against 5 observed (2008, 2011, 2015, 2018 and 2022). The 5% and 95% quantiles are $e^{0.11646\mp1.6449\times0.22207}$, that is 0.7797 and 1.6189 ([[ex:lognormal]]).
> >
> > [[LM17]] models a year instead as $m=246$ independent daily log returns, fitted to the 5,664 log returns from 3 January 2003 to 31 December 2025. Its annual log return has mean 0.1163, close to $\mu_X$, but standard deviation 0.1856 against $\sigma_X=0.22207$, so it gives a losing year 26.5%. Independent days scaled by $\sqrt m$ understate the annual spread ([[LM19]]).

## The Student-t Distribution {lo=5}

[[LM5]] wrote the Student-t density with $\nu=3$ "up to a constant" and quoted its kurtosis $3+6/(\nu-4)$ without proof. This section supplies the constant, proves the moments and fits the distribution to TAIEX. [[LM14]]'s Chebyshev and Cantelli bounds cap a tail probability but predict no count; a count needs a model with a shape.

### The gamma function

> [!definition] Gamma function
> For $a>0$,
> $$\begin{gathered}\Gamma(a):=\int_0^\infty y^{a-1}e^{-y}\,dy,\\ \Gamma(a+1)=a\,\Gamma(a),\qquad \Gamma(\tfrac12)=\sqrt\pi.\end{gathered} \tag{15.8}$$

The integral converges for $a>0$: near 0 by [[LM5]]'s power test, and at infinity because an exponential beats any power. Integration by parts (5.6), differentiating $y^a$ and integrating $e^{-y}$, gives $\Gamma(a+1)=\big[-y^ae^{-y}\big]_0^\infty+a\,\Gamma(a)=a\,\Gamma(a)$. With $\Gamma(1)=1$, the gamma function extends the factorial: $\Gamma(2)=1$, $\Gamma(3)=2$, $\Gamma(4)=6$. For $\Gamma(\tfrac12)$, substitute $y=z^2/2$ by (5.4) and use (5.7):

$$\Gamma(\tfrac12)=\int_0^\infty\sqrt2\,e^{-z^2/2}\,dz=\sqrt2\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{2}=\sqrt\pi.$$

### The density and its moments

> [!definition] Student-t distribution
> A random variable $Y$ has the standard **Student-t distribution** with $\nu>0$ **degrees of freedom**, $Y\sim t_\nu$, when
> $$f_Y(y)=\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{\nu+1}2\big)}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\;\Gamma\big(\frac\nu2\big)}\Big(1+\frac{y^2}{\nu}\Big)^{-\frac{\nu+1}2} \tag{15.9}$$
> for every real $y$.

The constant makes the total area 1. It follows from the beta integral and is stated here without proof (SciPy's `t` reference; Blitzstein and Hwang 2019, §10.4, treat the distribution). The numbers script checks the area numerically. The density is symmetric about 0, and its tails fall like $\lvert y\rvert^{-(\nu+1)}$. By [[LM5]]'s power test, the moments of order below $\nu$ exist and the others do not, so the mean, 0, exists for $\nu>1$.

Two limits frame the family. At $\nu=1$ the density is $1/\big(\pi(1+y^2)\big)$, the Cauchy law of [[LM5]]'s tails exhibit, with no mean. As $\nu\to\infty$, $(1+y^2/\nu)^{\nu}\to e^{y^2}$ by (1.6), so the shape tends to $e^{-y^2/2}$ and $t_\nu$ tends to $\mathcal N(0,1)$; [[eg:gamma]] checks the constant numerically.

> [!example] Constants from the gamma function {#gamma}
> Compute $\Gamma(\tfrac12)$, $\Gamma(\tfrac32)$, $\Gamma(2)$ and $\Gamma(\tfrac52)$. Then evaluate the constant of (15.9) at $\nu=3$, 1, 30 and 1,000.
>
> > [!solution]
> > By (15.8), $\Gamma(\tfrac12)=\sqrt\pi=1.772454$, $\Gamma(\tfrac32)=\tfrac12\sqrt\pi=0.886227$, $\Gamma(2)=1$ and $\Gamma(\tfrac52)=\tfrac32\Gamma(\tfrac32)=1.329340$.
> >
> > At $\nu=3$ the constant is $\Gamma(2)/\big(\sqrt{3\pi}\,\Gamma(\tfrac32)\big)=2/(\pi\sqrt3)=0.3676$, the constant that [[LM5]]'s text omitted. The density $\frac{2}{\pi\sqrt3}(1+y^2/3)^{-2}=6\sqrt3/\big(\pi(3+y^2)^2\big)$ is the $t_3$ density that [[LM5]]'s widget draws.
> >
> > At $\nu=1$ the constant is $\Gamma(1)/\big(\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(\tfrac12)\big)=1/\pi=0.3183$, the Cauchy constant. It is 0.3956 at $\nu=30$ and 0.3988 at $\nu=1{,}000$, approaching the normal's $1/\sqrt{2\pi}=0.3989$.

> [!theorem] Moments of the Student-t
> Let $Y\sim t_\nu$. Then
> $$\begin{gathered}\Var(Y)=\frac{\nu}{\nu-2}\ \ (\nu>2),\\ \operatorname{Kurt}(Y)=3+\frac{6}{\nu-4}\ \ (\nu>4).\end{gathered} \tag{15.10}$$
> For $\nu\le2$ the second moment is infinite. For $2<\nu\le4$ the kurtosis is infinite, and for $\nu\le2$ it is undefined.

Deep Dive 2 derives (15.10). To give a return model a chosen mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, scale $Y$:

$$R=\mu+\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\nu-2}{\nu}}\;Y,\qquad \nu>2. \tag{15.11}$$

By (14.4) and (15.10), this $R$ has mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2\cdot\frac{\nu-2}{\nu}\cdot\frac{\nu}{\nu-2}=\sigma^2$. A shift and a positive scale leave the kurtosis unchanged, so matching a kurtosis $\operatorname{Kurt}>3$ means solving $3+6/(\nu-4)=\operatorname{Kurt}$:

$$\nu=4+\frac{6}{\operatorname{Kurt}-3}. \tag{15.12}$$

> [!deepdive]- Deep Dive 2: the variance and kurtosis of the Student-t
> Here $y$ is the variable of $f_Y$ and $z$ a rescaled variable. By the chain rule (2.5),
> $$\begin{aligned}&y\Big(1+\frac{y^2}\nu\Big)^{-\frac{\nu+1}2}\\&\quad=-\frac{\nu}{\nu-1}\,\frac{d}{dy}\Big(1+\frac{y^2}\nu\Big)^{-\frac{\nu-1}2}.\end{aligned}$$
> Integrate $y$ times the left side by parts (5.6). For $\nu>2$ the boundary term, of order $\lvert y\rvert^{2-\nu}$, vanishes, and
> $$\begin{aligned}&\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}y^2\Big(1+\frac{y^2}\nu\Big)^{-\frac{\nu+1}2}dy\\&\quad=\frac{\nu}{\nu-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Big(1+\frac{y^2}\nu\Big)^{-\frac{\nu-1}2}dy.\end{aligned}$$
> Substitute $y=z\sqrt{\nu/(\nu-2)}$ by (5.4). The integrand becomes $\big(1+z^2/(\nu-2)\big)^{-((\nu-2)+1)/2}$, the shape of $t_{\nu-2}$, so the integral on the right is $\sqrt{\nu/(\nu-2)}$ times the reciprocal of (15.9)'s constant at $\nu-2$. Multiply by the constant at $\nu$ and use $\Gamma(a+1)=a\Gamma(a)$ twice:
> $$\begin{aligned}\E[Y^2]&=\frac{\nu}{\nu-1}\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{\nu-2}}\\&\quad\times\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{\nu+1}2\big)\,\Gamma\big(\frac{\nu-2}2\big)\sqrt{(\nu-2)\pi}}{\Gamma\big(\frac{\nu-1}2\big)\,\Gamma\big(\frac\nu2\big)\sqrt{\nu\pi}}\\&=\frac{\nu}{\nu-1}\cdot\frac{\nu-1}{\nu-2}=\frac{\nu}{\nu-2}.\end{aligned}$$
> Once more, integrate $y^3$ times that left side by parts. For $\nu>4$ the boundary term, of order $\lvert y\rvert^{4-\nu}$, vanishes, and the same substitution turns the remaining integral into the second moment of $t_{\nu-2}$, $(\nu-2)/(\nu-4)$. Collecting the factors as before,
> $$\begin{aligned}\E[Y^4]&=\frac{3\nu}{\nu-2}\cdot\frac{\nu}{\nu-2}\cdot\frac{\nu-2}{\nu-4}\\&=\frac{3\nu^2}{(\nu-2)(\nu-4)}.\end{aligned}$$
> Hence $\operatorname{Kurt}(Y)=\E[Y^4]/(\E[Y^2])^2=3(\nu-2)/(\nu-4)=3+6/(\nu-4)$. For $2<\nu\le4$ the integral of $y^4f_Y(y)$ diverges by the power test, so the kurtosis is infinite. $\blacksquare$

### Chords of a power tail

Counts and quantiles need no moments. Recall [[LM5]]'s power tail: a probability that falls like $x^{-\alpha}$ is a line of slope $-\alpha$ on log–log axes.

> [!proposition] Tail index from two levels
> Let $0<x_1<x_2$, and suppose that $\Pr(R\le-x)$ is a constant multiple of $x^{-\alpha}$ for $x_1\le x\le x_2$. Then
> $$\alpha=\frac{\ln\big[\Pr(R\le-x_1)/\Pr(R\le-x_2)\big]}{\ln(x_2/x_1)}. \tag{15.13}$$
> For any other law, the right side, the **chord** between the two levels, is a reading of the tail, not a parameter.

*Proof.* The constant cancels in the ratio, which equals $(x_2/x_1)^{\alpha}$; take logarithms. $\blacksquare$

> [!example] Reading a tail index {#chords}
> Use [[ex:tailtable]]'s counts at −2% to −6% to compute the chords (15.13) between adjacent levels. Compare the chord from −3% to −6% with those of the three models.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The counts 252, 108, 44, 15 and 6 give, for example, $\ln(252/108)/\ln1.5=2.09$ from −2% to −3%. The adjacent chords are 2.09, 3.12, 4.82 and 5.03: the chord rises with the level ([[ex:loglog]]). All ten pairs of levels from −2% to −6% give chords between 2.09 and 5.03, mostly inside [[LM5]]'s range of 2 to 5.
> >
> > From −3% to −6% the data give $\ln(108/6)/\ln2=4.17$. The normal's chord is 14.4, the maximum-likelihood fit's 2.77 and the kurtosis fit's 4.16. Report the chords, not one value: [[LM20]] estimates a tail index with its standard error.

Large falls outnumber large rises, 108 against 67; [[LM14]] computed TR23's skewness, −0.34, and [[LM19]] studies it as a statistic. Extreme value theory models the largest losses directly; it is beyond this book.

> [!pitfall] The price limit truncates the tail
> TWSE caps each stock's daily move, at 7% until 29 May 2015 and at 10% from 1 June 2015, so the index cannot fall by more in one session. Before June 2015 every TR23 return lay between −6.6789% and +6.7420%. The sample's worst day, −9.6997% on 7 April 2025, was capped for many stocks. A model without bounds spreads mass beyond the limit: compare models inside it.

### Choosing a model

[[ex:choice]] resolves the case: the question decides the model, and each model has an assumption that limits it.

> [!exhibit] Which model for which question {#choice}
> | Question | Model | Evidence | Limiting assumption |
> |:--|:--|:--|:--|
> | Days at or below −2% | normal | 257 days against 252 | thin tails: fails from −3% |
> | The 5% quantile | normal | −1.925% against −1.862% | thin tails |
> | Days from −3% to −5%; the 1% quantile | Student-t, maximum likelihood | 79.0, 36.6, 19.6 days against 108, 44, 15; −3.365% against −3.567% | symmetry; too heavy from −5% |
> | Days beyond −7% | counts | 2, 2, 1 days at −7%, −8%, −9%; the kurtosis fit's 1.75, 0.926, 0.524 come close, but its $\nu$ rests on the unstable kurtosis | few days, cut by the price limit |
> | A calendar year's growth | lognormal | losing years 6.90 expected against 5; beyond the 5% and 95% quantiles 2.3 against 2; mean +15.16% against 14.97% | independent years |
> | Large moves in a month | none: not the Poisson | variance 1.846 against mean 0.612 | clustering |
> | Up days in 20 sessions | binomial | variance 4.72 against 4.95 | independent days |
>
> Note: evidence from [[ex:case]], [[ex:binom]], [[ex:poisson]], [[ex:tailtable]], [[eg:annual]] and [[ex:lognormal]]; every model is fitted in-sample. The maximum-likelihood Student-t's 1% quantile is $0.0997\%+0.7761\%\times F_Y^{-1}(0.01)$ with $\nu=3.065$.

Tail means rank the models the same way. The 15 days at or below −5% averaged −6.26%. The normal's (5.11) gives −5.26%, and the maximum-likelihood Student-t gives −7.57%. With its sign reversed, the mean return beyond a quantile is the expected shortfall of [[LM32]].

> [!zh] 尾部：少用高階動差
> 樣本峰態（kurtosis）由少數極端日主導：2025 年 4 月 7 日一天就讓台股的超額峰態（excess kurtosis）從 4.25 跳到 4.94，同一天 1% 分位數（quantile）只移動 0.005 個百分點，−3% 以下的天數只多 1 天。最大的十個四次方項占全部的 35.0%，拿掉四個最大的漲跌日，超額峰態就降到 3.74。描述尾巴應優先使用需要較少動差（moment）的量：超過門檻的天數、分位數，以及雙對數圖（log–log plot）上相鄰門檻之間的弦（chord）斜率，也就是尾部指數（tail index）的讀數；台股在 2.09 到 5.03 之間，門檻越深越陡。沒有一個分布能回答所有問題：−2% 用常態分布（normal distribution），−3% 到 −5% 用 maximum likelihood 的 t 分布（Student-t distribution），年度成長用對數常態分布（lognormal distribution），每個模型都有限制它的假設。

## Distributions in Python {lo=8}

SciPy's distributions share `pdf`, `cdf`, `ppf`, `sf` and `fit`. The calls of this module:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
# R: TR23's 5,849 daily returns as a NumPy array
T = len(R)
down5 = int((R <= -0.05 + 1e-12).sum())        # 15 days: the guard
loc, scale = stats.norm.fit(R)                  # mean and s, divisor T
nu, loc_t, sc_t = stats.t.fit(R)                # maximum likelihood
print(T * stats.norm.cdf(-0.05, loc, scale))    # 0.0817 days
print(T * stats.t.cdf(-0.05, nu, loc_t, sc_t))  # 19.6 days
print(stats.t.ppf(0.01, nu, loc_t, sc_t))       # -0.03365
print(np.quantile(R, 0.01, method="inverted_cdf"))  # -0.03567
print(stats.kurtosis(R))                        # 5.27, excess kurtosis
lg = stats.lognorm(s=0.22207, scale=np.exp(0.11646))
print(lg.median(), lg.mean(), lg.ppf([0.05, 0.95]))
print(286 * stats.poisson(mu=175 / 286).pmf(0))  # 155.1 months
print(292 * stats.binom(20, 0.548).cdf(5))       # 1.98 blocks
```

[[ex:scipy]] maps SciPy's parameters to this module's symbols. [[LM18]] draws from these laws.

> [!exhibit] SciPy's distributions in this module's symbols {#scipy}
> | Call | Parameters | Trap |
> |:--|:--|:--|
> | `norm(loc, scale)` | $\mu$, $\sigma$ | `norm.fit` returns $\bar R$ and $s$, divisor $T$ |
> | `lognorm(s, scale)` | $\sigma_X$, $e^{\mu_X}$ | `s` is $\sigma_X$, not a standard deviation of $G$ |
> | `t(df, loc, scale)` | $\nu$, location, $\sigma\sqrt{(\nu-2)/\nu}$ | `scale` is not $\sigma$; the variance is `scale**2*df/(df-2)` for `df > 2` |
> | `poisson(mu)` | $\lambda$ | the parameter is named `mu` |
> | `binom(n, p)` | $n$, $p$ | `cdf(5)` includes 5 |
> | `.fit(data)` | maximum likelihood | the default; `method="MM"` fits the first three raw moments, not the kurtosis of (15.12), and on TR23 it stops with an error |
> | `stats.kurtosis(R)`, `.stats(moments="k")` | excess kurtosis | `stats.kurtosis` uses divisor-$T$ moments, `.stats` the model's; `kurtosis(R, fisher=False)` returns $\operatorname{Kurt}$ |
> | `np.quantile(R, u, method="inverted_cdf")` | $\hat F^{-1}(u)=R_{(\lceil uT\rceil)}$ | the default method interpolates |
>
> Note: SciPy 1.18 and NumPy 2.5 references; the divisor of `norm.fit` and the variance of `t` were checked by computation (`data/lm15_numbers.py`).

> [!pitfall] Excess or not
> SciPy's `stats.kurtosis` returns the excess kurtosis $\operatorname{Kurt}-3$ by default (`fisher=True`), and a distribution's `.stats(moments="k")` does too. [[LM5]]'s tails exhibit and (15.10) give the kurtosis itself.

## Summary {-}

| Question | Distribution | Result in this module |
|:--|:--|:--|
| How many successes in $n$ trials? | binomial (15.1)–(15.2) | up days in 20 sessions: variance 4.72 against 4.95 |
| How many rare events? | Poisson (15.3)–(15.5) | large moves a month: variance 1.846 against mean 0.612 |
| Which loss on 1% of days? | normal quantile (15.6) | −2.749% against TAIEX's −3.567% |
| How does a year grow? | lognormal (15.7) | median 1.1235, mean 1.1516, losing year 30.0% |
| How heavy is the tail? | Student-t (15.9)–(15.12); chords (15.13) | $\nu=3.065$; 19.6 days at −5% against 15 |

- The binomial fits up days in blocks, which is consistent with independent days but does not prove it.
- Rare binomial trials tend to the Poisson. Monthly counts of large moves are overdispersed: large moves cluster.
- TAIEX's QQ plot bends away from the normal's line in both tails.
- A lognormal's median is $e^{\mu_X}$; fitted to calendar years, it is the compound rate.
- The Student-t's kurtosis needs $\nu>4$, and maximum likelihood puts TAIEX at $\nu=3.065$.
- A sample kurtosis rests on a few days; counts, quantiles and chords need fewer moments.

## Solutions {-}

1. **A** is correct: by (15.2), $20\times0.548\times0.452=4.95$. B, 10.96, is the mean $np$. C, 2.23, is the standard deviation.

2. By (15.3), $\Pr(X=0)=e^{-0.612}=0.542$ and $\Pr(X\ge5)=1-\sum_{j\le4}\Pr(X=j)=0.000431$. Of 286 months, the model expects $286e^{-0.612}=155.1$ and $286\times0.000431=0.123$ months. The model misses both ends, 210 calm and 9 turbulent months. Large moves cluster because volatility changes over time; the Poisson assumes a constant rate.

3. **C** is correct: $1.846/0.612=3.02$. A, 0.33, inverts the ratio. B, 1.00, is a Poisson count's ratio; the data are overdispersed.

4. Drop the terms $j=0$ and 1 and shift the index by 2: $\E[X(X-1)]=\lambda^2\sum_{j\ge2}e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{j-2}/(j-2)!=\lambda^2$, by (3.2). Then $\Var(X)=\E[X(X-1)]+\E[X]-(\E[X])^2=\lambda$. By (15.2), $\operatorname{Bin}(n,\lambda/n)$ has variance $n\cdot\frac\lambda n\big(1-\frac\lambda n\big)=\lambda(1-\lambda/n)$, which tends to $\lambda$.

5. By (15.6), the 1% quantile is $0.0625\%-1.2084\%\times2.3263=-2.749\%$ and the 99% quantile $+2.874\%$. The normal misses the lower tail more: by 0.82 points, against 0.25 in the upper tail. Large falls are larger than large rises: TR23's skewness is −0.34 ([[LM14]]).

6. **B** is correct: $\Phi(-0.11646/0.22207)=\Phi(-0.5244)=30.0\%$. A, 21.7%, is the sample's 5 losing years of 23, not the model's probability. C, 70.0%, reverses the sign: it is $\Pr(G>1)$.

7. By (15.7), the median is $e^{0.11646}=1.1235$, the mean $e^{0.11646+0.22207^2/2}=1.1516$ and the standard deviation 0.2589. The quantiles are $e^{0.11646\mp1.6449\times0.22207}=0.7797$ and 1.6189. SciPy's `median()`, `mean()`, `std()` and `ppf([0.05, 0.95])` return the same values.

8. With $\Gamma(\tfrac52)=\tfrac34\sqrt\pi$ and $\Gamma(2)=1$, the constant is $\tfrac34\sqrt\pi/(2\sqrt\pi)=3/8$. By (15.10), $t_5$ has variance $5/3$ and kurtosis 9, and $t_6$ has variance $3/2$ and kurtosis 6. For $t_4$ the variance, 2, exists, but the kurtosis is infinite. For $t_2$ the variance is infinite and the kurtosis undefined.

9. **C** is correct: by (15.12), $\nu=4+6/5.27=5.14$. A, 1.14, forgets the 4. B, 6.64, takes the excess kurtosis for the kurtosis, $4+6/(5.27-3)$.

10. The chords are $\ln(252/44)/\ln2=2.52$, $\ln(44/6)/\ln1.5=4.91$ and $\ln(252/6)/\ln3=3.40$, the mean of the first two weighted by $\ln2$ and $\ln1.5$. In TR23 the chord rises with the level, so one power law does not describe the counts from −2% to −6%. The deeper chord rests on 6 days and on the price limit ([[ex:loglog]]).

    Far in its tail a Student-t's probability falls like $x^{-\nu}$, so 3.065 is that model's tail index. At these levels the fit's own chords, from [[ex:tailtable]]'s 210, 36.6 and 11.6 days, are 2.52, 2.83 and 2.64. They equal the data's from −2% to −4% and lie far below them from −4% to −6%.

11. Before June 2015: $T=3{,}077$, $s=1.257\%$, excess kurtosis 3.39, $\nu=2.990$, 1% quantile −3.621%. From June 2015: $T=2{,}772$, $s=1.152\%$, excess kurtosis 8.07, $\nu=3.237$, 1% quantile −3.467%. The excess kurtosis moved most, more than doubling ([[ex:stability]]). The comparison is a stability check: each part is fitted in-sample, and no standard error says whether a change exceeds chance ([[LM20]], [[LM21]]).

12. TAIEX has 67 days at or above +3% and 13 at or above +5%. The normal expects 44.0 and 0.128. The maximum-likelihood Student-t expects 94.2 and 22.0, so it overstates the upper tail at both levels. In the lower tail it understates at −3% (79.0 against 108) and overstates at −5% (19.6 against 15).

    A symmetric model mirrors its tails about its centre. Centred at +0.0997%, the Student-t even expects more large rises than large falls, while TAIEX has 108 large falls and 67 large rises.

## Glossary {-}

| Term | 中文 | Meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Bernoulli distribution | 柏努利分布 | Law of one trial: success with probability $p$; mean $p$, variance $p(1-p)$. |
| Binomial distribution | 二項分布 | Number of successes in $n$ independent trials with one $p$, (15.1); mean $np$, variance $np(1-p)$. |
| Poisson distribution | 卜瓦松分布 | Law of a count with $\Pr(X=j)=e^{-\lambda}\lambda^j/j!$, (15.3); mean and variance $\lambda$. |
| Overdispersion | 過度離散 | A count's variance above its mean: a sign that a Poisson does not fit. |
| Independent and identically distributed (iid) | 獨立同分布 | Independent random variables that share one distribution. |
| Quantile function | 分位數函數 | $F_X^{-1}(u)$, the smallest value at which $F_X$ reaches $u$; for a normal, $\mu+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(u)$, (15.6). |
| QQ plot | 分位數-分位數點圖 | Sorted standardized returns against normal quantiles at the levels $(j-\tfrac12)/T$. |
| Empirical distribution | 經驗分布 | Mass $1/T$ on each observation; its quantiles are order statistics. |
| Lognormal distribution | 對數常態分布 | Law of $G=e^X$ for a normal $X$, (15.7); median $e^{\mu_X}$, mean $e^{\mu_X+\sigma_X^2/2}$. |
| Gamma function | 伽瑪函數 | $\Gamma(a)=\int_0^\infty y^{a-1}e^{-y}dy$, (15.8), with $\Gamma(a+1)=a\Gamma(a)$. |
| Student-t distribution | t 分布 | Symmetric law with density (15.9) and power tails; tends to the normal as $\nu\to\infty$. |
| Degrees of freedom | 自由度 | The Student-t's parameter $\nu$: moments of order below $\nu$ exist. |
| Excess kurtosis | 超額峰態 | $\operatorname{Kurt}-3$; 0 for a normal; what SciPy's `kurtosis` returns. |
| Heavy tail | 厚尾 | A tail that falls like a power of the loss rather than like the normal's $e^{-z^2/2}$. |
| Tail index | 尾部指數 | The exponent $\alpha$ of a power tail; between two levels, read as a chord (15.13). |
