## Introduction

[[LM20]] gave a past mean a standard error. Here the unknown mean itself is uncertain: a prior distribution states a belief before the data, and Bayes' rule updates it to a posterior distribution. The posterior mean is a weighted average of the data and the prior, so it shrinks the sample mean toward a target.

> [!case] Last decade's winners
> Split the 28 TWSE sector price indices of [[LM10]] (U28, no dividends) at [[LM11]]'s midpoint. From December 2008 to October 2017, Automobile led with a mean return of 21.65% a year. From October 2017 to October 2026 it ranked 20th, at 3.67%. ShippingTransportation went from last, 1.89%, to fifth, 17.57%. Across the 28 sectors, the two halves' means correlate −0.05 ([[ex:case]]).
>
> Predict each sector's second half by its own first-half mean, and the typical miss is 9.8 points a year. Predict every sector by the first half's average of all 28, 10.49%, and the miss is 8.5 points.
>
> The TAIEX's total return averaged 15.38% a year over 23.8 years ([[LM19]]), and its plug-in Kelly leverage is 4.28 ([[LM20]]). [[LM4]] advised betting a quarter to a half of that. Should you believe a past mean, and what would a sceptic have to believe to bet so little?

## Bayes' Rule for a Parameter {lo=1}

[[LM13]]'s manager beat the index in 8 of 12 months. [[LM13]] allowed two types: 10% of managers win a month with probability 0.55, the rest with 0.5. But a manager's monthly chance $\theta$ can be any number between 0 and 1, so a prior should be a density on $[0,1]$.

Given $\theta$, months are independent, so by (13.7) a given record of $j$ good months in $n$ has probability $\theta^j(1-\theta)^{n-j}$. As a function of $\theta$, this is the record's **likelihood** $\mathcal L(\theta)$ ([[LM20]]). Split $[0,1]$ into short intervals of width $\Delta$. By (13.9) and (13.8), the interval at $x_k$, with prior probability about $f_\theta(x_k)\Delta$, gets the posterior probability about

$$\frac{\mathcal L(x_k)\,f_\theta(x_k)\,\Delta}{\sum_l\mathcal L(x_l)\,f_\theta(x_l)\,\Delta}.$$

Dividing by $\Delta$ and letting $\Delta\to0$ turns the sum into an integral; the limit is taken as given here (Wasserman 2004, ch. 11).

> [!theorem] Bayes' rule for a parameter
> Let a parameter $\theta$ have a prior density $f_\theta$, and let the data have the likelihood $\mathcal L(\theta)$, with $0<\int\mathcal L(x)f_\theta(x)\,dx<\infty$. Then the posterior density is
> $$f_{\theta\mid\text{data}}(\theta)=\frac{\mathcal L(\theta)\,f_\theta(\theta)}{\int\mathcal L(x)\,f_\theta(x)\,dx}. \tag{24.1}$$

The denominator does not depend on $\theta$, so constant factors of the likelihood or the prior cancel.

> [!definition] Beta distribution
> For $\alpha_0,\beta_0>0$, the **beta distribution** $\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha_0,\beta_0)$ has the density, for $0<x<1$,
> $$\begin{aligned}f_\theta(x)&=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_0+\beta_0)}{\Gamma(\alpha_0)\,\Gamma(\beta_0)}\\&\quad\times x^{\alpha_0-1}(1-x)^{\beta_0-1},\end{aligned}$$
> with $\Gamma$ from (15.8). That this density integrates to 1 is stated without proof.

Multiplying the density by $x$ gives a constant times the $\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha_0+1,\beta_0)$ density, and the recursion of (15.8) for $\Gamma$ gives the mean $\alpha_0/(\alpha_0+\beta_0)$. The same step twice gives the variance

$$\Var\theta=\frac{\alpha_0\beta_0}{(\alpha_0+\beta_0)^2(\alpha_0+\beta_0+1)}.$$

> [!result] Beta prior, binomial record
> Let $\theta\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha_0,\beta_0)$, and let a record have $j$ successes in $n$ independent trials with probability $\theta$. Then the posterior is $\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha_0+j,\beta_0+n-j)$, with mean
> $$\begin{aligned}\E[\theta\mid\text{data}]&=\frac{\alpha_0}{\alpha_0+\beta_0}\\&\quad+c\,\Big(\frac jn-\frac{\alpha_0}{\alpha_0+\beta_0}\Big),\\ c&=\frac{n}{n+\alpha_0+\beta_0}.\end{aligned} \tag{24.2}$$

*Proof.* By (24.1), the posterior is proportional to $x^j(1-x)^{n-j}\cdot x^{\alpha_0-1}(1-x)^{\beta_0-1}$, the shape of the stated beta density. Its mean $(\alpha_0+j)/(\alpha_0+\beta_0+n)$ rearranges into (24.2). $\blacksquare$

A prior whose posterior stays in its family is **conjugate**. The **data's weight** $c$ sets $n$ trials against the prior's worth of $n_0:=\alpha_0+\beta_0$ trials: $c=n/(n+n_0)$.

To match [[LM13]], choose a symmetric prior $\operatorname{Beta}(a,a)$ under which 10% of managers have $\theta\ge0.55$: $a=81.96$. The integer 82 still gives 10.0% and keeps every probability an exact fraction. This **sceptic's prior** has standard deviation 0.039 and is worth $n_0=164$ months.

> [!example] The manager under a sceptic's prior {#sceptic}
> Under the prior Beta(82, 82), find the posterior after [[LM13]]'s 8 good months of 12, its mean, the data's weight and $\Pr(\theta\ge0.55\mid\text{record})$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > By (24.2), the posterior is Beta(90, 86), with mean $90/176=45/88=0.5114$. The data's weight is $c=12/176=3/44=0.068$: twelve months weigh 6.8% against the prior's 164. So the record moves the mean from 0.5 by only $\tfrac3{44}\times(\tfrac8{12}-\tfrac12)=\tfrac1{88}=0.0114$. Exactly, $\Pr(\theta\ge0.55\mid\text{record})=15.3\%$ ([[ex:manager]]), against [[LM13]]'s two-type 13.5%.

> [!example] A flat prior, and how long a record convinces the sceptic {#flat}
> Repeat [[eg:sceptic]] with the flat prior Beta(1, 1). Then find how many months of two good in three raise the sceptic's $\Pr(\theta\ge0.55)$ above one half.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The posterior is Beta(9, 5), with mean $9/14=0.643$ and $c=12/14=6/7$: the data dominate. Exactly, $\Pr(\theta>0.5)=7{,}099/8{,}192=86.7\%$ and $\Pr(\theta\ge0.55)=77.2\%$. The central 95% of the posterior lies between 0.39 and 0.86.
> >
> > Under Beta(82, 82), a two-in-three record of $n$ months gives $\operatorname{Beta}(82+2n/3,\,82+n/3)$. The probability first exceeds one half at $n=72$ months (51.2%; 49.4% at 69), when $c=72/236=0.305$: six years barely convince the sceptic.

> [!zh] 參數的貝氏推論
> 貝氏推論（Bayesian inference）把未知的參數（parameter）當成有分布（distribution）的量：事前分布（prior distribution）描述看資料之前的信念，乘上概度函數（likelihood function）再除以常數，就是事後分布（posterior distribution），即 (24.1)。貝他(β)分布（beta distribution）配上二項資料（binomial data），事後仍是貝他(β)分布，所以它是共軛事前分布（conjugate prior）。事後平均數（posterior mean）是事前平均數與資料頻率的加權平均（weighted average），資料權重（data's weight）$c=n/(n+n_0)$，其中 $n_0=\alpha_0+\beta_0$ 是事前分布相當的月數。懷疑性事前分布（sceptical prior）Beta(82, 82) 相當於 164 個月，所以 12 個月的紀錄只占 6.8% 的權重。均勻的事前分布看似中立，其實也是一種選擇。

## The Normal Mean with Known Variance {lo=2}

Now let the parameter be TAIEX's mean daily return $\mu$, with daily returns iid $\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ and $\sigma$ known. Expanding each square around $\bar R$, the cross term vanishes because $\sum_t(R_t-\bar R)=0$:

$$\sum_{t=1}^T(R_t-\mu)^2=\sum_{t=1}^T(R_t-\bar R)^2+T(\bar R-\mu)^2.$$

So the likelihood $\mathcal L(\mu)=\prod_t\varphi\big((R_t-\mu)/\sigma\big)/\sigma$ is a constant times $e^{-T(\bar R-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}$. It depends on the data only through $\bar R$, and by (24.1) so does the posterior.

Before the data, $\mu\sim\mathcal N(\mu_0,\sigma_0^2)$, and $\bar R=\mu+\bar\varepsilon$ with noise $\bar\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2/T)$ independent of $\mu$. So $(\mu,\bar R)$ is a normal pair by (16.7), with $\Var\bar R=\sigma_0^2+\sigma^2/T$ and $\Cov(\mu,\bar R)=\sigma_0^2$ by (16.3) and (16.4). In (16.8), with $X=\bar R$ and $Y=\mu$, the slope is $\sigma_0^2/(\sigma_0^2+\sigma^2/T)$, written $c$, and the residual variance is $\sigma_0^2(1-c)=c\,\sigma^2/T$.

> [!result] Posterior of a normal mean
> Let the returns $R_1,\dots,R_T$ be iid $\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ with $\sigma$ known, and let the prior be $\mu\sim\mathcal N(\mu_0,\sigma_0^2)$. Then
> $$\begin{gathered}\mu\mid\text{data}\sim\mathcal N\big(\mu_0+c\,(\bar R-\mu_0),\;c\,\sigma^2/T\big),\\ c=\frac{\sigma_0^2}{\sigma_0^2+\sigma^2/T}=\frac{T}{T+n_0},\qquad n_0=\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma_0^2}.\end{gathered} \tag{24.3}$$

Inverting the posterior variance gives $1/\sigma_0^2+T/\sigma^2$: the **precision** of the posterior, one over its variance, is the prior's precision plus the data's (Gelman et al. 2013, §2.5). The prior is worth $n_0$ returns.

> [!deepdive]- Completing the square
> Let $m$ and $v>0$ be numbers. By (24.1), the log posterior density of $\mu$ is a constant plus
> $$-\frac{(\mu-\mu_0)^2}{2\sigma_0^2}-\frac{T(\bar R-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}.$$
> This log density is a quadratic in $\mu$ with leading coefficient $-\tfrac12(1/\sigma_0^2+T/\sigma^2)$. Its derivative vanishes at
> $$\frac{\mu_0/\sigma_0^2+T\bar R/\sigma^2}{1/\sigma_0^2+T/\sigma^2}=\mu_0+c\,(\bar R-\mu_0).$$
> A density whose logarithm is a constant minus $(\mu-m)^2/(2v)$ is the normal density $\varphi\big((\mu-m)/\sqrt v\big)/\sqrt v$. So the posterior is normal with the mean and variance of (24.3), without (16.8).

On TR23, the model sets $\sigma=s=1.2084\%$, the normal maximum-likelihood estimate (20.5). [[LM19]]'s annualization exhibit gives $A\bar R=15.38\%$ and $\sqrt A\,s=18.96\%$ a year, and [[LM20]]'s (20.2) gives the standard error $As/\sqrt T=3.89$ points. On the annual scale, $c=(A\sigma_0)^2/\big((A\sigma_0)^2+3.89^2\big)$. A prior SD equal to the standard error gives $c=\tfrac12$: the posterior mean halves the sample's, to 7.69%, with SD $3.89/\sqrt2=2.75$ points ([[ex:normal]]).

For $\mu_0=0$, the posterior mean $c\bar R$ is [[LM20]]'s scaled mean $\eta\bar R$ with $\eta=c$. By (20.1), $\operatorname{MSE}(\eta\bar R)=\eta^2\sigma^2/T+(1-\eta)^2\mu^2$, so the best $\eta$ needs the unknown $\mu$. Averaged over the prior, $\mu^2$ becomes $\sigma_0^2$, and the minimizing $\eta$ is $c$. [[LM20]]'s $\eta^*=0.940$ is $c$ at a prior SD equal to the mean itself.

> [!example] TAIEX's mean under a prior of 10 points {#taiexpost}
> Take a prior centred at zero with $A\sigma_0=10$ points a year. Find the data's weight, the posterior mean and SD of the annual mean, and the prior's worth in years.
>
> > [!solution]
> > On the annual scale, $c=10^2/(10^2+3.89^2)=0.869$. By (24.3), the posterior mean is $0.869\times15.38\%=13.4\%$, and the posterior SD is $\sqrt{0.869}\times3.89=3.6$ points. The prior is worth $n_0=s^2/\sigma_0^2$ returns, or $n_0/A=(18.96/10)^2=3.59$ years of data.
> >
> > The model assumes normal days, which TAIEX's are not; (17.5) still makes $\bar R$ approximately normal. The iid standard error ignores volatility clustering, and robust errors of a mean are [[LM22]]'s and [[LM23]]'s.

> [!example] Where the prior's variance goes {#split}
> Check the law of total variance (16.6) for $\mu$ and $\bar R$ at $A\sigma_0=10$ points, on the annual scale.
>
> > [!solution]
> > By (16.6), $\Var\mu=\Var\big(\E[\mu\mid\bar R]\big)+\E\big[\Var(\mu\mid\bar R)\big]$. Here $\E[\mu\mid\bar R]=c\bar R$ has variance $c^2(\sigma_0^2+\sigma^2/T)=c\,\sigma_0^2$. The posterior variance is $(1-c)\sigma_0^2$, whatever $\bar R$ is. In points², $100=86.9+13.1$. Before the data arrive, most of the prior's variance is variance of the coming posterior mean.

> [!pitfall] A prior SD per day or per year
> The formulas take $\sigma_0$ per day, while the text quotes $A\sigma_0$ a year. A prior SD belongs to a mean, so it annualizes by $A$ like the mean, not by $\sqrt A$ like a volatility. Dividing 10 points by $\sqrt A$ gives a daily $\sigma_0$ of 0.637% instead of 0.0406%, and $c=0.9994$ instead of 0.869: the sceptic disappears. Also, $c$ is a ratio of variances, never of standard deviations (Problem 2).

> [!zh] 精確度相加
> 在變異數（variance）已知的常態模型裡，若平均數（mean）$\mu$ 的事前分布（prior distribution）是常態分布（normal distribution），事後分布（posterior distribution）也是常態分布。事後平均數（posterior mean）是 $\bar R$ 與事前平均數 $\mu_0$ 的加權平均（weighted average），資料權重（data's weight）$c$ 等於事前變異數除以（事前變異數加上 $\sigma^2/T$）。事後變異數的倒數稱為精確度（precision），它等於事前的精確度加上資料的精確度 $T/\sigma^2$。事前分布相當於 $n_0=\sigma^2/\sigma_0^2$ 筆報酬，所以 $c=T/(T+n_0)$。加權股價報酬指數 23.8 年的資料，碰上標準差（standard deviation）每年 10 個百分點的事前分布，權重是 0.869，事後平均數由 15.38% 縮為 13.4%。

## Credible and Confidence Intervals {lo=3}

A posterior answers probability questions about $\mu$ directly. Below, 1.96 is $\Phi^{-1}(0.975)$, as in [[LM15]].

> [!definition] Credible interval
> A **95% credible interval** is an interval to which the posterior gives probability 95%. For a normal posterior, the central one is the posterior mean $\pm1.96$ posterior SD.

[[LM20]]'s large-sample 95% confidence interval, $\bar R\pm1.96\,s/\sqrt T$, says something else. The procedure that builds it covers the fixed, unknown $\mu$ in about 95% of samples, while one computed interval either holds $\mu$ or misses it. As $\sigma_0\to\infty$, the prior flattens and $c\to1$. The credible interval then becomes numerically the confidence interval, and the two differ only in what they claim ([[ex:priors]]).

> > With $c=1$, the posterior is $\mathcal N(\bar R,s^2/T)$. On the annual scale, $15.38\%\pm1.96\times3.89$ runs from 7.8% to 23.0%, numerically [[LM20]]'s confidence interval. The posterior probability of a negative mean is
> > $$\begin{aligned}\Pr(\mu\le0\mid\text{data})&=\Phi\Big(-\frac{\bar R}{s/\sqrt T}\Big)\\&=3.8\times10^{-5}.\end{aligned}$$
> > [[LM21]]'s one-sided p-value, (21.2) at its t-stat 3.96, divides by $s_{\mathrm u}$ instead of $s$, and the two agree to the printed digits. Yet the posterior probability is a statement about $\mu$, and the p-value a statement about the data under $H_0:\mu=0$.
> >
> > At $A\sigma_0=10$ points, the interval is 6.3% to 20.5% and the probability $1.1\times10^{-4}$ ([[ex:priors]]). Even under the prior of the first row, the posterior puts 97.6% on $\mu>0$.

> [!pitfall] A confidence interval is not a probability for $\mu$
> The sentence "$\mu$ lies between 7.8% and 23.0% with probability 95%" belongs to a credible interval, and it needs a prior: here the flat one. Under the prior with $A\sigma_0=3.89$ points, the same data give 2.3% to 13.1%. A confidence interval's 95% belongs to the procedure, not to the interval in hand.

> [!zh] 可靠區間與信賴區間
> 可靠區間（credible interval）是事後分布（posterior distribution）的機率（probability）敘述：給定資料與事前分布（prior distribution），$\mu$ 落在區間內的機率是 95%。信賴區間（confidence interval）則是程序的性質：重複抽樣（repeated sampling）時，用同一作法建出的區間有 95% 會蓋住固定的 $\mu$，手上這一個區間則只有蓋住或沒蓋住兩種情形。事前分布越平，$c$ 越接近 1，兩者在數字上就越接近，例如加權股價報酬指數年平均報酬（mean return）的 7.8% 到 23.0%。同樣地，$\Pr(\mu\le0\mid\text{data})$ 是關於 $\mu$ 的機率；p值（p-value）則是在虛無假設（null hypothesis）成立時，關於資料的機率。兩者在這裡數值幾乎相同，意義卻不同。

## Shrinking Many Means: James–Stein {lo=4}

Now take $N$ sector means $\bar R_1,\dots,\bar R_N$ of $T$ returns each, under one prior: the true means $\mu_i$ are iid $\mathcal N(\mu_0,\sigma_0^2)$. Given the $\mu_i$, the $\bar R_i$ are independent, with $\bar R_i\sim\mathcal N(\mu_i,\sigma^2/T)$ and one common noise variance. By (24.3), with $\bar R_i$ as sector $i$'s data, every Bayes estimate $\mu_0+c(\bar R_i-\mu_0)$ moves toward $\mu_0$ by the same weight $c$.

The data can estimate $\mu_0$ and $c$ themselves: before the $\mu_i$ are known, the $\bar R_i$ are iid $\mathcal N(\mu_0,\sigma_0^2+\sigma^2/T)$ by (16.7). Their grand mean $\bar{\bar R}:=\frac1N\sum_i\bar R_i$ estimates $\mu_0$, and their spread estimates $\sigma_0^2+\sigma^2/T$, the denominator of $1-c$. Plugging both in gives an **empirical Bayes** estimate (Efron and Morris 1973).

> [!theorem] James–Stein estimator toward the grand mean
> Let $N\ge4$ estimates $\bar R_1,\dots,\bar R_N$ satisfy:
> 1. each $\bar R_i\sim\mathcal N(\mu_i,\sigma^2/T)$;
> 2. the $\bar R_i$ are independent;
> 3. the noise variance $\sigma^2/T$ is common to all of them and known.
>
> Define
> $$\begin{gathered}\hat\mu_i^{\mathrm{JS}}=\bar{\bar R}+\hat c\,(\bar R_i-\bar{\bar R}),\\ \hat c=\max\Big(0,\ 1-\frac{(N-3)\,\sigma^2/T}{\sum_i(\bar R_i-\bar{\bar R})^2}\Big).\end{gathered} \tag{24.4}$$
> Then, for every $(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_N)$, the expected total squared error $\E\sum_i(\hat\mu_i^{\mathrm{JS}}-\mu_i)^2$ is below the raw means' $N\sigma^2/T$. Stated without proof (Efron and Hastie 2016, §7.1 and §7.5).

When the means spread several times their noise, $\hat c$ is near 1. When their spread (divisor $N$) is at most $\sqrt{(N-3)/N}$ times the noise, 0.945 for 28 means, $\hat c=0$, and every estimate is the grand mean. James and Stein (1961) shrank toward a fixed point. The $\max$ with 0, the **positive part**, stops the weight from turning negative and pushing estimates past the grand mean.

How can a biased rule beat the raw means? In the normal model each $\bar R_i$ attains the Cramér–Rao bound (20.7), so no unbiased estimator has a smaller variance. James–Stein accepts bias toward $\bar{\bar R}$ and removes more variance than the bias adds, as (20.1) allows. Applied by Efron and Morris (1975) to 18 players' batting averages after their first 45 at-bats of 1970, it had less than half the raw averages' squared error.

> [!deepdive]- Why $N-3$
> Under the model, the $\bar R_i$ are iid $\mathcal N(\mu_0,\sigma_0^2+\sigma^2/T)$. By Blitzstein and Hwang's Example 10.4.3, $\sum_i(\bar R_i-\bar{\bar R})^2/(\sigma_0^2+\sigma^2/T)$ then has the law $\chi^2_{N-1}$ of [[LM16]]. The $\chi^2_\nu$ density is $x^{\nu/2-1}e^{-x/2}/\big(2^{\nu/2}\Gamma(\nu/2)\big)$ for $x>0$ (stated without proof). For $\nu>2$, the substitution $x=2y$ in the integral of (15.8) and its recursion for $\Gamma$ give
> $$\begin{aligned}\E\Big[\frac1{\chi^2_\nu}\Big]&=\frac{\int_0^\infty x^{\nu/2-2}e^{-x/2}\,dx}{2^{\nu/2}\Gamma(\nu/2)}\\&=\frac{2^{\nu/2-1}\Gamma(\nu/2-1)}{2^{\nu/2}\Gamma(\nu/2)}=\frac1{\nu-2}.\end{aligned}$$
> With $\nu=N-1$, the expectation of $(N-3)(\sigma^2/T)/\sum_i(\bar R_i-\bar{\bar R})^2$ is $(\sigma^2/T)/(\sigma_0^2+\sigma^2/T)=1-c$. So $1-\hat c$, before the clip at 0, is an unbiased estimate of $1-c$. The grand mean uses one degree of freedom, and the expectation needs $\nu>2$: hence $N\ge4$.

> [!example] Experiment 1: shrinkage when the model holds {#exp1}
> Simulate 10,000 cross-sections of $N=28$ true means $\mu_i\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ and estimates $\bar R_i=\mu_i+$ noise, with noise $\mathcal N(0,1)$, so that $\sigma_0^2=\sigma^2/T=1$. Use `default_rng([1, 20261011])`: one 10,000×28 array of true means, then one of noise. Compare the average total squared error of the raw means, James–Stein without the clip, its positive part (24.4), and the Bayes rule $\tfrac12\bar R_i$, which knows $\mu_0=0$ and $c=\tfrac12$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The averages are 28.1 for the raw means, 15.5 for James–Stein and for its positive part, and 14.0 for the Bayes rule. Their standard errors are 0.08, 0.04, 0.04 and 0.04, and the mean of $\hat c$ is 0.50 (standard error 0.001).
> >
> > In theory, the raw means' error is $N\sigma^2/T=28$, and the Bayes rule's is $Nc\,\sigma^2/T=14$ by (24.3). James–Stein's average, $N-(N-3)/(1+\sigma_0^2)=28-25/2=15.5$, is stated without proof (Efron and Hastie 2016, equation 7.14). So estimating $\mu_0$ and $c$ costs 1.5 of the 14 that knowing them saves. (*Model.*)

> [!pitfall] A target chosen after seeing the data
> The theorem fixes the target in advance: the grand mean, or a number chosen before the data. Shrinking toward the best-looking sector, or toward a value picked because it fits, uses the data twice, and the guarantee is lost. Shrinking regression betas, hierarchical models and Markov chain Monte Carlo are not in this book.

## Sector Means Out of Sample {lo=5}

Return to the case, with U28's first half as the data and its second half as the judge, a split that [[LM11]] fixed. Two numbers decide (24.4): the noise of one sector's mean and the spread of the 28 means.

> [!example] James–Stein on the sector halves {#halves}
> On U28's first half ($T=2{,}191$, divisor $T$), take the common noise variance to be the average of the 28 values $s_i^2/T$. Find the noise and the spread of the 28 annual means, $\hat c$, and the out-of-sample misses at $c=0$ and $c=1$. Repeat after removing each day's mean of the 28 returns.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The noise of one annual mean is $A$ times the root of the average $s_i^2/T$: 7.18 points. The 28 annual means spread by only 4.50 points (divisor 28). By (24.4), $1-\hat c=25\times7.18^2/(28\times4.50^2)=2.27$, so $\hat c=0$, and every sector is shrunk to the grand mean, 10.49%.
> >
> > Judged on the second half, the grand mean misses by 8.5 points and the own means by 9.8 ([[ex:oos]]). The best weight in hindsight, the slope of second-half deviations on first-half ones, is −0.09: the deviations reversed slightly.
> >
> > Removing each day's mean of the 28 returns leaves the deviations of the 28 means unchanged, but it cuts the noise to 4.79 points. Then $1-\hat c=1.01$, and $\hat c=0$ again, by a hair. The second half's means spread by 8.49 points, almost twice the first half's: the true means moved, and no first-half estimate could know where.

[[eg:halves]] uses one noise variance for all 28 sectors, but the sectors' noise variances differ by a factor of about 4 (half-widths 9 to 19 points in [[ex:forest]]). Their average, estimated from 2,191 returns, is treated as known. With the total noise, (24.4) gives $\hat c=0$ even if every sector had the smallest noise, 4.8 points (the largest is 9.7), so $\hat c=0$ does not depend on the averaging.

[[ex:forest]] is consistent with the zero weight: every first-half interval contains the grand mean.

The theorem assumes independent estimates, but sectors share the market's moves. The common part of the noise cancels in the deviations $\bar R_i-\bar{\bar R}$ yet stays in $\sigma^2/T$, so $\hat c$ is biased toward full shrinkage. Subtracting each day's cross-sectional mean removes it, and the verdict stands; a market-model alpha ([[LM23]]) is another way.

But the 28 subtracted series sum to zero each day, still co-move and have unequal noise, so no dominance theorem covers them. "James–Stein shrank fully" describes these 28 means, not a law. Jorion (1986) carried an empirical-Bayes estimate of means into portfolio choice ([[LM30]]).

The misses, the hindsight slope and the case's correlation come from one fixed split of 28 co-moving sectors, so they carry no standard error. The reverse direction and the lab's thirds show how much the misses move; in the thirds the correlation is 0.13 and 0.24, and the hindsight slope 0.09 and 0.61.

## Kelly with an Uncertain Mean {lo=6,7}

[[LM4]] advised betting a quarter to a half of the Kelly fraction. Thorp (2006, §7.3) and MacLean, Thorp and Ziemba (2010) give prudential reasons, and Rising and Wyner (2012) trace the benefit to reduced estimation error. A stated model turns the rule of thumb into a result:

1. the index follows geometric Brownian motion with known volatility $\sigma$ and unknown drift $\mu$;
2. the prior is $\mu\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_0^2)$, centred at zero;
3. the investor holds a fixed fraction $f$ and maximizes expected log wealth;
4. borrowing costs nothing, $r_f=0$.

Under geometric Brownian motion ([[LM38]]) a fixed fraction grows at exactly $f\mu-\tfrac12f^2\sigma^2$ a day, and [[LM42]] derives Kelly in continuous time. Given $\mu$, expected log growth over $\tau$ years is therefore $(f\mu-\tfrac12f^2\sigma^2)A\tau$. The investor does not know $\mu$ and averages over the posterior, in which the growth is linear:

$$\begin{aligned}\E\Big[\ln\frac{W_\tau}{W_0}\Bigm|\text{data}\Big]&=\Big(f\,\E[\mu\mid\text{data}]\\&\qquad-\tfrac12f^2\sigma^2\Big)A\tau.\end{aligned}$$

> [!result] Bayes Kelly fraction
> In the model above, the fraction that maximizes posterior-expected log growth, over any horizon $\tau$, is
> $$f_{\mathrm B}=\frac{\E[\mu\mid\text{data}]}{\sigma^2}=c\,\hat f,\qquad\hat f=\frac{\bar R}{\sigma^2}, \tag{24.5}$$
> where the second form uses the prior centred at zero and (24.3). Applying (24.3) to the daily simple returns is an approximation under geometric Brownian motion. For daily returns the same follows from (4.2) without its $\mu^2$ term.

Fractional Kelly is thus the Bayes bet, and its fraction is the data's weight $c$. Browne and Whitt (1996) derive the general rule under log utility: the bet is linear in the posterior mean.

> [!example] TAIEX's Bayes fraction and its exact value {#bayeskelly}
> Take $A\sigma_0=10$ points. Find $f_{\mathrm B}$, the exact fraction on returns shifted to the posterior mean, the posterior SD of the model's $f^*=\mu/\sigma^2$, and the expected log growth.
>
> > [!solution]
> > By (24.5), $f_{\mathrm B}=0.869\times4.28=3.72$. The exact Kelly condition (4.5) on TR23's returns, each shifted by one constant to the posterior mean ([[LM4]]'s band method), gives 3.62. Averaging the sample's growth over the posterior of $\mu$ before maximizing gives 3.62 too.
> >
> > The posterior SD of $f^*=\mu/\sigma^2$ is $\sqrt c$ times [[LM20]]'s mean-only standard error 1.08, or 1.01, so $f^*$ lies between 1.7 and 5.7 with posterior probability 95%.
> >
> > The quadratic rule overstates the exact fraction by 0.14 at $c=1$ (4.28 against [[LM17]]'s $\hat f^*=4.14$), by 0.10 at $c=0.869$, and by 0.03 at $c=\tfrac12$ (2.14 against 2.11). Keeping the $\mu^2$ term of (4.2), which (24.5) drops, gives $\bar R/(s^2+\bar R^2)=4.27$ at $c=1$: it explains only 0.01 of the gap. The other 0.13 comes from the days' fat tails and negative skewness ([[LM19]]). These shave a few per cent off a fraction; the prior shaves 13% ([[ex:kelly]]).
> >
> > In the model, the posterior-expected log growth is about 25% a year at $f_{\mathrm B}$. The plug-in bet $\hat f=f_{\mathrm B}/c$ keeps $1-(1-1/0.869)^2=97.7\%$ of it, by (4.15) with $1/c$ as its fraction.

> [!example] The sceptics behind LM4's rule {#sceptics}

> > [!solution]

And $c=\tfrac12$ needs $A\sigma_0=3.89$ points, a prior worth the sample. After the data, both sceptics put high probability on a positive mean, 97.6% and 99.7% ([[ex:priors]]), yet they bet little. Size depends on how large the mean is, not only on its sign.
> >
> > Under the half-Kelly sceptic's posterior, the plug-in bet is twice the Bayes bet. By (4.15) it expects zero growth, against 8.2% a year at 2.14. Under the quarter-Kelly sceptic's posterior, it is four times the Bayes bet and expects −16.5% a year: $1-(1-4)^2=-8$ times the Bayes bet's 2.06%.

Four departures break the result:

- **A prior centred away from zero.** The Bayes bet is still $\E[\mu\mid\text{data}]/\sigma^2$, but no longer $c\hat f$ (knowledge check).
- **An unknown $\sigma$.** Under the conjugate prior of Gelman et al. (2013, §3.3), whose prior SD of $\mu$ is proportional to $\sigma$, the posterior of $\mu$ is a Student $t$. At $T=5{,}849$ this matters little. Volatility that changes from day to day is modelled in [[LM26]].
- **Non-normal days.** Fat tails and a negative skewness make the quadratic rule overstate the exact fraction ([[eg:bayeskelly]]).
- **Other utilities.** Power utility, under which fractional Kelly expresses risk aversion, is [[LM44]]'s subject, and sizing from a bootstrap distribution is [[LM45]]'s.

> [!pitfall] The prior is the decision
> Under (24.5) the fraction is $c$, so choosing the prior is choosing the bet. A prior picked to justify a wanted fraction repeats Section 5's error of a target chosen after the data. State the prior and its worth in years, as [[ex:priors]] does, and read the result as a model result, not advice. Haircuts for a backtest's Sharpe ratio are [[LM33]]'s.

> [!zh] 分數凱利是懷疑者的貝氏下注
> 第 7 節的模型假設波動率（volatility）已知、事前分布（prior distribution）以零為中心、目標是期望對數成長（expected log growth）。在這個模型裡，使事後期望對數成長最大的部位是 $f_{\mathrm B}=\E[\mu\mid\text{data}]/\sigma^2=c\hat f$，而且與投資期間長短無關。所以分數凱利（fractional Kelly）就是貝氏下注（Bayes bet），比例恰好是資料權重（data's weight）$c$。要得到 [[LM4]] 的四分之一或一半凱利，懷疑者的事前標準差（prior standard deviation）必須是每年 2.25 或 3.89 個百分點，相當於 71.3 年或 23.8 年的資料。在半凱利懷疑者的事後分布（posterior distribution）下，直接用估計值（estimate）下注就是兩倍凱利，期望成長為零。事前分布若不以零為中心，這個對應就不成立；報酬有厚尾（heavy tail）與負偏態（negative skewness）時，它也只是近似。

>
> > [!answer]-
> > By (24.3), the posterior mean is $8\%+\tfrac12(15.38\%-8\%)=11.69\%$ a year. By (24.5), $f_{\mathrm B}=11.69/15.38\times4.28=3.25$, which is 0.76 of $\hat f$, not $c\hat f=2.14$. The fraction reading needs a prior centred at zero.

## Bayesian Inference in Python {lo=8}

SciPy's `stats.beta` gives a beta law's mean, quantiles and tail probabilities; for integer parameters, the lab computes them exactly with `Fraction`. The functions below pass every divisor explicitly.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import optimize, stats

post = stats.beta(82 + 8, 82 + 4)      # sceptic's prior, 8 good months of 12
print(post.mean(), post.sf(0.55))      # 0.5114, 0.1526


def posterior(Rbar, s, T, sigma0, mu0=0.0):     # (24.3), daily units
    c = sigma0**2 / (sigma0**2 + s**2 / T)
    return c, mu0 + c * (Rbar - mu0), np.sqrt(c) * s / np.sqrt(T)


def james_stein(X):                    # X: T x N daily returns, (24.4)
    T, N = X.shape
    m = X.mean(axis=0)
    noise = (X.var(axis=0, ddof=0) / T).mean()  # common noise variance
    c_hat = max(0.0, 1 - (N - 3) * noise / ((m - m.mean()) ** 2).sum())
    return c_hat, m.mean() + c_hat * (m - m.mean())


def kelly_exact(R, target):            # root of (4.5), returns shifted
    x = R - R.mean() + target
    return optimize.brentq(lambda f: (x / (1 + f * x)).mean(),
                           1e-6, 1 / abs(x.min()) - 1e-6)
```

## Summary {-}

- Bayes' rule for a parameter (24.1) multiplies a prior density by the likelihood; a beta prior and a binomial record give a beta posterior (24.2).
- Each posterior mean moves from the prior mean toward the data by the data's weight $c$; for a normal mean with known variance, precisions add (24.3).
- A credible interval is a probability statement about the parameter; a confidence interval describes a procedure.
- James–Stein (24.4) estimates the weight from the spread of many means and beats the raw means under its conditions.
- On U28's halves $\hat c=0$: the 28 sector means carry almost nothing beyond their average.
- In the stated model the Bayes Kelly fraction is $c\hat f$ (24.5). Quarter and half Kelly are the bets of sceptics whose priors are worth three times and once the sample.

## Solutions {-}

1. **B** is correct. By (24.2), the posterior is Beta(7, 9), with mean $7/16=0.4375$; equivalently, $0.3+\tfrac{6}{16}(\tfrac46-0.3)$. A is the data alone, $c=1$. C, $7/10$, leaves $n$ out of the denominator.

2. **C** is correct: $c=6^2/(6^2+4^2)=36/52=0.69$. A, $6/(6+4)=0.60$, is a ratio of standard deviations. B, $16/52=0.31$, is the prior's weight.

3. **A** is correct. Precisions add, so the posterior SD is $1/\sqrt{1/36+1/16}=3.33$, which is also $\sqrt c\times4$. B adds the variances, $\sqrt{52}=7.21$. C treats standard deviations as variances, $6\times4/10=2.40$.

4. **B** is correct: $\hat c=1-(10-3)/18=0.611$. A, $1-8/18=0.556$, uses $N-2$, the rule toward a fixed point. C, $7/18=0.389$, is $1-\hat c$.

5. **A** is correct: $f_{\mathrm B}=0.4\times3.0=1.20$ by (24.5). B uses $\sqrt c$, $1.90$. C uses $c^2$, $0.48$.

6. **C** is correct. The plug-in bet is $f_{\mathrm B}/c=2f_{\mathrm B}$, twice the Bayes Kelly bet, and (4.15) with fraction 2 gives $1-(1-2)^2=0$. A, $1-(1-\tfrac12)^2=75\%$, is half Kelly read the wrong way round. B is $c$, the ratio of the bets.

7. (a) See the Deep Dive of Section 3.
   (b) Here $c=25/(25+3.89^2)=0.623$. The posterior mean's variance is $c\,\sigma_0^2=15.6$ points² and the expected posterior variance $(1-c)\sigma_0^2=9.4$; they sum to the prior's 25.0.

8. (a) See the Deep Dive of Section 5.
   (b) The sum of squared deviations over $\sigma_0^2+\sigma^2/T$ is $\chi^2_{N-1}$, so the expectation is $(N-3)(\sigma^2/T)\big/\big((N-3)(\sigma_0^2+\sigma^2/T)\big)=1-c$.
   (c) At $N=3$, the degrees of freedom are $\nu=2$, and the integral of $x^{-1}e^{-x/2}$ diverges at 0, so $\E[1/\chi^2_2]$ is infinite. The factor $N-3$ is 0, so $\hat c=1$: the rule returns the raw means and has nothing to estimate the weight with. Hence $N\ge4$.

9. On the annual scale, $c=25/(25+3.89^2)=0.623$. The posterior mean is $0.623\times15.38\%=9.58\%$, or 9.6% to one decimal, and the SD is $\sqrt{0.623}\times3.89=3.07$ points, or 3.1. From these two-decimal values, the 95% credible interval runs from 3.6% to 15.6%, and $\Pr(\mu\le0\mid\text{data})=0.0009$. The Bayes fraction is $0.623\times4.28=2.67$, and the prior is worth $(18.96/5)^2=14.4$ years.

10. At spreads 0, 1 and 3, the raw means average 28.0 each time. James–Stein averages 3.0, 15.5 and 25.5, and its positive part 2.1, 15.5 and 25.5, with standard errors from 0.02 to 0.05; theory gives 3, 15.5 and 25.5. The positive part helps when the means hardly spread, because the unclipped weight is then often negative.

11. The noise falls to 5.0 points, $1-\hat c=1.10$, and $\hat c=0$ again, but the grand mean misses by 12.1 points. Subtracting the index shifts every sector's mean in a half by the same amount, so the deviations from each half's grand mean, and their miss of 8.49, do not change. The sectors' average excess over the index moved from −0.3 to −9.0 points a year, as the index rose 10.8% and then 19.1% a year. No first-half estimate sees that common shift.

12. By (24.2), the posterior is Beta(98, 90), with mean 0.521, and exactly $\Pr(\theta\ge0.55\mid\text{record})=21.6\%$, against 18.02%. The two-type prior allows only 0.5 and 0.55. The beta prior also puts mass above 0.55, where a record of $16/24=0.667$ is more likely, so more posterior mass lies past 0.55.

## Glossary {-}

| Term | 中文 | Meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Bayesian inference | 貝氏推論 | Treating an unknown parameter as uncertain, with a prior updated to a posterior by Bayes' rule. |
| Prior distribution | 事前分布 | The distribution of a parameter before the data. |
| Likelihood | 概度函數 | The probability or density of the data as a function of the parameter. |
| Posterior distribution | 事後分布 | The distribution of a parameter given the data, (24.1). |
| Beta distribution | 貝他(β)分布 | The law on $(0,1)$ with density proportional to $x^{\alpha_0-1}(1-x)^{\beta_0-1}$. |
| Conjugate prior | 共軛事前分布 | A prior whose posterior stays in its family, as beta with binomial data. |
| Data's weight | 資料權重 | $c$: the fraction of the gap from prior mean to data that the posterior mean moves. |
| Precision | 精確度 | One over a variance; posterior precision = prior precision + data precision. |
| Credible interval | 可靠區間 | An interval to which the posterior gives a stated probability. |
| Shrinkage estimator | 收縮估計量 | An estimator that pulls estimates toward a target. |
| Empirical Bayes estimator | 經驗貝氏估計量 | A Bayes estimate whose prior is estimated from the data. |
| James–Stein estimator | James-Stein 估計量 | (24.4): shrinks many means toward their grand mean by an estimated weight. |
| Grand mean | 總平均數 | The average of several means. |
| Fractional Kelly | 分數凱利 | A position $cf^*$ with $c<1$; in Section 7's model, the Bayes bet $c\hat f$. |
