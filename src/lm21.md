## Introduction

[[LM20]] attached a standard error to an estimate. A hypothesis test asks a sharper question: if the true value were zero, how surprising would the estimate be? This module tests a mean, a frequency and a table of counts on TAIEX data, then asks what happens when many tests run on the same data.

> [!case] The best day to own the TAIEX
> From 3 January 2003 to 8 October 2026 the TAIEX total-return index, with dividends reinvested and no costs, gained 0.0625% on an average day. Single days varied far more, with a standard deviation of 1.21%. Is the mean really positive? It lies 3.96 standard errors above zero: 2.05 before the daily price limit widened in June 2015 and 3.68 after it ([[ex:case]]).
>
> Now sort the same 5,849 days by calendar month and by weekday, 17 groups in all. Wednesdays stand out. They averaged 0.1332%, against 0.0446% on the other days, a gap of 2.36 standard errors with a p-value of 0.018, well below the usual 5%. Should an investor own the TAIEX only on Wednesdays?

## What a Test Decides {lo=1}

> [!example] Eight good months in twelve {#manager}
> [[LM13]]'s hypothetical manager beat the index in 8 of 12 months, independent trials with probability $\theta$ of a good month. Is the record evidence of skill, $\theta>\tfrac12$? Find the p-value, the 5% rejection region and its size, and the chance of a rejection at [[LM13]]'s $\theta=0.55$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > Under $H_0:\theta=\tfrac12$ the number $X$ of good months is $\operatorname{Bin}(12,\tfrac12)$, by (15.1). Many good months speak for $H_1:\theta>\tfrac12$, so the p-value is the upper tail $\Pr(X\ge8)=794/4{,}096=0.194$. The record is not significant at 5%.
> >
> > The 5% test rejects when $X\ge x$ for the smallest $x$ with $\Pr(X\ge x)\le0.05$. Since $\Pr(X\ge9)=0.073$ and $\Pr(X\ge10)=79/4{,}096=0.0193$, the region is $X\ge10$. Its size, 0.0193, lies below the level.
> >
> > For a skilled manager, $\operatorname{Bin}(12,0.55)$ gives $\Pr(X\ge10)=0.042$, so the test misses one with probability 0.958. [[LM13]]'s posterior of 13.5% answers another question, the probability of skill given the record ([[LM24]]).

> [!definition] Hypothesis test
> A **null hypothesis** $H_0$ fixes a parameter's value, such as $\theta=\tfrac12$. The **alternative** $H_1$ says where the value lies otherwise: on one side, $\theta>\tfrac12$ (**one-sided**), or on both, $\mu\ne0$ (**two-sided**). Both are fixed before the data are seen. A **test statistic** is computed from the data; its **null law** is its distribution under $H_0$. The test rejects $H_0$ when the statistic falls in a **rejection region**. Its probability under $H_0$, the test's **size**, is at most the **level** $\alpha$.

> [!definition] p-value
> The **p-value** of an observed statistic is the probability under $H_0$ of a statistic at least as extreme, in the direction of $H_1$. The test at level $\alpha$ rejects exactly when the p-value is at most $\alpha$. For a statistic that is approximately $\mathcal N(0,1)$ under $H_0$, the p-value is
> $$\begin{aligned}\text{two-sided:}&\quad2\Phi(-\lvert\text{t-stat}\rvert),\\ \text{one-sided, }H_1\text{ above:}&\quad\Phi(-\text{t-stat}).\end{aligned} \tag{21.2}$$

Under a continuous null law the p-value is uniform on $(0,1)$, so $\Pr(\text{p-value}\le u)=u$; this is the inverse transform (18.1) run backwards. A true null therefore gives a p-value below 0.05 one time in twenty.

Two kinds of mistake are possible.

| Decision | $H_0$ true | $H_0$ false |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Reject $H_0$ | type I error, probability at most $\alpha$ | correct, probability = power |
| Do not reject | correct | type II error, probability = 1 − power |

A **type I error** rejects a true null, and a **type II error** keeps a false one. The **power** of a test is its probability of rejecting when a given alternative is true: 0.042 in [[eg:manager]]. The likelihood-ratio test and the Neyman–Pearson lemma are not in this book (Wasserman 2004, §10.6 and §10.10.1).

> [!example] Up days {#updays}
> TR23 has 3,205 up days among its 5,849 returns, and no return is exactly zero. Test $H_0:\theta=\tfrac12$, a day as likely to rise as to fall, against $H_1:\theta>\tfrac12$. Treat the days as independent trials.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The frequency is $\hat\theta=3{,}205/5{,}849=0.548$. Under $H_0$ the count is $\operatorname{Bin}(5{,}849,\tfrac12)$, so $\hat\theta$ has variance $1/(4T)$ by (15.2). By (17.5) the statistic
> > $$z=\frac{\hat\theta-\tfrac12}{\sqrt{1/(4T)}}=7.3$$
> > is approximately standard normal. The exact binomial tail is $\Pr(X\ge3{,}205)=1.2\times10^{-13}$, so up days beat a fair coin. Both null laws assume independent days, which Section 5 checks.

> [!pitfall] A p-value is not the probability that $H_0$ is true
> The p-value is a probability of data given $H_0$, not of $H_0$ given the data; [[LM24]] computes the latter, as [[LM13]] did for the manager. "Not rejected" is not "true" either: [[eg:manager]]'s test has power 4%, so it keeps $H_0$ for most skilled managers. And "significant" is not "large": Wednesday's edge is 0.0886% a day.

> [!zh] 假設檢定在問什麼
> 假設檢定（hypothesis testing）問的是：如果什麼都沒有，資料會有多令人意外？虛無假設（null hypothesis）說「沒有效果」，對立假設（alternative hypothesis）說效果在哪一邊，兩者都要在看資料之前定好。p值（p-value）是在虛無假設成立時，出現至少這麼極端的結果的機率（probability），不是虛無假設為真的機率。p值不超過顯著水準（significance level）就拒絕虛無假設。拒絕了真的虛無假設，叫第一型錯誤（type I error）；沒拒絕假的虛無假設，叫第二型錯誤（type II error）。經理人 12 個月勝 8 個月，p值 0.194，不顯著；就算他真有實力，這個檢定（test）也只有 4% 的機會拒絕。

> [!kc]
> A rule produced 60 up days in 100 sessions. Test $\theta=\tfrac12$, two-sided, with the normal approximation. Do you reject at 5%? At 1%?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > Here $z=(0.6-0.5)/\sqrt{0.25/100}=2.00$, so the two-sided p-value is $2\Phi(-2)=0.0455$. Reject at 5%, not at 1%.

## Testing a Mean Return {lo=2}

[[LM20]] derives $\operatorname{SE}(\bar R)=\sigma/\sqrt T$, (20.2), and the 95% interval (20.4). This section tests the value that the interval estimates.

> [!result] The one-sample t-test
> Let $R_1,\dots,R_T$ be iid with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2<\infty$, and test $H_0:\mu=\mu_{\text{null}}$ with
> $$\text{t-stat}=\frac{\bar R-\mu_{\text{null}}}{s_{\mathrm u}/\sqrt T}. \tag{21.1}$$
> Under $H_0$, the t-stat is approximately $\mathcal N(0,1)$ for large $T$. If the returns are iid normal, it has exactly the $t_{T-1}$ law for every $T\ge2$.

The large-$T$ law follows from (17.5), because $s_{\mathrm u}$ settles at $\sigma$ ([[LM20]]). Here "large" means thousands of days.

The exact law takes one line. For iid normal returns, Blitzstein and Hwang (2019) show that $\bar R$ and $s_{\mathrm u}^2$ are independent (Example 7.5.9) and that $V:=(T-1)s_{\mathrm u}^2/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{T-1}$ (Example 10.4.3). With $Z:=\sqrt T(\bar R-\mu)/\sigma$, their Definition 10.4.4 makes $Z/\sqrt{V/(T-1)}$ a $t_{T-1}$ variable. The $\sigma$'s cancel, so this ratio is (21.1) at $\mu_{\text{null}}=\mu$.

The $t_{T-1}$ law has heavier tails than $\mathcal N(0,1)$, but at $T=5{,}849$ the two nearly coincide ([[ex:nulldist]]).

> [!example] Is TAIEX's mean positive? {#mean}
> Test $H_0:\mu=0$ against $H_1:\mu\ne0$ on TR23 at the 5% level, with [[LM19]]'s $\bar R=0.0625\%$ and $s_{\mathrm u}=1.2085\%$ a day.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The standard error is $s_{\mathrm u}/\sqrt T=1.2085\%/\sqrt{5849}=0.0158\%$, and (21.1) gives $\text{t-stat}=3.96$ from unrounded inputs. By (21.2) the two-sided p-value is $7.6\times10^{-5}$. The $t_{5848}$ law gives $7.7\times10^{-5}$, and the one-sided p-value is $3.8\times10^{-5}$. Reject $H_0$: the mean is positive at any usual level. With $s$ in place of $s_{\mathrm u}$ the t-stat is still 3.956 to three decimals, so at this $T$ the divisor does not matter.

[[LM20]]'s 95% interval for the annual mean, 7.8% to 23.0% a year, excludes 0: the same decision at 5%. With the same standard error, a two-sided test at level $\alpha$ rejects $\mu_{\text{null}}$ exactly when $\mu_{\text{null}}$ lies outside $\bar R\pm\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)\operatorname{SE}$. An interval is the set of null values that the test keeps.

The conditions matter more than normality. By (16.3), uncorrelated returns give $\Var\bar R=\sigma^2/T$ even when volatility clusters, while autocorrelated returns change it. TR23's log returns have a lag-1 autocorrelation of 0.0393 ([[LM19]]), and [[LM23]]'s HAC errors correct for autocorrelation.

> [!result] Two-sample statistic with unpooled variances
> Let two independent groups of $T_1$ and $T_2$ returns each be iid, with means $\mu_1$, $\mu_2$ and finite variances. Under $H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2$, for large $T_1$ and $T_2$,
> $$\frac{\bar R_1-\bar R_2}{\sqrt{s_{\mathrm u,1}^2/T_1+s_{\mathrm u,2}^2/T_2}} \tag{21.3}$$
> is approximately $\mathcal N(0,1)$.

The denominator estimates $\sqrt{\Var(\bar R_1-\bar R_2)}$, which (16.3) gives for independent groups. SciPy computes (21.3) as `ttest_ind(..., equal_var=False)`, with a $t$ reference law.

> [!example] Did the mean change in June 2015? {#regimes}
> Before 1 June 2015 TR23's mean was 0.0464% a day, with a t-stat of 2.05; from that date it was 0.0804%, with 3.68. The standard errors are 0.0227% and 0.0219% ([[ex:case]]). Treat the regimes as independent groups. Is the difference significant at 5%?
>
> > [!solution]
> > The standard error of the difference is $\sqrt{0.0227^2+0.0219^2}=0.0315\%$. So (21.3) gives $(0.0804-0.0464)/0.0315=1.08$, a two-sided p-value of 0.28; SciPy's $t$ reference gives 0.280. One period passes 1% and the other 5%, yet the difference between them is not significant. Stability check.

A change in variance between the regimes needs [[LM20]]'s standard error of a volatility, and volatility clustering needs [[LM22]]'s blocks of days. That standard error needs a finite fourth moment, which [[LM15]]'s Student $t$ fit ($\nu=3.065$) lacks, so it is nominal. [[LM20]] gives the kurtosis no standard error at all.

> [!pitfall] Significant in one period, not in the other
> "Significant after 2015, not before" does not show that the mean changed. Test the difference itself: [[eg:regimes]] gives 1.08.

> [!kc]
> A strategy averages 0.04% a day with $s_{\mathrm u}=1\%$ over 900 days. Find its t-stat. With the same mean and $s_{\mathrm u}$, how many days give a t-stat of 2?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > By (21.1), $0.04/(1/\sqrt{900})=1.20$. A t-stat of 2 needs $\sqrt T=2/0.04=50$, that is 2,500 days, about ten years.

## Power and the Years a Sharpe Ratio Needs {lo=3}

Let $\mathrm{SR}=\sqrt A\,\mu/\sigma$ be the annual Sharpe ratio of iid returns with known $\sigma$ and $r_f=0$. Over $\tau$ years, $T=A\tau$, so the statistic $\sqrt T\bar R/\sigma$ is approximately $\mathcal N(\sqrt T\mu/\sigma,1)$, and $\sqrt T\mu/\sigma=\mathrm{SR}\sqrt\tau$. The two-sided test rejects when $\lvert\text{t-stat}\rvert\ge\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)$, and (5.5) gives the probability of each tail.

> [!result] Power of the test of a mean
> Let returns be iid with known $\sigma$ and annual Sharpe ratio $\mathrm{SR}$ ($r_f=0$), observed for $\tau$ years. At level $\alpha$, the two-sided test of $\mu=0$ has
> $$\begin{aligned}\text{power}&\approx\Phi\big(\mathrm{SR}\sqrt\tau-\Phi^{-1}(1-\tfrac\alpha2)\big)\\&\quad+\Phi\big(-\mathrm{SR}\sqrt\tau-\Phi^{-1}(1-\tfrac\alpha2)\big),\\ \tau&\approx\Big(\frac{\Phi^{-1}(1-\frac\alpha2)+\Phi^{-1}(\text{power})}{\mathrm{SR}}\Big)^2,\end{aligned} \tag{21.4}$$
> the second when the first line's second term is negligible.

[[LM19]] annualizes a daily Sharpe ratio by $\sqrt A$, a convention on simple returns; even for log returns it needs uncorrelated days (Lo 2002). With $s$ in place of $\sigma$ the identity is exact on data: $\sqrt T\bar R/s=\mathrm{SR}\sqrt\tau$.

> [!example] Years of data for 80% power {#years}
> How many years of daily data give 80% power at the 5% level against annual Sharpe ratios of 1, 0.5 and 0.25? And against TR23's own, [[LM19]]'s $\sqrt{246.12}\times0.0625\%/1.2084\%=0.81$?
>
> > [!solution]
> > With $\Phi^{-1}(0.975)=1.959964$ and $\Phi^{-1}(0.8)=0.841621$, (21.4) gives $\tau=(2.801585/\mathrm{SR})^2$: 7.85, 31.4 and 125.6 years. TR23's ratio, 0.8115 to four decimals, needs 11.9 years. Its t-stat is $0.8115\times\sqrt{23.7645}=3.956$, by the identity above. If 0.81 were the true ratio, TR23's 23.76 years would give power 0.98; at 0.5 the power would be only 0.68 ([[ex:power]]). Model.

> [!pitfall] More frequent data do not buy power
> Power depends on $\mathrm{SR}\sqrt\tau$, the span in years, not on the number of observations. Hourly returns over the same 23.76 years would not shrink the standard error of the annual mean; [[LM20]] shows why (Merton 1980).

> [!zh] 檢定力：為什麼 23.8 年還不夠
> 檢定力（power）是對立假設（alternative hypothesis）為真時，檢定（test）拒絕虛無假設（null hypothesis）的機率（probability）。平均報酬（mean return）的 t 統計量（t-statistic）約等於年化夏普比率（annualized Sharpe ratio）乘以資料年數的平方根，所以標準誤（standard error）只隨年數縮小，與每天或每小時抽樣（sampling）無關。在 5% 的顯著水準（significance level）下要有 80% 的檢定力，夏普比率為 0.5 需要 31.4 年的日資料，0.25 更需要 125.6 年。加權股價報酬指數有 23.8 年資料，對 0.5 的檢定力也只有 0.68。回測（backtest）幾年就宣稱找到優勢，多半還分不出真假。

> [!kc]
> At an annual Sharpe ratio of 0.5, what power does the two-sided 5% test have with ten years of data? How many years make the expected t-stat equal to 3 at $\mathrm{SR}=1$ and at $\mathrm{SR}=0.5$?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > By (21.4), the power is
> > $$\begin{aligned}&\Phi(0.5\sqrt{10}-1.96)\\&\quad+\Phi(-0.5\sqrt{10}-1.96)=0.35.\end{aligned}$$
> > The expected t-stat is $\mathrm{SR}\sqrt\tau$, so 3 needs $\tau=(3/\mathrm{SR})^2$: 9 years at $\mathrm{SR}=1$ and 36 at 0.5.

## Testing Frequencies and Independence {lo=4}

[[LM13]] counted 1,226 down days after a down day, against 1,195.0 under independence: 31 days in 5,848. Is the gap larger than chance? [[ex:independence]] tests it and one more gap.

> [!result] The chi-square test of a 2×2 table
> Classify $n$ pairs by two indicators, with cells $n_{11}$, $n_{12}$, $n_{21}$, $n_{22}$, row totals $n_{1\cdot}$, $n_{2\cdot}$ and column totals $n_{\cdot1}$, $n_{\cdot2}$. By (13.6), the expected count of a cell under independence is its row total times its column total over $n$. Then
> $$\begin{gathered}\chi^2:=\sum_{\text{cells}}\frac{(\text{observed}-\text{expected})^2}{\text{expected}}\\=n\hat\rho^2,\end{gathered} \tag{21.5}$$
> with $\hat\rho$ the correlation of the two indicators over the $n$ pairs. If the pairs are iid and the two indicators independent, $\chi^2$ is approximately $\chi^2_1$ for large $n$.

For one series on consecutive days, $\hat\rho$ is, up to its first and last day, [[LM19]]'s lag-1 autocorrelation $\hat\rho_1$ of the indicator series. Under independent days $\sqrt T\hat\rho_1$ is approximately standard normal; this is [[LM19]]'s band (19.6). So $n\hat\rho^2$ is approximately a squared standard normal, which is $\chi^2_1$ by [[LM16]]'s definition. For two series on the same days, the $\chi^2_1$ law is stated without proof.

> [!deepdive]- Why the 2×2 $\chi^2$ is $n\hat\rho^2$
> Let $D:=n_{11}n_{22}-n_{12}n_{21}$. Cell (1,1) has observed minus expected $n_{11}-n_{1\cdot}n_{\cdot1}/n=D/n$, because $n\,n_{11}-(n_{11}+n_{12})(n_{11}+n_{21})=D$. The fixed margins force the other three cells to $\pm D/n$ as well. The reciprocals of the four expected counts sum to
> $$\begin{aligned}&\frac{n}{n_{1\cdot}n_{\cdot1}}+\frac{n}{n_{1\cdot}n_{\cdot2}}+\frac{n}{n_{2\cdot}n_{\cdot1}}+\frac{n}{n_{2\cdot}n_{\cdot2}}\\&\quad=\frac{n^3}{n_{1\cdot}n_{2\cdot}n_{\cdot1}n_{\cdot2}},\end{aligned}$$
> since $n_{1\cdot}+n_{2\cdot}=n_{\cdot1}+n_{\cdot2}=n$. Hence $\chi^2=nD^2/(n_{1\cdot}n_{2\cdot}n_{\cdot1}n_{\cdot2})$. The covariance of the two indicators is $D/n^2$, and their variances are $n_{1\cdot}n_{2\cdot}/n^2$ and $n_{\cdot1}n_{\cdot2}/n^2$ ([[LM16]]). So $\hat\rho=D/\sqrt{n_{1\cdot}n_{2\cdot}n_{\cdot1}n_{\cdot2}}$, and $\chi^2=n\hat\rho^2$.

> [!example] Down after down {#signs}
> Test the independence of TR23's signs on [[LM13]]'s 5,848 pairs. There were 1,226 downs after a down; yesterday's margins are 2,643 down and 3,205 up, and today's 2,644 and 3,204.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The expected counts are 1,194.95, 1,448.05, 1,449.05 and 1,755.95, as exact fractions. Each observed count differs from its expected count by 31.05. Then (21.5) gives $\chi^2=2.69$, or $5{,}848\times0.02143^2$, with $\hat\rho=0.0214$, [[LM15]]'s $\rho_1$. Its $\chi^2_1$ upper tail is a p-value of 0.10: no evidence against independent signs at 5%. SciPy's `chi2_contingency` applies Yates' continuity correction by default and prints 2.60, with a p-value of 0.107.

> [!example] Falls after falls {#falls}
> On the pair space, 108 days follow a fall of 3% or more, and 13 of them fell 3% or more too ([[LM13]]). Test whether such falls are independent of the day before.
>
> > [!solution]
> > Under independence each of the 108 days falls with probability $\theta_0=108/5{,}848$. The count is then $\operatorname{Bin}(108,\theta_0)$, with mean $108^2/5{,}848=2.0$. The exact one-sided tail is $\Pr(X\ge13)=1.2\times10^{-7}$. Large moves cluster, so the days are not independent. The null laws of [[eg:updays]] and [[eg:signs]] hold for the signs only approximately.

Clustering of large moves breaks the count tests of extreme days, such as [[eg:falls]]'s and [[LM16]]'s. A count is a sum of indicators, and (16.3) adds twice their covariances to their variances. Clustered extreme days make those covariances positive, so the count spreads more than the binomial says and the test rejects too often. [[LM22]]'s block bootstrap is the tool for this, and [[LM26]] models the clustering.

On U28, [[LM16]] found 177 days outside the normal model's whitened circle of radius 3, against a binomial mean of 48.68 and standard deviation of 6.94. From those printed counts, $z=(177-48.68)/6.94=18.5$. Clustering would have to widen the spread 6.2-fold, to 42.8 days, to bring $z$ down to 3.

[[LM16]]'s five-sector counts read as 4,382 tests on $\chi^2_5$ at the 1% level, one a day: 43.8 rejections expected, 291 found. Its crash correlation, 0.788 against the model's 0.860, needs the sampling law of a conditional correlation, which [[LM22]]'s block bootstrap estimates.

> [!pitfall] Two-proportion tests on clustered days
> Comparing the share of extreme days in two regimes, as [[LM16]]'s lab does, invites a test of two proportions. That test treats clustered days as independent, so its p-value is too small; [[LM22]]'s block bootstrap is the tool for this.

> [!kc]
> In 10,000 day pairs, how large must $\lvert\hat\rho\rvert$ be for the 2×2 $\chi^2$ to exceed the $\chi^2_1$ 5% point, 3.84?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > By (21.5), $10{,}000\,\hat\rho^2>3.84$ needs $\lvert\hat\rho\rvert>0.0196$, which is $1.96/\sqrt n$. The $\chi^2$ test and the two-sided band (19.6) agree.

The **family-wise error rate** (FWER) is the probability of at least one false rejection in the family. For 17 independent tests of true nulls at 5%, (13.7) gives $1-0.95^{17}=0.58$.

> [!result] Bonferroni's correction
> Let $m$ tests include $m_0$ true nulls, and run each test at level $\alpha/m$. Then, under any dependence,
> $$\text{FWER}\le m_0\frac{\alpha}{m}\le\alpha. \tag{21.6}$$
> For $m$ independent tests of true nulls, each of size $\alpha$ (as under a continuous null law), $\text{FWER}=1-(1-\alpha)^m$. Under any dependence, the expected number of false rejections is the sum of the true nulls' sizes, at most $m_0$ times each test's level.

The bound is (13.2) over the $m_0$ events "true null $i$ is rejected", each of probability at most $\alpha/m$. The independent case is (13.7), and the expected count is (14.1) with (14.3). For the calendar, Bonferroni's level is $0.05/17=0.00294$, or $\lvert z\rvert\ge2.974$. Equivalently, multiply each p-value by $m$ and compare with $\alpha$.

## Holm and the False Discovery Rate {lo=6}

Bonferroni gives every test the same level. Holm's rule starts at the same level and relaxes it after each rejection.

> [!result] Holm's procedure
> Order the p-values, $p_{(1)}\le\dots\le p_{(m)}$, and let $i_{\mathrm H}$ be the largest $i$ such that
> $$p_{(j)}\le\frac{\alpha}{m-j+1}\quad\text{for every }j\le i. \tag{21.7}$$
> Reject the hypotheses of $p_{(1)},\dots,p_{(i_{\mathrm H})}$, and none if $p_{(1)}$ misses. Then $\text{FWER}\le\alpha$ under any dependence (Holm 1979).

Holm compares $p_{(1)}$ with $\alpha/m$, then $p_{(2)}$ with $\alpha/(m-1)$, and stops at the first miss. Its levels are never below Bonferroni's.

> [!deepdive]- Why Holm controls the family-wise error rate
> Suppose Holm rejects a true null, and look at the first true null it rejects, at rank $i$. The $i-1$ hypotheses rejected before it are false nulls, so $i-1\le m-m_0$, that is $m-i+1\ge m_0$. This true null has the smallest p-value among the true nulls, and it passed: that p-value is at most $\alpha/(m-i+1)\le\alpha/m_0$.
>
> So a false rejection by Holm requires some true null with a p-value at most $\alpha/m_0$. Each true null's p-value is that small with probability at most $\alpha/m_0$. By (13.2) the event has probability at most $m_0\cdot\alpha/m_0=\alpha$, whatever the dependence.

When many tests run, avoiding every false rejection can cost too many true ones. The **false discovery rate** (FDR) is the expected share of false rejections among all rejections, counted as zero when nothing is rejected.

> [!result] The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure
> Order the p-values and let
> $$i_{\mathrm{BH}}:=\max\Big\{i:\ p_{(i)}\le\frac{i\,q}{m}\Big\}, \tag{21.8}$$
> or 0 if no rank passes. Reject the hypotheses of $p_{(1)},\dots,p_{(i_{\mathrm{BH}})}$. For independent test statistics the FDR is then at most $q$ (stated without proof; Benjamini and Hochberg 1995).

The rule steps up: it looks for the largest rank that passes, so a p-value above its own level can still be rejected. When every null is true, every rejection is false, and the FDR equals the FWER (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995). SciPy's `false_discovery_control` with `method="by"` (Benjamini and Yekutieli 2001, Theorem 1.3) runs (21.8) with $q$ divided by $\sum_{i=1}^m1/i$ (3.44 for $m=17$), and controls the FDR under any dependence. [[LM23]] applies these corrections to 28 sector alphas.

> [!example] Three rules on ten p-values {#holmbh}
> Ten tests give the p-values 0.002, 0.0054, 0.0061, 0.011, 0.024, 0.031, 0.09, 0.22, 0.47 and 0.86 (constructed). At $\alpha=q=5\%$, how many hypotheses do Bonferroni, Holm and Benjamini–Hochberg reject?
>
> > [!solution]
> > Bonferroni's level is $0.05/10=0.005$, and only 0.002 passes: 1 rejection. Holm's first four levels are 0.005, 0.00556, 0.00625 and 0.00714. The p-values 0.002, 0.0054 and 0.0061 pass, and 0.011 misses: 3 rejections.
> >
> > Benjamini–Hochberg's levels $iq/m$ run 0.005, 0.010, …, 0.050. The fifth rank passes, $0.024\le0.025$, and every later one misses, from $0.031>0.030$ and $0.09>0.035$ onwards: 5 rejections.
> >
> > On the calendar, $p_{(1)}=0.018$ exceeds 0.00294, the first level of Bonferroni and Holm at 5%, so neither rejects anything. Benjamini–Hochberg steps up, so every rank must be checked: each $p_{(i)}$ exceeds its level $iq/m$, at least threefold even at $q=10\%$ ([[ex:pvalues]]). Its adjusted p-values, $\min_{j\ge i}mp_{(j)}/j$ (at most 1), start at 0.308.

> [!pitfall] Choosing the rule after seeing the p-values
> Benjamini–Hochberg rejects at least as many hypotheses as Holm, and Holm at least as many as Bonferroni. Picking the rule that delivers the hoped-for result is one more search: fix the family, the error and the level before the data. Benjamini–Hochberg's guarantee also needs independent statistics. The calendar's 17 are dependent, though the shuffle shows that the FWER barely moves (0.580 against 0.582).

> [!zh] 偽發現率：控制錯誤的比例
> 族系誤差率（family-wise error rate）要求一個都不能錯，檢定（test）一多就太嚴，真正的效果也會被漏掉。偽發現率（false discovery rate）改為控制錯誤拒絕（false rejection）在所有拒絕中所占的比例：拒絕 20 個、錯 1 個，比例是 5%。Benjamini–Hochberg 的方法把排序後第 $i$ 小的 p值（p-value）和 $iq/m$ 比較，在各檢定彼此獨立（independent）時，偽發現率不超過 $q$。Holm 的方法從最小的 p值往上逐一比較，遇到第一個不通過就停；不論檢定是否獨立，都能控制族系誤差率。月份與星期的 17 個檢定，三種方法都一個也不拒絕。

## Searching Many Strategies: the Hurdle of 3 {lo=7}

A strategy search is a family too, usually a large one whose members go unreported. Model it as $m$ tests whose null statistics are independent and standard normal. The largest of them exceeds a level when any one does, and (16.1) multiplies the probabilities that none does.

> [!result] The largest of $m$ null statistics
> For independent standard normal $Z_1,\dots,Z_m$ and every $x$,
> $$\Pr\big(\max_iZ_i\le x\big)=\Phi(x)^m. \tag{21.9}$$
> The largest has median $\Phi^{-1}(0.5^{1/m})$ and $u$ quantile $\Phi^{-1}(u^{1/m})$.

The median of the best grows with $m$: 1.75 at $m=17$, 2.46 at 100, 3.20 at 1,000 and 3.81 at 10,000. At $m=1{,}000$ its middle 90% runs from 2.75 to 3.88, and it exceeds 3 with probability 0.74 ([[ex:maxt]]).

> [!example] Ten thousand random rules on TAIEX {#search}
> Experiment 10 draws every position in one call on `default_rng([10, 20261011])`, `integers(0, 2, size=(10000, T), dtype=np.int8)`, with 1 for long and 0 for short. Rule $i$ of the 10,000 is long or short one unit each day by this fair coin, $f_{i,t}=\pm1$. Its daily return is $f_{i,t}R_t$, and (21.1) gives its t-stat with $\mu_{\text{null}}=0$. How large is the best t-stat?
>
> > [!solution]
> > The 10,000 t-stats average −0.01 (standard error 0.01), with a standard deviation of 0.995 (batch standard error 0.007, from 20 batches of 500, [[LM18]]). They behave like standard normal draws. 13 reach 3, against $10{,}000\,\Phi(-3)=13.5$ expected.
> >
> > The best after 100 rules is 2.95 (rule 90), and it is still 2.95 after 1,000. Rule 1,022 reaches 3.28, and the best of all 10,000 is 3.97 (rule 7,840). This is one search, a single draw with no standard error. Under (21.9) the best of 1,000 has median 3.20 and exceeds 3 with probability 0.74. Model on TR23's returns.

Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016) argue that a new factor needs "a t-statistic greater than 3.0". The two-sided p-value at $\lvert\text{t-stat}\rvert=3$ is $2\Phi(-3)=0.0027$, Bonferroni's level for about 18.5 tests at 5%. So a hurdle of 3 corrects for a search of about 18.5 tests, while the median best of 1,000 null rules already passes it. TR23's mean clears both bars: its t-stat, 3.96, exceeds 3 and Bonferroni's 3.134 for this module's 29 tests.

## Tests in Python {lo=8}

NumPy computes each statistic, and SciPy supplies the tails.

```python
import numpy as np
import polars as pl
from scipy import stats
from statsmodels.stats.multitest import multipletests

tr = pl.read_csv("data/taiex_tr.csv")["price"].to_numpy()
R = tr[1:] / tr[:-1] - 1                     # T = 5,849 simple returns
T = len(R)
tstat = R.mean() / (R.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(T))   # (21.1): 3.96
print(2 * stats.norm.sf(abs(tstat)))         # 7.6e-05, normal
print(2 * stats.t.sf(abs(tstat), T - 1))     # 7.7e-05, t with T - 1
print(stats.binom.sf(3205 - 1, T, 0.5))      # 1.2e-13, up days
print(stats.chi2.sf(2.69, 1))                # 0.10, signs, (21.5)
p = np.array([0.002, 0.0054, 0.0061, 0.011, 0.024,
              0.031, 0.09, 0.22, 0.47, 0.86])
for method in ("bonferroni", "holm", "fdr_bh"):  # 1, 3, 5
    print(method, multipletests(p, alpha=0.05, method=method)[0].sum())
print((stats.false_discovery_control(p) <= 0.05).sum())  # 5, BH
```

Check each function's defaults. `multipletests` defaults to Holm–Šidák (`method="hs"`), not Holm, so always pass `method`. Holm–Šidák, for independent tests, runs Holm's steps at Šidák's levels $1-(1-\alpha)^{1/(m-i+1)}$ in place of $\alpha/(m-i+1)$. SciPy's `ttest_ind` pools the two variances unless given `equal_var=False`. Its `chi2_contingency` applies Yates' correction to a 2×2 table unless given `correction=False`. `jarque_bera` uses moments with divisor $T$. NumPy's `std` divides by $T$ and Polars' by $T-1$ unless given `ddof`.

## Glossary {-}

| Term | 中文 | Meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Hypothesis test | 假設檢定 | A rule that rejects or keeps a null hypothesis from data, at a fixed level. |
| Null hypothesis | 虛無假設 | $H_0$, the parameter value under test. |
| Alternative hypothesis | 對立假設 | $H_1$, where the parameter lies if $H_0$ is false; one- or two-sided. |
| Significance level | 顯著水準 | $\alpha$, the largest allowed probability of a type I error. |
| p-value | p值 | The probability under $H_0$ of a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed, (21.2). |
| Type I error | 第一型錯誤 | Rejecting a true null hypothesis. |
| Type II error | 第二型錯誤 | Keeping a false null hypothesis. |
| Power | 檢定力 | The probability of rejecting $H_0$ under a given alternative, (21.4). |
| Multiple testing | 多重假設檢定 | Running many tests on the same data. |
| Family-wise error rate | 族系誤差率 | The probability of at least one false rejection in a family, (21.6). |
| Bonferroni correction | Bonferroni 校正 | Running each of $m$ tests at level $\alpha/m$, (21.6). |
| False discovery rate | 偽發現率 | The expected share of false rejections among all rejections, zero if none, (21.8). |
| Data snooping | 資料偵伺 | Searching many models or rules on one data set and reporting the best, (21.9). |
