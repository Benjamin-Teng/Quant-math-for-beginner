## Introduction

Every return in finance, from a deposit rate to a fund's lifetime performance, describes one quantity: the **growth factor**, ending value divided by starting value. Growth compounds, so it multiplies, and the mathematics of repeated multiplication is the exponential function and its inverse, the logarithm. This module builds both from first principles, derives the number $e$ from compound interest, and turns them into conversions that practitioners perform every day. The same tools recur in every later module, from the Taylor approximations of [[LM3]] to the lognormal prices of [[LM29]].

> [!case] One investment, three numbers
> Suppose you bought Yuanta Taiwan 50 (0050) at the close of 31 October 2014 and reinvested every cash dividend until 8 October 2026. NT\$100,000 became NT\$1,002,247.
>
> Three reports describe the holding. A fact sheet says the fund returned **+902%**. A performance review says it earned **21.3% a year**. A backtest log says it grew at **19.3% a year**.
>
> All three are correct, and they describe the same money. Yet 21.3% and 19.3% cannot both be "the" annual return, and neither equals 902% divided by twelve years. Which number belongs in a client report, which belongs in a model, and how does each convert into the others? Section 4 resolves the case; Sections 2 and 3 build the tools.

## Exponents and Logarithms {lo=1}

### Growth multiplies

Wealth grows by factors. Returns $R_1$ in the first period and $R_2$ in the second turn $W_0$ into $W_0(1+R_1)(1+R_2)$. With the same return $R$ in each of $T$ periods,

$$W_T=W_0\,(1+R)^{T}. \tag{1.1}$$

The count $T$ sits in the exponent, so wealth is an **exponential function** of time. Doubling the number of periods squares the growth factor instead of doubling it, which is why long-horizon numbers defy intuition.

### Laws of exponents

For a base $\beta>0$ and real exponents $x$ and $z$,

$$\beta^x\beta^z=\beta^{x+z},\qquad \frac{\beta^x}{\beta^z}=\beta^{x-z},\qquad (\beta^x)^z=\beta^{xz},\qquad \beta^0=1,\qquad \beta^{-x}=\frac{1}{\beta^x}.$$

For whole-number exponents these are counting rules: $\beta^2\beta^3$ contains $2+3$ factors of $\beta$. Defining $\beta^{1/m}$ as the positive $m$-th root, and fractional powers as powers of such roots, extends them to rational exponents. Continuity extends them to all real exponents (stated without proof; see Stewart, Clegg and Watson, and [[LM2]] for continuity). [[ex:laws]] gives each law a financial reading.

### Logarithms

> [!definition] Logarithm
> For a base $\beta>0$ with $\beta\ne1$ and a number $x>0$, the **logarithm** $\log_\beta x$ is the exponent to which $\beta$ must be raised to give $x$:
> $$\log_\beta x=z\iff \beta^z=x.$$
> The **natural logarithm** $\ln x:=\log_ex$ uses the base $e=2.71828\ldots$, a constant whose origin in compound interest is the subject of Section 3.

Because $\log_\beta$ undoes $\beta^x$, every law of exponents becomes a law of logarithms. For $x,z>0$, write $x=\beta^{\log_\beta x}$ and $z=\beta^{\log_\beta z}$. The product law gives $xz=\beta^{\log_\beta x+\log_\beta z}$, which by the definition means

$$\log_\beta(xz)=\log_\beta x+\log_\beta z. \tag{1.2}$$

The same steps give $\log_\beta(x/z)=\log_\beta x-\log_\beta z$ and $\log_\beta1=0$. From $x^\alpha=\big(\beta^{\log_\beta x}\big)^\alpha=\beta^{\alpha\log_\beta x}$ follows the power rule $\log_\beta(x^\alpha)=\alpha\log_\beta x$ for every real $\alpha$. A logarithm turns multiplication into addition and powers into multiples. That is why log returns add over time (Section 5) and why growth equations are solved with logs (Section 6).

> [!exhibit] Laws of exponents and logarithms, read as finance {#laws}
> | Rule | Exponent form<br>Logarithm form | Financial reading |
> |:--|:--|:--|
> | Product | ${\beta^x\beta^z=\beta^{x+z}}$<br>${\log(xz)=\log x+\log z}$ | Growth over consecutive periods multiplies; log growth adds |
> | Quotient | ${\beta^x/\beta^z=\beta^{x-z}}$<br>${\log(x/z)=\log x-\log z}$ | The log return between two dates is a difference of log prices |
> | Power | ${(\beta^x)^z=\beta^{xz}}$<br>${\log x^\alpha=\alpha\log x}$ | $T$ periods at a constant rate: $\ln\big[(1+R)^T\big]=T\ln(1+R)$ |
> | Reciprocal | ${\beta^{-x}=1/\beta^x}$<br>${\log(1/x)=-\log x}$ | Discounting, $1.05^{-1}=0.9524$; a loss and the gain that undoes it have opposite logs |
> | Root | ${\beta^{1/m}=\sqrt[m]{\beta}}$<br>${\log x^{1/m}=\tfrac1m\log x}$ | De-annualizing: $1.01725^{1/12}-1=0.1426\%$ a month |
> | Change of base | ${\beta^x=e^{x\ln\beta}}$<br>${\log_\beta x=\ln x/\ln\beta}$ | Doublings in a growth factor: $\log_2G=\ln G/\ln2$ |

### Change of base

Any logarithm is the natural logarithm rescaled. Since $x=\beta^{\log_\beta x}$, taking natural logs of both sides and applying the power rule gives $\ln x=\log_\beta x\cdot\ln\beta$, so

$$\log_\beta x=\frac{\ln x}{\ln\beta}. \tag{1.3}$$

The base is a unit of measurement, like kilometers and miles. For 0050, $\ln G=\ln10.0225=2.3048$. In base 2 that is $2.3048/\ln2=3.325$ doublings; in base 10 it is $2.3048/\ln10=1.001$ tenfold increases. The fund grew almost exactly tenfold, which is why its log growth is close to $\ln10=2.3026$.

> [!example] Splitting 0050's growth into price and dividends {#split}
> 0050 closed at NT\$65.30 on 31 October 2014 and at NT\$114.95 on 8 October 2026, after a 4-for-1 split on 18 June 2025. It paid 22 cash dividends in between, and its total-return growth factor was $G=10.0225$. Split the log growth into a price part and a dividend part, and express each in doublings.
>
> > [!solution]
> > One 2014 share became four 2026 shares, so the price-only growth factor is $G_P=4\times114.95/65.30=459.80/65.30=7.0413$, a price return of +604.13%.
> >
> > For each daily return, restate the previous close per current share: $\tilde P_{t-1}=P_{t-1}/4$ on the split day and $\tilde P_{t-1}=P_{t-1}$ on every other day. The total return then factors exactly as
> > $$1+R_t=\frac{P_t+D_t}{\tilde P_{t-1}}=\frac{P_t}{\tilde P_{t-1}}\Big(1+\frac{D_t}{P_t}\Big),$$
> > where $D_t$ is the cash dividend on an ex-date and zero otherwise.
> >
> > The price factors $P_t/\tilde P_{t-1}$ multiply to $G_P$; without the restatement they would multiply to $G_P/4=1.7603$. Applying the product rule (1.2) to the product over all days,
> > $$\ln G=\ln G_P+\sum_{\text{ex-dates}}\ln\Big(1+\frac{D_t}{P_t}\Big)=1.9518+0.3530=2.3048 .$$
> > Dividends supplied 15.3% of the log growth. In doublings, dividing each term by $\ln2$: price $2.816$ plus dividends $0.509$ equals $3.325$.
> >
> > In simple returns the decomposition is multiplicative: $G=G_P\,e^{0.3530}$, so reinvested dividends multiplied ending wealth by $e^{0.3530}=1.423$. The price return of +604.13% and the dividend effect of +42.3% compound to +902.25%; they do not add. Logs make the split exact and additive.

> [!pitfall] Log of what, in which base
> Take logs of growth factors or prices, never of returns: $\ln(1+R)$ exists for every $R>-100\%$, while $\ln R$ fails for every loss. Check the base too: in Excel, `LOG(x)` is base 10 unless a base is given and `LN(x)` is natural. In Python, `math.log` and `np.log` are natural and `np.log10` is base 10. Mixing them scales every result by $\ln10=2.3026$, an error that changes no signs and is easy to miss.

### Log-scale charts

A logarithmic axis places each value $W$ at a height that rises in step with $\ln W$. Equal changes in $\ln W$ are equal distances on the page, with two consequences.

By the quotient rule, $\ln W_2-\ln W_1=\ln(W_2/W_1)$, so equal vertical distances are equal ratios. The step from 1 to 2 is as tall as the step from 4 to 8. Constant growth $W_t=W_0e^{gt}$, with $t$ in years, plots as the straight line $\ln W_t=\ln W_0+gt$, by the product rule and $\ln e^{gt}=gt$. Its slope is the log growth rate $g$.

[[ex:loglin]] plots 0050's total-return index on both axes, with gridlines at successive doublings and the constant-rate path that ends at the same value.

> <div data-chart="lm01-linear"></div>
> <div data-chart="lm01-log"></div>
>
> Note: growth of 1 unit with dividends reinvested; gridlines at 1, 2, 4 and 8. The dashed path grows at a constant 21.3% a year and ends at the same value, 10.02. Markers show the first close at or above each doubling. Source: FinMind, retrieved 10 October 2026; `data/lm01_charts.py`.

On the linear axis the first six years look flat and the end looks explosive. Even the constant-rate path, which by construction grows at the same rate every year, appears to accelerate. On the log axis that path is a straight line, so only genuine changes in the growth rate bend the index away from it.

The doublings put numbers on the picture. The index first closed above 2 on 11 November 2020, above 4 on 4 July 2024 and above 8 on 27 April 2026. The three doublings took 6.0, 3.6 and 1.8 years.

Declines are distorted as well. The January–April 2025 decline cost 1.18 units of the index and the April–August 2015 decline 0.23 units, a ratio of five. In percentage terms they were −27.5% and −21.0%, or 0.32 and 0.24 log points.

> [!zh] 對數座標：看倍數，不看差額
> 對數把乘法變成加法，把「倍數」變成「距離」。在對數座標上，從 1 漲到 2 和從 4 漲到 8 的高度一樣，因為都是翻一倍；固定成長率的路徑是一條直線，斜率就是對數成長率 $g$。底數只是單位：$\ln$ 是自然單位，$\log_2$ 數「翻了幾番」（翻倍幾次），$\log_{10}$ 數「成長了幾個十倍」，彼此只差一個常數倍。0050 在 2025 年 1 至 4 月的回撤，在線性圖上看起來是 2015 年 4 至 8 月那次的五倍，換成對數其實只是 0.32 對 0.24。看長期走勢、比較不同年代的漲跌，請用對數座標。

## Compounding Frequency and the Number e {lo=2}

### Simple and compound interest

Under **simple interest**, interest accrues on the principal only, so wealth grows linearly: $W_\tau=W_0(1+i\tau)$ after $\tau$ years at an interest rate $i$ per year. Under **compound interest** credited yearly, interest joins the balance and earns interest itself, which gives the exponential growth of (1.1): $W_\tau=W_0(1+i)^\tau$. The two agree after exactly one year and diverge after that. Both appear in Taiwan retail finance, as the examples below show.

### Periodic, stated and effective rates

Banks quote rates per year but credit interest more often. A **stated annual rate** $r^{(n)}$ (名目年利率) credited $n$ times a year pays the **periodic rate** $i:=r^{(n)}/n$ each period; yearly crediting, $n=1$, gives the annual rate $i$ above. After one year a unit has grown to $(1+i)^n$.

> [!definition] Effective annual rate
> The **effective annual rate** (EAR, 有效年利率) of a stated rate $r^{(n)}$ compounded $n$ times a year is the annual simple return it delivers:
> $$R=\Big(1+\frac{r^{(n)}}{n}\Big)^{n}-1=(1+i)^n-1. \tag{1.4}$$
> Conversely, the stated rate with frequency $n$ that delivers a given EAR $R$ is $r^{(n)}=n\big[(1+R)^{1/n}-1\big]$.

The EAR is the common currency for comparing quotes: two rates are equivalent when their EARs match. [[ex:ear]] applies (1.4) to two Taiwan benchmarks: Bank of Taiwan's rate for one-year time savings deposits (定期儲蓄存款) and the legal cap on credit-card revolving interest.

> | Compounding | $n$ | Periodic rate $i$ at 15% | EAR at 1.725% | EAR at 15% |
> |:--|--:|--:|--:|--:|
> | Annual | 1 | 15.0000% | 1.7250% | 15.000% |
> | Semiannual | 2 | 7.5000% | 1.7324% | 15.563% |
> | Quarterly | 4 | 3.7500% | 1.7362% | 15.865% |
> | Monthly | 12 | 1.2500% | 1.7387% | 16.075% |
> | Weekly | 52 | 0.2885% | 1.7397% | 16.158% |

> | Continuous | $\infty$ | — | 1.7400% | 16.183% |

Two features stand out. The EAR rises with $n$ but by ever smaller steps, and it levels off at a finite limit. And frequency matters far more at high rates: moving from annual to continuous compounding adds 1.5 bp at 1.725% but 118 bp at 15%. The gap grows roughly with the square of the rate, a second-order effect that [[LM3]] quantifies.

> [!example] A Taiwan time deposit, two ways {#deposit}
> Bank of Taiwan posts a fixed rate of 1.725% a year for one-year time savings deposits (定期儲蓄存款). Typical Taiwan bank terms (DBS Bank Taiwan's, for instance) offer two forms. Under 整存整付 interest is compounded monthly and paid at maturity; under 存本取息 it accrues daily and is paid out monthly, counting days on the actual/365 basis that NTD deposits use. Compare the interest on NT\$1,000,000 over one year.
>
> > [!solution]
> > 整存整付 compounds the periodic rate $i=0.01725/12=0.14375\%$ twelve times: $R=1.0014375^{12}-1=1.7387\%$, or NT\$17,387 of interest at maturity.
> >
> > 存本取息 pays $1{,}000{,}000\times1.725\%\times d/365$ for a month of $d$ days: NT\$1,465.07 for 31 days, NT\$1,417.81 for 30 days and NT\$1,323.29 for 28 days. Over a 365-day year the payments total NT\$17,250, an effective 1.7250% unless the saver reinvests each payment.
> >
> > The same posted rate yields NT\$137 more under monthly compounding, 1.37 bp of principal. At deposit rates near 2% the compounding frequency is almost a rounding error. The next example shows where it is not.

> [!example] The credit-card cap and the ban on compounding {#card}
> Since 1 September 2015, Article 47-1 of Taiwan's Banking Act has capped credit-card revolving interest at 15% a year. Issuers accrue it as balance × rate × days/365, their own day count. Article 48 of the Regulations Governing Institutions Engaging in Credit Card Business forbids compounding it.
>
> Suppose a revolving balance of NT\$100,000 runs for a year at the cap, with no repayments and no new purchases. Compute the interest, and compare it with daily compounding at the same stated rate.
>
> > [!solution]
> > Without compounding, interest accrues on the principal only: $100{,}000\times15\%\times365/365=\text{NT\$}15{,}000$, an EAR of exactly 15.000%.
> >
> > With daily compounding the periodic rate is $i=0.15/365=0.041096\%$ a day (about 萬分之 4.11), and (1.4) gives $R=1.00041096^{365}-1=16.180\%$, or NT\$16,180. Continuous compounding would give $e^{0.15}-1=16.183\%$.
> >
> > The no-compounding rule is worth NT\$1,180 a year on this balance. It also makes the legal 15% an honest effective rate: a lender allowed to compound daily could quote 13.979% and still collect a 15% EAR.

### From discrete to continuous: the number $e$

Push the frequency to its limit. In 1683 Jacob Bernoulli asked what happens to one unit at 100% interest a year when interest is credited ever more often (O'Connor and Robertson). With $n$ credits the year-end value is $(1+1/n)^n$, and [[ex:bernoulli]] shows it rising but leveling off.

> [!exhibit] Bernoulli's sequence $(1+1/n)^n$ {#bernoulli}
> | Crediting | $n$ | $(1+1/n)^n$ | Distance to $e$ |
> |:--|--:|--:|--:|
> | Annual | 1 | 2.000000 | 0.718282 |
> | Semiannual | 2 | 2.250000 | 0.468282 |
> | Quarterly | 4 | 2.441406 | 0.276876 |
> | Monthly | 12 | 2.613035 | 0.105247 |
> | Weekly | 52 | 2.692597 | 0.025685 |
> | Daily | 365 | 2.714567 | 0.003714 |
> | Hourly | 8,760 | 2.718127 | 0.000155 |
> | Every minute | 525,600 | 2.718279 | 0.000003 |
>
> Note: $e=2.718281828\ldots$; the distance to $e$ shrinks roughly in proportion to $1/n$.

A **limit** is the value a sequence approaches, as closely as we like, once $n$ is large enough. [[LM2]] makes limits and continuity precise; this section uses them informally.

> [!definition] The number $e$
> $$e:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Big(1+\frac1n\Big)^{n}=2.718281828\ldots \tag{1.5}$$
> The sequence increases with $n$ and never exceeds 3, so the limit exists; the Deep Dive below proves both facts.

The general limit follows from (1.5) by a change of variable.

> [!proposition] Continuous compounding
> For every real number $x$,
> $$\lim_{n\to\infty}\Big(1+\frac{x}{n}\Big)^{n}=e^{x}. \tag{1.6}$$

*Proof.* For $x=0$ both sides equal 1. For $x>0$, put $m=n/x$, so that $x/n=1/m$ and $n=mx$:
$$\Big(1+\frac{x}{n}\Big)^{n}=\Big[\Big(1+\frac1m\Big)^{m}\Big]^{x}.$$
As $n\to\infty$, $m\to\infty$ through real values and the bracket tends to $e$; the Deep Dive shows that non-integer $m$ are allowed. Because raising to a fixed power $x$ is a continuous operation ([[LM2]]), the whole expression tends to $e^x$.

For $x=-z<0$, note that $1-z/n=1/\big(1+\tfrac{z}{n-z}\big)$ for $n>z$, so that
$$\Big(1-\frac{z}{n}\Big)^{n}=\frac{1}{\big(1+\frac{z}{n-z}\big)^{n-z}\big(1+\frac{z}{n-z}\big)^{z}}\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{1}{e^{z}\cdot1}=e^{-z},$$
by the case already proved, applied with $n-z$ in place of $n$. $\blacksquare$

Applied to money, (1.6) says that a stated rate $r$ compounded continuously turns $W_0$ into

$$W_\tau=W_0\,e^{r\tau} \tag{1.7}$$

after $\tau$ years, so its effective annual rate, and the inverse conversion, are

$$R=e^{r}-1,\qquad r=\ln(1+R). \tag{1.8}$$

The continuously compounded rate equivalent to an EAR $R$ is the natural logarithm of one plus the EAR, $r=\ln(1+R)$, not the logarithm of $R$ itself. Every stated rate with the same EAR lies between $R$ and $r$: $r^{(n)}$ falls from $r^{(1)}=R$ toward $r$ as $n$ grows. More frequent crediting needs a lower quote to deliver the same EAR.

> [!deepdive]- Why $(1+1/n)^n$ converges, and why $e=\sum_{j\ge0}1/j!$
> **Increasing.** Apply the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality (stated without proof; Section 5 gives its weighted form, which [[LM14]] derives) to the $n+1$ positive numbers $1,\,1+\tfrac1n,\dots,1+\tfrac1n$. Their arithmetic mean is $\frac{1+n(1+1/n)}{n+1}=1+\frac1{n+1}$ and their geometric mean is $\big(1+\tfrac1n\big)^{n/(n+1)}$. The numbers are not all equal, so the geometric mean is strictly smaller:
> $$\Big(1+\frac1n\Big)^{n/(n+1)}<1+\frac1{n+1}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Big(1+\frac1n\Big)^{n}<\Big(1+\frac1{n+1}\Big)^{n+1}.$$
> **Bounded.** By the binomial theorem,
> $$\Big(1+\frac1n\Big)^{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom nj\frac1{n^j}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac1{j!}\cdot\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-j+1)}{n^j}\le\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac1{j!}\le1+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac1{2^{j-1}}<3,$$
> because each factor $(n-l)/n\le1$ and $j!\ge2^{j-1}$. An increasing sequence that is bounded above converges (the monotone convergence theorem, stated without proof; see Stewart, Clegg and Watson), so $e$ exists and $e\le3$.
>
> **The series.** The display also shows $(1+1/n)^n\le\sum_{j=0}^{n}1/j!$. Conversely, fix $K$ and keep only the first $K+1$ terms: for $n>K$, $(1+1/n)^n\ge\sum_{j=0}^{K}\frac1{j!}\prod_{l<j}\big(1-\frac ln\big)$, which tends to $\sum_{j=0}^{K}1/j!$ as $n\to\infty$. Hence $e=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}1/j!$. Its partial sums 2, 2.5, 2.666667, 2.708333, 2.716667 and 2.718056 converge far faster than Bernoulli's sequence.
>
> **Real $m$.** For an integer $j$ with $j\le m<j+1$, $\big(1+\tfrac1{j+1}\big)^{j}\le\big(1+\tfrac1m\big)^{m}\le\big(1+\tfrac1j\big)^{j+1}$, and both bounds tend to $e$ as $j\to\infty$. So (1.5) also holds as $m\to\infty$ through real numbers. $\blacksquare$

> [!pitfall] Comparing stated rates across frequencies
> A stated rate means little until its compounding frequency is known. A deposit at 1.74% paid annually beats one at 1.725% compounded monthly, but only by 0.13 bp: 1.7400% against 1.7387%. Convert every quote to an EAR, or to a continuously compounded rate, before comparing. The same holds for any annual figure built from shorter periods, including the return figures of Section 4.

> [!zh] e 從哪裡來
> $e$ 不是憑空出現的常數：本金 1 元、年利率 100%、每年複利無限多次，一年後的本利和就是 $e=2.71828\ldots$。複利次數越多，有效年利率越高，但增加的幅度越來越小，最後收斂到 $e^r-1$；所以「連續複利利率」$r$ 和「有效年利率」$R$ 只是一組換算：$R=e^r-1$、$r=\ln(1+R)$。利率越高，複利頻率越重要：臺銀一年期定儲 1.725% 按月複利只多 1.4 bp；信用卡循環利率上限 15% 若按日複利，會多出約 118 bp，這正是法規禁止循環利息「利滾利」的意義。

> [!kc]
> A bank pays 5.85% compounded monthly; a competitor pays 6.00% compounded annually. (a) Which pays the higher EAR? (b) Give each rate's continuously compounded equivalent.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > (a) $(1+0.0585/12)^{12}-1=6.0094\%$ against $6.0000\%$: the lower stated rate wins because it compounds monthly. (b) $12\ln(1+0.0585/12)=5.8358\%$ and $\ln1.06=5.8269\%$. The ranking is the same on every consistent scale.

## Holding-Period, Annualized and Continuously Compounded Returns {lo=3,4}

### Holding-period return

> [!definition] Holding-period return
> The **holding-period return** (HPR) from time 0 to time $T$ is the gain per unit invested, income included:
> $$R_{0,T}=\frac{P_T-P_0+D}{P_0},$$
> where $D$ is the cash dividend received. Over $T$ periods with income reinvested, period returns chain by multiplication: $1+R_{0,T}=\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1+R_t)=G$.

The HPR carries no time unit: +902.25% describes twelve years, while +10% might describe a week. A **price return** omits income; a **total return** includes it. For 0050 over the case period the price return was +604.13% and the total return +902.25% ([[eg:split]]). Fund comparisons should use total returns, because funds differ in how much they distribute.

### Annualized return

To compare holdings of different lengths, express each as a constant annual rate. Measure the horizon in years as $\tau=$ calendar days ÷ 365.25, which averages the leap-year cycle. The **annualized return** $R_G$ is the return which, compounded once a year for $\tau$ years, produces the same growth factor:

$$(1+R_G)^{\tau}=G\quad\Longrightarrow\quad R_G=G^{1/\tau}-1. \tag{1.9}$$

The root law of [[ex:laws]] does the work. For $\tau>1$ the exponent $1/\tau$ pulls the growth factor toward 1, shrinking gains and losses alike. For $\tau<1$ it pushes the factor away from 1 and compounds a short-period return forward, which is extrapolation rather than measurement. [[LM3]] calls $R_G$ the geometric mean return and compares it with the arithmetic mean.

### Continuously compounded return

The **log return** over the holding period is $r_{0,T}=\ln(1+R_{0,T})=\ln G$. Divided by the horizon, it gives the **continuously compounded return per year**, also called the log growth rate:

$$g=\frac{\ln G}{\tau}=\ln(1+R_G). \tag{1.10}$$

The second equality is the power rule, $\ln G/\tau=\ln\big(G^{1/\tau}\big)=\ln(1+R_G)$. Comparing (1.10) with (1.8) shows what $g$ is: the continuously compounded rate equivalent to the effective annual rate $R_G$. The annualized return and the continuously compounded return are one growth rate quoted under two compounding conventions. [[ex:conv]] collects the four expressions of a growth factor with their values for 0050.

> [!exhibit] One growth factor, four expressions {#conv}
> | Quantity | Definition | Back to $G$ | 0050, 2014–2026 |
> |:--|:--|:--|--:|
> | Holding-period return | $R_{0,T}=G-1$ | $G=1+R_{0,T}$ | +902.25% |
> | Annualized return | $R_G=G^{1/\tau}-1$ | $G=(1+R_G)^{\tau}$ | 21.30% |
> | Log return over the period | $r_{0,T}=\ln G$ | $G=e^{r_{0,T}}$ | 2.3048 |
> | Continuously compounded return per year | $g=\ln G/\tau=\ln(1+R_G)$ | $G=e^{g\tau}$ | 19.31% |
>
> Note: $\tau=4{,}360/365.25=11.94$ years. Between the two annual measures, $R_G=e^{g}-1$ and $g=\ln(1+R_G)$, exactly as between an EAR and its continuous rate in (1.8).

> [!example] One investment, three numbers, resolved {#three}
> Use [[ex:case]] to verify that +902.25%, 21.30% a year and 19.31% a year describe the same holding. Then decide which figure to quote to a client and which to use in a model.
>
> > [!solution]
> > All three come from $G=10.0225$ and $\tau=4{,}360/365.25=11.9370$ years. By (1.9), $R_G=10.0225^{1/11.9370}-1=21.298\%$. By (1.10), $g=\ln10.0225/11.9370=2.30483/11.9370=19.308\%$. They are linked by (1.8): $e^{0.19308}-1=21.298\%$ and $\ln1.21298=0.19308$.
> >
> > With the inverse of (1.4), the same growth can be quoted at any frequency: 20.27% compounded semiannually, 19.46% compounded monthly, 19.31% compounded daily or continuously. The gap between 21.30% and 19.31% is a quoting convention, not a disagreement about performance.
> >
> > For a client, quote the annualized return. Like a deposit's EAR it compounds once a year, so it compares directly with deposit rates and with other funds' annualized figures.
> >
> > For modeling, use $g$. It adds over time (Section 5) and scales linearly with the horizon, $\ln G=g\tau$. It is the growth rate of log wealth: $\mu-\sigma^2/2$ under geometric Brownian motion ([[LM29]]), and the quantity Kelly betting maximizes ([[LM42]]). Quote the cumulative +902% only together with its horizon.

> [!pitfall] Dividing a cumulative return by the number of years
> $902.25\%/11.94=75.6\%$ "a year" is the simple-interest rate that produces 902.25%: $1+0.7558\times11.94=10.02$. It ignores compounding and overstates 0050's annualized return by a factor of 3.5. Divide log returns by time, never simple returns.

### Annualizing across horizons and day-count conventions

A return over part of a year annualizes the same way; only the measure of $\tau$ changes. Over $d$ calendar days, $\tau=d/365.25$; over $T$ trading sessions, $\tau=T/A$, where $A$ is the number of sessions per year. Then

$$R_G=(1+R)^{1/\tau}-1,\qquad g=\frac{\ln(1+R)}{\tau}. \tag{1.11}$$

The log version is linear in $1/\tau$ because logs add over time. Scaling a simple return linearly, $R/\tau$, gives a stated rate compounded $1/\tau$ times a year, not an EAR. Instruments with their own day count keep it: NTD deposits accrue on an actual/365 basis, as in [[eg:deposit]].

Many analysts and software packages set $A=252$, but Taiwan's market is less busy. Counting the days on which 0050 or 00631L traded, the Taiwan Stock Exchange held between 239 and 247 sessions a year in 2015–2025. The yearly counts were 244, 244, 246, 247, 242, 245, 244, 246, 239, 242 and 243, an average of 243.8. Within one sample, the right $A$ is the number of return observations per year in that sample, as the next two examples show.

> [!example] Annualizing a 90-day return {#ninety}
> Between the closes of 9 July and 7 October 2026, 90 calendar days and 61 sessions apart, 0050's price rose from NT\$105.80 to NT\$116.05. The fund also paid a NT\$0.60 dividend with ex-date 21 July. Its total return was 10.3302%. Annualize it five ways.
>
> > [!solution]
> > Calendar days, with $\tau=90/365.25$ in (1.11): $1.103302^{365.25/90}-1=49.03\%$. Trading days with $A=252$: $1.103302^{252/61}-1=50.10\%$; with Taiwan's 2015–2025 average $A=243.8$: $1.103302^{243.8/61}-1=48.13\%$. Continuously compounded: $\ln1.103302\times365.25/90=0.098308\times4.0583=39.90\%$ a year. Simple scaling: $10.3302\%\times365.25/90=41.92\%$.
> >
> > One 90-day return yields "annual" figures from 39.9% to 50.1%. Each is a correct conversion under its own convention, and each assumes that the same return recurs every 90 days for a year. Even the length of the year matters: with 365 days instead of 365.25 the calendar figure is 48.99%. Report the 10.33% for the period, and state the convention whenever you annualize.

> [!example] Annualizing a daily mean log return with $A=252$ and $A=243.1$ {#daily}
> The case series has $T=2{,}902$ daily returns; it is the series of [[LM3]], aligned with 00631L's trading days. Their mean log return was 0.079422%. Annualize it with $A=252$ and with the sample's own frequency, and compare the results with $g$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > With $A=252$: $0.079422\%\times252=20.01\%$. The sample's own annualization factor is $A=T/\tau=2{,}902/11.937=243.1$ returns a year, and $0.079422\%\times243.1=19.31\%$, which is exactly $g$. The reason is (1.10) and Section 5. The mean log return times $T$ is the total log growth $\ln G$, and dividing by $\tau$ gives $g$.
> >
> > The sample's $A$ is below the exchange's average of 243.8. Over the same span the exchange held 2,911 sessions, 243.9 a year. 0050 did not trade on five of them, in June 2025. The alignment with 00631L folds four March 2026 sessions, on which 00631L did not trade, into the following return, leaving 2,902.
> >
> > The 252-day convention overstates the growth rate by 0.7 percentage points a year. As an EAR it gives $e^{0.20014}-1=22.16\%$ against the true 21.30%. Nothing about the data changed, only the annualization factor.

## Adding Returns Across Time and Across Assets {lo=5}

### Across time, log returns add

Over $T$ consecutive periods growth factors multiply, $G=\prod_t(1+R_t)$. The product rule (1.2) turns the product into a sum:

$$\ln G=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ln(1+R_t)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_t. \tag{1.12}$$

The identity is exact for any path. Simple returns do not add over time, and their sum has no meaning. [[ex:blocks]] splits 0050's twelve years into six two-year blocks.

> [!exhibit] 0050 in two-year blocks: simple returns compound, log returns add {#blocks}
> | Block (close to close) | Days | Simple return $R$ | Log return $r$ |
> |:--|--:|--:|--:|
> | 2014-10-31 → 2016-10-31 | 731 | 16.03% | 0.1487 |
> | 2016-10-31 → 2018-10-31 | 730 | 14.06% | 0.1316 |
> | 2018-10-31 → 2020-10-30 | 730 | 43.56% | 0.3616 |
> | 2020-10-30 → 2022-10-31 | 731 | 2.35% | 0.0232 |
> | 2022-10-31 → 2024-10-30 | 730 | 106.47% | 0.7250 |
> | 2024-10-30 → 2026-10-08 | 708 | 149.61% | 0.9147 |
> | **Whole period** | **4,360** | **902.25%** (compounded) | **2.3048** (summed) |
>
> Note: blocks end at the last close on or before 31 October. The whole-period simple return is $\prod(1+R)-1$; adding the six simple returns gives 332.08%, which describes nothing. The six log returns sum to $\ln G=2.3048$.

The table also shows how simple returns distort comparisons. The last block's 149.61% looks more than ten times the second block's 14.06%; in log points it is seven times, 0.9147 against 0.1316. The final two blocks supplied 71% of the twelve-year log growth, the steep end of the log chart in [[ex:loglin]]. Averaging the six simple returns would also overstate the typical compound return; [[LM3]] derives that gap, volatility drag, and [[LM14]] its general form, Jensen's inequality.

### Across assets, simple returns add

A portfolio is a sum of money, not a product. Let $J$ positions start with values $V_{j,0}$, total value $V_0>0$ and weights $w_j=V_{j,0}/V_0$, which sum to 1; a short position has $V_{j,0}<0$ and $w_j<0$. If position $j$ returns $R_j$ over the period, the portfolio ends at $\sum_jV_{j,0}(1+R_j)$, so

$$R_p=\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_jR_j \tag{1.13}$$

exactly, with weights measured at the start of the period, whatever their signs. Log returns do not aggregate this way. The portfolio's log return is $r_p=\ln\big(\sum_jw_je^{r_j}\big)$.

Consider a long-only portfolio, with every $w_j\ge0$ and $\sum_jw_j=1$. For positive $x_j$, the weighted arithmetic–geometric mean inequality states that $\sum_jw_jx_j\ge\prod_jx_j^{w_j}$, with equality only when all $x_j$ with $w_j>0$ are equal. It is stated here without proof; [[LM14]] derives it from Jensen's inequality. Applied with $x_j=e^{r_j}$ and followed by logs, it gives

$$r_p=\ln\Big(\sum_jw_je^{r_j}\Big)\ \ge\ \sum_jw_jr_j\qquad\big(w_j\ge0,\ \textstyle\sum_jw_j=1\big), \tag{1.14}$$

with equality only when every position with $w_j>0$ has the same log return. Averaging log returns across a long-only portfolio therefore understates its return, and the error grows with the dispersion of the positions' returns. With short positions the inequality can fail: weights $(1.5,-0.5)$ and log returns $(0.10,0.20)$ give $r_p=\ln\big(1.5e^{0.10}-0.5e^{0.20}\big)=0.0460$, below $\sum_jw_jr_j=0.0500$.

> [!example] Averaging log returns across two funds {#avglog}
> On 31 October 2014 an investor puts half her money in 0050 and half in 00631L, the daily 2× fund on the same index. She holds both until 8 October 2026, a long-only buy-and-hold portfolio. 0050's growth factor was 10.02 and 00631L's 44.54, log growths of 2.3048 and 3.7965. Compute the portfolio's return correctly and by averaging log returns.
>
> > [!solution]
> > By (1.13) the portfolio's growth factor is $0.5\times10.02+0.5\times44.54=27.28$, a return of +2,628%.
> >
> > Averaging log returns gives $0.5\times2.3048+0.5\times3.7965=3.0507$, which implies $e^{3.0507}=21.13$, or +2,013%. That understates the portfolio by 615 percentage points. The averaged figure is the log of $\sqrt{10.02\times44.54}=21.13$, the geometric mean of the two growth factors, which no buy-and-hold investor earned.
> >
> > The correct portfolio log return is $\ln27.28=3.306$, larger than the average 3.0507, as (1.14) requires for long-only weights.

### Across multiplicative factors: currency and inflation

Some returns combine by multiplication rather than by weighting. A Taiwan investor in a US-dollar asset earns the asset's dollar return and the change in the TWD price of a dollar:

$$1+R^{\text{TWD}}=(1+R^{\text{USD}})(1+R^{\text{FX}})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad r^{\text{TWD}}=r^{\text{USD}}+r^{\text{FX}} \tag{1.15}$$

exactly in logs. In simple returns, $R^{\text{TWD}}=R^{\text{USD}}+R^{\text{FX}}+R^{\text{USD}}R^{\text{FX}}$ carries a cross term. Inflation works the same way: a real return satisfies $1+R^{\text{real}}=(1+R)/(1+\pi)$, so $r^{\text{real}}=r-\ln(1+\pi)$ exactly. The familiar shortcut $R^{\text{real}}\approx R-\pi$ drops a second-order term, which [[LM3]] measures.

> [!example] A US-dollar asset seen from Taiwan {#fx}
> A US equity fund gains 20% in US dollars over a year in which USD/TWD falls from 32.0 to 29.6 (hypothetical figures). Compute the TWD return exactly, by adding log returns, and by adding simple returns.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The dollar lost $29.6/32.0-1=-7.50\%$ against the TWD. Exactly, $1.20\times0.925-1=11.00\%$. In logs, $\ln1.20+\ln0.925=0.18232-0.07796=0.10436$, and $e^{0.10436}-1=11.00\%$.
> >
> > Adding simple returns gives $20\%-7.5\%=12.5\%$, which overstates by the cross term $0.20\times(-0.075)=-1.50\%$. The currency loss applies to the whole dollar value, gains included.

> [!result] Aggregation rules
> - **Across time:** log returns add, (1.12); simple returns compound.
> - **Across positions** over one period: simple returns add with start-of-period weights of any sign, (1.13). Log returns do not; for long-only weights their average understates the portfolio, (1.14).
> - **Across multiplicative factors** such as currency and inflation: log returns add exactly, (1.15); simple returns need a cross term.

> [!pitfall] Averaging log returns across positions
> Index and portfolio returns are weighted averages of simple returns with start-of-period weights. In a long-only sector or factor portfolio, the members' average log return is the log of a weighted geometric mean of their growth factors. By (1.14) it lies below what an investor holding the members earned. Aggregate simple returns across names first; take logs of the portfolio afterwards if a log-return series is needed.

> [!zh] 時間用對數加，資產用簡單報酬加
> 對數報酬「時間可加」：各期的對數報酬加總就是整段期間的對數成長，0050 六個兩年區間的對數報酬相加正好是 2.3048；簡單報酬只能連乘，硬加起來的 332% 沒有意義。投資組合的錢卻是相加的，所以跨資產要用期初權重，對簡單報酬做加權平均。對權重都不為負的多頭組合，把各資產的對數報酬加權平均，等於算成長倍數的加權幾何平均，一定低估組合報酬：0050 與 00631L 各半的組合實際賺了 2,628%，平均對數報酬卻只說 2,013%；組合含放空部位時，這個不等式就不一定成立。匯率、通膨這類「相乘」的因素，用對數報酬可以精確相加，簡單報酬則多出一個交叉項。

> [!kc]
> Two long positions of equal size have log returns of +0.10 and −0.10 over a month. What is the portfolio's simple return, and what does averaging the two log returns suggest?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > The simple returns are $e^{0.10}-1=10.52\%$ and $e^{-0.10}-1=-9.52\%$, so $R_p=0.5(10.52\%)+0.5(-9.52\%)=0.50\%$. The average log return is zero, which suggests a flat month. The portfolio gained because the winner's gain in money exceeds the loser's loss.

## Solving Growth Equations with Logarithms {lo=6}

### One equation, four unknowns

With an annual rate $R$, equation (1.1) over $\tau$ years reads $W_\tau=W_0(1+R)^\tau$. It links four quantities, and any three determine the fourth. The ending value needs only multiplication; the starting value, a present value, is the subject of [[LM6]]. The other two need logarithms or roots:

$$\tau=\frac{\ln(W_\tau/W_0)}{\ln(1+R)}=\frac{\ln(W_\tau/W_0)}{g},\qquad R=\Big(\frac{W_\tau}{W_0}\Big)^{1/\tau}-1,\qquad g=\frac{\ln(W_\tau/W_0)}{\tau}. \tag{1.16}$$

The time formula takes logs of both sides and applies the power rule, $\ln(W_\tau/W_0)=\tau\ln(1+R)$. In log terms every growth problem is a division: the log growth required, divided by the growth rate available or by the time available.

### Doubling time

Setting $W_\tau/W_0=2$ gives the **doubling time** $\tau_2=\ln2/\ln(1+R)=\ln2/g$. With a continuously compounded rate the formula is exact and simple, $\tau_2=0.6931/g$. The familiar rule of 72 approximates $\ln2/\ln(1+R)$ for annual rates; [[LM3]] derives it from a Taylor expansion and explains why it works best near 8%. [[ex:doubling]] compares the two for rates that appear in this module.

### Time to a goal and the required return

> [!example] A lump-sum retirement target {#retire}
> An investor aged 35 holds NT\$3,000,000 and wants NT\$20,000,000 at age 60, with no further contributions. (a) What annualized return is required? (b) If she earns 6% a year (an illustrative assumption), when does she reach the target? (c) If the target is NT\$20,000,000 in today's money and inflation runs at 2% a year (also illustrative), what nominal return is required?
>
> > [!solution]
> > (a) By (1.16) with $\tau=25$: $R=(20/3)^{1/25}-1=6.6667^{0.04}-1=7.88\%$. In log terms, $g=\ln6.6667/25=1.8971/25=7.59\%$.
> >
> > (b) $\tau=\ln6.6667/\ln1.06=1.8971/0.05827=32.56$ years, at age 67.6. At 60 she would hold $3{,}000{,}000\times1.06^{25}=\text{NT\$}12{,}875{,}612$.
> >
> > (c) Real growth and inflation add in logs, by (1.15): $g^{\text{nom}}=7.59\%+\ln1.02=7.59\%+1.98\%=9.57\%$, so $R^{\text{nom}}=e^{0.0957}-1=10.04\%$. Directly, $1.0788\times1.02-1=10.04\%$. Two points of inflation raise the required return by more than two points, because inflation compounds too.

### Drawdowns and recovery

A **drawdown** is the percentage decline from the highest value reached so far, $\mathrm{DD}_t:=1-W_t/\max(W_0,W_1,\dots,W_t)$; the running peak includes the starting value $W_0$. Recovering from a drawdown $\mathrm{DD}$ requires a gain $R_{\text{rec}}$ with $(1-\mathrm{DD})(1+R_{\text{rec}})=1$:

$$R_{\text{rec}}=\frac{1}{1-\mathrm{DD}}-1=\frac{\mathrm{DD}}{1-\mathrm{DD}}. \tag{1.17}$$

The required gain grows faster than the loss: a 10% drawdown needs +11.1%, a 50% drawdown +100% and a 90% drawdown +900%. In logs the asymmetry disappears. A drawdown is a log return of $\ln(1-\mathrm{DD})$, and recovery is the same distance back, $-\ln(1-\mathrm{DD})$. At a log growth rate $g$, recovery therefore takes $\tau_{\text{rec}}=-\ln(1-\mathrm{DD})/g$ years by (1.16); [[ex:recovery]] tabulates both views.

> [!pitfall] Gains and losses do not cancel
> A −20% month followed by a +20% month leaves −4%, because $0.8\times1.2=0.96$. Equal percentage moves in opposite directions always lose money; equal log moves cancel exactly. Translate percentages into log points before judging a recovery, a strategy's worst loss or a "bounce".

## Return Calculations in Python {lo=7}

Three habits keep return code honest. Store prices and cash flows exactly as delivered and apply corporate actions explicitly. Compute log returns with `log1p`, which stays accurate for tiny returns. Keep every convention, such as the base date, the day count and $A$, in a named variable instead of a hard-coded number.

The first block rebuilds 0050's total-return series from the raw FinMind files and reproduces the case.

```python
import glob
from datetime import date

import numpy as np
import polars as pl

# 1. raw closes (FinMind TaiwanStockPrice) and cash dividends (TaiwanStockDividendResult)
px = (pl.concat([pl.read_csv(f) for f in sorted(glob.glob("data/raw/0050__*.csv"))])
        .sort("date").select("date", "close"))
div = pl.read_csv("data/raw/0050_dividends.csv").select("date", pl.col("cash_dividend").alias("D"))
SPLIT_DATE, SPLIT_RATIO = "2025-06-18", 4.0                     # 4-for-1, effective date

# 2. total return R_t = (P_t + D_t) / (P_{t-1} / ratio_t) - 1: the previous close is restated per new share
ret = (px.join(div, on="date", how="left")
         .sort("date")                                            # never rely on row order after a join
         .with_columns(pl.col("D").fill_null(0.0),
                       pl.when(pl.col("date") == pl.lit(SPLIT_DATE)).then(pl.lit(SPLIT_RATIO))
                         .otherwise(pl.lit(1.0)).alias("ratio"))
         .with_columns(((pl.col("close") + pl.col("D")) / (pl.col("close").shift(1) / pl.col("ratio")) - 1).alias("R"))
         .drop_nulls("R")
         .with_columns(pl.col("R").log1p().alias("r"), pl.col("date").str.to_date()))

# 3. growth factor, annualized and continuously compounded returns, eqs (1.9), (1.10), (1.12)
BASE, YEAR = date(2014, 10, 31), 365.25                           # base close; days per year for spans in years
G = float(np.exp(ret["r"].sum()))
tau = (ret["date"][-1] - BASE).days / YEAR
print(f"G = {G:.4f}  HPR = {G - 1:.2%}  R_G = {G ** (1 / tau) - 1:.2%}  g = {np.log(G) / tau:.2%}")
# G = 10.0225  HPR = 902.25%  R_G = 21.30%  g = 19.31%
```

The second block computes drawdowns from the running peak and finds the 2025 recovery date of [[eg:recover]].

```python
# 4. drawdown DD_t = 1 - W_t / running peak (the peak includes W_0 = 1), gain needed (1.17), recovery date
dd = (ret.select("date", (1 + pl.col("R")).cum_prod().alias("W"))
         .with_columns(pl.max_horizontal(pl.col("W").cum_max(), pl.lit(1.0)).alias("peak"))
         .with_columns((1 - pl.col("W") / pl.col("peak")).alias("DD")))
trough = (dd.filter(pl.col("date").is_between(date(2025, 1, 1), date(2025, 6, 30)))
            .sort("DD", descending=True).row(0, named=True))
back = dd.filter((pl.col("date") > trough["date"]) & (pl.col("W") >= trough["peak"]))["date"][0]
need = trough["DD"] / (1 - trough["DD"])
print(trough["date"], f"DD {trough['DD']:.2%}", f"gain needed {need:.2%}", "recovered", back, (back - trough["date"]).days, "days")
# 2025-04-09 DD 27.49% gain needed 37.91% recovered 2025-07-17 99 days
```

The raw-file series counts 0050's own 2,906 sessions, four more than the aligned series of the daily example. It keeps the four March 2026 sessions on which 00631L did not trade. Growth factors, drawdowns and recovery dates are identical in both.

## Summary {-}

- Growth multiplies and logarithms add: the product rule (1.2) turns products into sums and powers into multiples. Any logarithm is the natural log rescaled, $\log_\beta x=\ln x/\ln\beta$; 0050's log growth of 2.3048 equals 3.325 doublings.
- On a log axis equal distances are equal ratios and constant growth is a straight line. Linear axes make early history look flat and recent moves look extreme.
- A stated rate $r^{(n)}$ compounded $n$ times a year has an EAR of $(1+r^{(n)}/n)^n-1$, rising with $n$ toward $e^r-1$. The number $e=\lim(1+1/n)^n=2.71828\ldots$ is one unit compounded continuously at 100% for a year.
- Compounding frequency matters little at deposit rates (1.4 bp at 1.725%, monthly) and a great deal at high rates (118 bp at 15%, daily). Taiwan caps card revolving interest at 15% and forbids compounding it.
- The holding-period return, the annualized return $R_G=G^{1/\tau}-1$ and the continuously compounded return $g=\ln G/\tau$ express one growth factor three ways. Here $\tau$ is in years, calendar days ÷ 365.25. $R_G$ and $g$ are one rate quoted annually and continuously, $R_G=e^g-1$; for 0050 they were 21.30% and 19.31%.
- Annualizing short-period returns is extrapolation and depends on conventions: a 90-day return of 10.33% annualizes to anything from 39.9% to 50.1%. Taiwan's market held about 244 sessions a year in 2015–2025, and the case sample has $A=243.1$, not 252.
- Log returns add across time and simple returns add across positions. For long-only weights, averaging log returns across positions understates the portfolio; for a 50/50 mix of 0050 and 00631L the error was 615 percentage points.
- Time to goal and required return come from one equation solved with logs. A drawdown $\mathrm{DD}$ needs a gain of $\mathrm{DD}/(1-\mathrm{DD})$ but only $-\ln(1-\mathrm{DD})$ log points. 0050 regained its January–April 2025 drawdown of 27.49%, which needed +37.91%, in 99 days.
