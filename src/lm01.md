## Introduction

Every return in finance, from a deposit rate to a fund's lifetime performance, describes one quantity: the **growth factor**, ending value divided by starting value. Growth compounds, so it multiplies, and the mathematics of repeated multiplication is the exponential function and its inverse, the logarithm. This module builds both from first principles, derives the number $e$ from compound interest, and turns them into conversions that practitioners perform every day. The same tools recur in every later module, from the Taylor approximations of [[LM3]] to the lognormal prices of [[LM29]].

> [!case] One investment, three numbers
> Suppose you bought Yuanta Taiwan 50 (0050) at the close of 31 October 2014 and reinvested every cash dividend until 8 October 2026. NT\$100,000 became NT\$1,002,247.
>
> Three reports describe the holding. A fact sheet says the fund returned **+902%**. A performance review says it earned **21.3% a year**. A backtest log says it grew at **19.3% a year**.
>
> All three are correct, and they describe the same money. Yet 21.3% and 19.3% cannot both be "the" annual return, and neither equals 902% divided by twelve years. Which number belongs in a client report, which belongs in a model, and how does each convert into the others? Section 4 resolves the case; Sections 2 and 3 build the tools.

Section 2 develops exponents, logarithms and log-scale charts. Section 3 turns periodic interest into effective annual rates, takes the limit of continuous compounding and arrives at $e$. Section 4 defines the three return measures, converts among them and resolves the case.

Section 5 shows how returns add across time and across positions. Section 6 solves growth equations for time, required return and drawdown recovery, using 0050's crashes of 2024 and 2025. Section 7 assembles the calculations in Python.

> [!note] Notation used throughout the book
> $W_t$ is wealth at time $t$ and $G=W_T/W_0$ the **growth factor** over a holding period. $R$ is a **simple return**; over a single holding period with income reinvested, $R=G-1$. Its partner $r=\ln(1+R)$ is the matching **log return**.
>
> Over a horizon of $T$ years, $R_G$ is the **annualized return** and $g$ the **continuously compounded return per year**, or log growth rate. A rate quoted per year but credited $n$ times a year is written $r^{(n)}$; the superscript labels the frequency and is not a power. We write $=$ only for exact statements, $\approx$ for approximations whose neglected order is stated, and $:=$ for definitions.

## Exponents and Logarithms {lo=1}

### Growth multiplies

Wealth grows by factors. A return $R_1$ in the first period and $R_2$ in the second turn $W_0$ into $W_0(1+R_1)(1+R_2)$. With the same return $R$ in each of $T$ periods,

$$W_T=W_0\,(1+R)^{T}. \tag{1.1}$$

The variable $T$ sits in the exponent, so wealth is an **exponential function** of time. Doubling the horizon squares the growth factor instead of doubling it, which is why long-horizon numbers defy intuition.

### Laws of exponents

For a base $a>0$ and real exponents $x$ and $y$,

$$a^xa^y=a^{x+y},\qquad \frac{a^x}{a^y}=a^{x-y},\qquad (a^x)^y=a^{xy},\qquad a^0=1,\qquad a^{-x}=\frac{1}{a^x}.$$

For whole-number exponents these are counting rules: $a^2a^3$ contains $2+3$ factors of $a$. Defining $a^{1/m}$ as the positive $m$-th root and $a^{p/m}:=(a^{1/m})^p$ extends them to fractions. Continuity extends them to all real exponents (stated without proof; see Stewart's chapter on exponential functions). [[ex:laws]] gives each law a financial reading.

### Logarithms

> [!definition] Logarithm
> For a base $b>0$ with $b\ne1$ and a number $y>0$, the **logarithm** $\log_by$ is the exponent to which $b$ must be raised to give $y$:
> $$\log_by=x\iff b^x=y.$$
> The **natural logarithm** $\ln y:=\log_ey$ uses the base $e=2.71828\ldots$, a constant whose origin in compound interest is the subject of Section 3.

Because $\log_b$ undoes $b^x$, every law of exponents becomes a law of logarithms. Let $u=\log_bx$ and $v=\log_by$, so that $x=b^u$ and $y=b^v$. Then $xy=b^ub^v=b^{u+v}$, which by the definition means

$$\log_b(xy)=\log_bx+\log_by. \tag{1.2}$$

The same steps give $\log_b(x/y)=\log_bx-\log_by$ and $\log_b1=0$. From $x^p=(b^u)^p=b^{pu}$ follows the power rule $\log_b(x^p)=p\log_bx$. A logarithm turns multiplication into addition and powers into multiples. That is why log returns add over time (Section 5) and why growth equations are solved with logs (Section 6).

> [!exhibit] Laws of exponents and logarithms, read as finance {#laws}
> | Rule | Exponent form<br>Logarithm form | Financial reading |
> |:--|:--|:--|
> | Product | ${a^xa^y=a^{x+y}}$<br>${\log(xy)=\log x+\log y}$ | Growth over consecutive periods multiplies; log growth adds |
> | Quotient | ${a^x/a^y=a^{x-y}}$<br>${\log(x/y)=\log x-\log y}$ | The log return between two dates is a difference of log prices |
> | Power | ${(a^x)^y=a^{xy}}$<br>${\log x^p=p\log x}$ | $T$ periods at a constant rate: $\ln(1+R)^T=T\ln(1+R)$ |
> | Reciprocal | ${a^{-x}=1/a^x}$<br>${\log(1/x)=-\log x}$ | Discounting, $1.05^{-1}=0.9524$; a loss and the gain that undoes it have opposite logs |
> | Root | ${a^{1/m}=\sqrt[m]{a}}$<br>${\log x^{1/m}=\tfrac1m\log x}$ | De-annualizing: $1.01725^{1/12}-1=0.1426\%$ a month |
> | Change of base | ${b^x=e^{x\ln b}}$<br>${\log_bx=\ln x/\ln b}$ | Doublings in a growth factor: $\log_2G=\ln G/\ln2$ |

### Change of base

Any logarithm is the natural logarithm rescaled. Since $x=b^{\log_bx}$, taking natural logs of both sides and applying the power rule gives $\ln x=\log_bx\cdot\ln b$, so

$$\log_bx=\frac{\ln x}{\ln b}. \tag{1.3}$$

The base is a unit of measurement, like kilometers and miles. For 0050, $\ln G=\ln10.0225=2.3048$. In base 2 that is $2.3048/\ln2=3.325$ doublings; in base 10 it is $2.3048/\ln10=1.001$ tenfold increases. The fund grew almost exactly tenfold, which is why its log growth is close to $\ln10=2.3026$.

> [!example] Splitting 0050's growth into price and dividends {#split}
> 0050 closed at NT\$65.30 on 31 October 2014 and at NT\$114.95 on 8 October 2026, after a 4-for-1 split. It paid 22 cash dividends in between, and its total-return growth factor was $G=10.0225$. Split the log growth into a price part and a dividend part, and express each in doublings.
>
> > [!solution]
> > One 2014 share became four 2026 shares, so the price-only growth factor is $G_P=4\times114.95/65.30=459.80/65.30=7.0413$, a price return of +604.13%.
> >
> > On every day the total return factors exactly as
> > $$1+R_t=\frac{P_t+D_t}{P_{t-1}}=\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\Big(1+\frac{D_t}{P_t}\Big),$$
> > where $D_t$ is the cash dividend on an ex-date and zero otherwise. Multiplying over all days and applying the product rule (1.2),
> > $$\ln G=\ln G_P+\sum_{\text{ex-dates}}\ln\Big(1+\frac{D_t}{P_t}\Big)=1.9518+0.3530=2.3048 .$$
> > Dividends supplied 15.3% of the log growth. In doublings, dividing each term by $\ln2$: price $2.816$ plus dividends $0.509$ equals $3.325$.
> >
> > In simple returns the decomposition is multiplicative: $G=G_P\,e^{0.3530}$, so reinvested dividends multiplied ending wealth by $e^{0.3530}=1.423$. The price return of +604.13% and the dividend effect of +42.3% compound to +902.25%; they do not add. Logs make the split exact and additive.

> [!pitfall] Log of what, in which base
> Take logs of growth factors or prices, never of returns: $\ln(1+R)$ exists for every $R>-100\%$, while $\ln R$ fails for every loss. Check the base too: in Excel, `LOG(x)` is base 10 unless a base is given and `LN(x)` is natural. In Python, `math.log` and `np.log` are natural and `np.log10` is base 10. Mixing them scales every result by $\ln10=2.3026$, an error that changes no signs and is easy to miss.

### Log-scale charts

A logarithmic axis places a value $W$ at a height proportional to $\ln W$. Two consequences follow from the quotient rule. Equal vertical distances are equal ratios, so the step from 1 to 2 is as tall as the step from 4 to 8. And constant growth $W_t=W_0e^{gt}$ becomes the straight line $\ln W_t=\ln W_0+gt$, whose slope is the log growth rate $g$.

[[ex:loglin]] plots 0050's total-return index on both axes, with gridlines at successive doublings and the constant-rate path that ends at the same value.

> [!exhibit] 0050 total-return index on a linear and a logarithmic axis, November 2014 – October 2026 {#loglin}
> <div data-chart="lm01-linear"></div>
> <div data-chart="lm01-log"></div>
>
> Note: growth of 1 unit with dividends reinvested; gridlines at 1, 2, 4 and 8. The dashed path grows at a constant 21.3% a year and ends at the same value, 10.02. Markers show the first close at or above each doubling. Source: FinMind, retrieved 10 October 2026; `data/lm01_charts.py`.

On the linear axis the first six years look flat and the end looks explosive. Even the constant-rate path, which by construction grows at the same rate every year, appears to accelerate. On the log axis that path is a straight line, so only genuine changes in the growth rate bend the index away from it.

The doublings put numbers on the picture. The index first closed above 2 on 11 November 2020, above 4 on 4 July 2024 and above 8 on 27 April 2026. The three doublings took 6.0, 3.6 and 1.8 years.

Declines are distorted as well. The April 2025 decline cost 1.18 units of the index and the 2015 decline 0.23 units, a ratio of five. In percentage terms they were −27.5% and −21.0%, or 0.32 and 0.24 log points.

> [!zh] 對數座標：看倍數，不看差額
> 對數把乘法變成加法，把「倍數」變成「距離」。在對數座標上，從 1 漲到 2 和從 4 漲到 8 的高度一樣，因為都是翻一倍；固定成長率的路徑是一條直線，斜率就是對數成長率 $g$。底數只是單位：$\ln$ 是自然單位，$\log_2$ 數「翻了幾倍」，$\log_{10}$ 數「成長了幾個十倍」，彼此只差一個常數倍。0050 在 2025 年 4 月的下跌，在線性圖上看起來是 2015 年那次的五倍，換成對數其實只是 0.32 對 0.24。看長期走勢、比較不同年代的漲跌，請用對數座標。

## Compounding Frequency and the Number e {lo=2}

### Simple and compound interest

Under **simple interest**, interest accrues on the principal only, so wealth grows linearly: $W_T=W_0(1+qT)$ at a rate $q$ a year. Under **compound interest**, interest is credited to the balance and earns interest itself, which gives the exponential growth of (1.1). The two agree after exactly one period and diverge after that. Both appear in Taiwan retail finance, as the examples below show.

### Periodic, stated and effective rates

Banks quote rates per year but credit interest more often. A **stated annual rate** $r^{(n)}$ (名目年利率) credited $n$ times a year pays the **periodic rate** $r^{(n)}/n$ each period. After one year a unit has grown to $(1+r^{(n)}/n)^n$.

> [!definition] Effective annual rate
> The **effective annual rate** (EAR, 有效年利率) of a stated rate $r^{(n)}$ compounded $n$ times a year is the annual simple return it delivers:
> $$R=\Big(1+\frac{r^{(n)}}{n}\Big)^{n}-1. \tag{1.4}$$
> Conversely, the stated rate with frequency $n$ that delivers a given EAR $R$ is $r^{(n)}=n\big[(1+R)^{1/n}-1\big]$.

The EAR is the common currency for comparing quotes: two rates are equivalent when their EARs match. [[ex:ear]] applies (1.4) to two Taiwan benchmarks, Bank of Taiwan's one-year time-deposit rate and the legal cap on credit-card revolving interest.

> [!exhibit] Effective annual rate by compounding frequency {#ear}
> | Compounding | $n$ | Periodic rate at 15% | EAR at 1.725% | EAR at 15% |
> |:--|--:|--:|--:|--:|
> | Annual | 1 | 15.0000% | 1.7250% | 15.000% |
> | Semiannual | 2 | 7.5000% | 1.7324% | 15.562% |
> | Quarterly | 4 | 3.7500% | 1.7362% | 15.865% |
> | Monthly | 12 | 1.2500% | 1.7387% | 16.075% |
> | Weekly | 52 | 0.2885% | 1.7397% | 16.158% |
> | Daily | 365 | 0.0411% | 1.7399% | 16.180% |
> | Continuous | $\infty$ | — | 1.7400% | 16.183% |
>
> Note: 1.725% is Bank of Taiwan's posted fixed rate for one-year time savings deposits (定期儲蓄存款) on 10 October 2026; 15% is the legal maximum for credit-card revolving interest (Banking Act, Article 47-1). The continuous row is $e^{r}-1$, derived below.

Two features stand out. The EAR rises with $n$ but by ever smaller steps, and it levels off at a finite limit. And frequency matters far more at high rates: moving from annual to continuous compounding adds 1.5 bp at 1.725% but 118 bp at 15%. The gap grows roughly with the square of the rate, a second-order effect that [[LM3]] quantifies.

> [!example] A Taiwan time deposit, two ways {#deposit}
> Bank of Taiwan posts a fixed rate of 1.725% a year for one-year time savings deposits. Typical Taiwan bank terms (DBS Bank Taiwan's, for instance) offer two forms. Under 整存整付 interest is compounded monthly and paid at maturity; under 存本取息 it accrues daily on an actual/365 basis and is paid out monthly. Compare the interest on NT\$1,000,000 over one year.
>
> > [!solution]
> > 整存整付 compounds the periodic rate $0.01725/12=0.14375\%$ twelve times: $R=1.0014375^{12}-1=1.7387\%$, or NT\$17,387 of interest at maturity.
> >
> > 存本取息 pays $1{,}000{,}000\times1.725\%\times d/365$ for a month of $d$ days: NT\$1,465.07 for 31 days, NT\$1,417.81 for 30 days and NT\$1,323.29 for 28 days. Over a 365-day year the payments total NT\$17,250, an effective 1.7250% unless the saver reinvests each payment.
> >
> > The same posted rate yields NT\$137 more under monthly compounding, 1.37 bp of principal. At deposit rates near 2% the compounding frequency is almost a rounding error. The next example shows where it is not.

> [!example] The credit-card cap and the ban on compounding {#card}
> Since 1 September 2015, Article 47-1 of Taiwan's Banking Act has capped credit-card revolving interest at 15% a year. Issuers accrue it as balance × rate × days/365, and Article 48 of the Regulations Governing Institutions Engaging in Credit Card Business forbids compounding it. Suppose a revolving balance of NT\$100,000 runs for a year at the cap, with no repayments and no new purchases. Compute the interest, and compare it with daily compounding at the same stated rate.
>
> > [!solution]
> > Without compounding, interest accrues on the principal only: $100{,}000\times15\%\times365/365=15{,}000$, so NT\$15,000 and an EAR of exactly 15.000%.
> >
> > With daily compounding the periodic rate is $0.15/365=0.041096\%$ a day (about 萬分之 4.11), and (1.4) gives $R=1.00041096^{365}-1=16.180\%$, or NT\$16,180. Continuous compounding would give $e^{0.15}-1=16.183\%$.
> >
> > The no-compounding rule is worth NT\$1,180 a year on this balance. It also makes the legal 15% an honest effective rate: a lender allowed to compound daily could quote 13.979% and still collect a 15% EAR.

### From discrete to continuous: the number $e$

Push the frequency to its limit. In 1683 Jacob Bernoulli asked what happens to one unit at 100% interest a year when interest is credited ever more often (O'Connor and Robertson). With $n$ credits the year-end value is $(1+1/n)^n$, and [[ex:bernoulli]] shows it rising but leveling off.

> [!exhibit] Bernoulli's sequence $(1+1/n)^n$ {#bernoulli}
> | Crediting | $n$ | $(1+1/n)^n$ | Distance to $e$ |
> |:--|--:|--:|--:|
> | Annual | 1 | 2.000000 | 0.718282 |
> | Semiannual | 2 | 2.250000 | 0.468282 |
> | Quarterly | 4 | 2.441406 | 0.276876 |
> | Monthly | 12 | 2.613035 | 0.105247 |
> | Weekly | 52 | 2.692597 | 0.025685 |
> | Daily | 365 | 2.714567 | 0.003714 |
> | Hourly | 8,760 | 2.718127 | 0.000155 |
> | Every minute | 525,600 | 2.718279 | 0.000003 |
>
> Note: $e=2.718281828\ldots$; the distance to $e$ shrinks roughly in proportion to $1/n$.

> [!definition] The number $e$
> $$e:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Big(1+\frac1n\Big)^{n}=2.718281828\ldots \tag{1.5}$$
> The sequence increases with $n$ and never exceeds 3, so the limit exists; the Deep Dive below proves both facts.

The general limit follows from (1.5) by a change of variable.

> [!proposition] Continuous compounding
> For every real number $x$,
> $$\lim_{n\to\infty}\Big(1+\frac{x}{n}\Big)^{n}=e^{x}. \tag{1.6}$$

*Proof.* For $x=0$ both sides equal 1. For $x>0$, put $m=n/x$, so that $x/n=1/m$ and $n=mx$:
$$\Big(1+\frac{x}{n}\Big)^{n}=\Big[\Big(1+\frac1m\Big)^{m}\Big]^{x}.$$
As $n\to\infty$, $m\to\infty$ through real values and the bracket tends to $e$; the Deep Dive shows that non-integer $m$ are allowed. Because $t\mapsto t^x$ is continuous, the whole expression tends to $e^x$. For $x=-y<0$, note that $1-y/n=1/\big(1+\tfrac{y}{n-y}\big)$, so that
$$\Big(1-\frac{y}{n}\Big)^{n}=\frac{1}{\big(1+\frac{y}{n-y}\big)^{n-y}\big(1+\frac{y}{n-y}\big)^{y}}\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{1}{e^{y}\cdot1}=e^{-y},$$
by the case already proved, applied with $n-y$ in place of $n$. $\blacksquare$

Applied to money, (1.6) says that a stated rate $r$ compounded continuously turns $W_0$ into

$$W_T=W_0\,e^{rT}, \tag{1.7}$$

so its effective annual rate, and the inverse conversion, are

$$R=e^{r}-1,\qquad r=\ln(1+R). \tag{1.8}$$

The continuously compounded rate equivalent to an EAR is simply its natural logarithm. Every stated rate with the same EAR lies between the two: $r^{(n)}$ falls from $r^{(1)}=R$ toward $r$ as $n$ grows. More frequent crediting needs a lower quote to deliver the same EAR.

> [!deepdive]- Why $(1+1/n)^n$ converges, and why $e=\sum_{k\ge0}1/k!$
> **Increasing.** Apply the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality (stated without proof) to the $n+1$ numbers $1,\,1+\tfrac1n,\dots,1+\tfrac1n$. Their arithmetic mean is $\frac{1+n(1+1/n)}{n+1}=1+\frac1{n+1}$ and their geometric mean is $\big(1+\tfrac1n\big)^{n/(n+1)}$. The numbers are not all equal, so the geometric mean is strictly smaller:
> $$\Big(1+\frac1n\Big)^{n/(n+1)}<1+\frac1{n+1}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Big(1+\frac1n\Big)^{n}<\Big(1+\frac1{n+1}\Big)^{n+1}.$$
> **Bounded.** By the binomial theorem,
> $$\Big(1+\frac1n\Big)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom nk\frac1{n^k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac1{k!}\cdot\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{n^k}\le\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac1{k!}\le1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac1{2^{k-1}}<3,$$
> because each factor $(n-j)/n\le1$ and $k!\ge2^{k-1}$. An increasing sequence that is bounded above converges, so $e$ exists and $e\le3$.
>
> **The series.** The display also shows $(1+1/n)^n\le\sum_{k=0}^{n}1/k!$. Conversely, fix $m$ and keep only the first $m+1$ terms: for $n>m$, $(1+1/n)^n\ge\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac1{k!}\prod_{j<k}\big(1-\frac jn\big)$, which tends to $\sum_{k=0}^{m}1/k!$ as $n\to\infty$. Hence $e=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}1/k!$. Its partial sums 2, 2.5, 2.666667, 2.708333, 2.716667 and 2.718056 converge far faster than Bernoulli's sequence.
>
> **Real $m$.** For $k\le m<k+1$, $\big(1+\tfrac1{k+1}\big)^{k}\le\big(1+\tfrac1m\big)^{m}\le\big(1+\tfrac1k\big)^{k+1}$, and both bounds tend to $e$ as $k\to\infty$. So (1.5) also holds as $m\to\infty$ through real numbers. $\blacksquare$

> [!pitfall] Comparing stated rates across frequencies
> A stated rate means little until its compounding frequency is known. A deposit at 1.74% paid annually beats one at 1.725% compounded monthly, but only by 0.13 bp: 1.7400% against 1.7387%. Convert every quote to an EAR, or to a continuously compounded rate, before comparing. The same holds for any annual figure built from shorter periods, including the return figures of Section 4.

## Holding-Period, Annualized and Continuously Compounded Returns {lo=3,4}

### Holding-period return

> [!definition] Holding-period return
> The **holding-period return** (HPR) from time 0 to time $T$ is the gain per unit invested, income included:
> $$R_{0,T}=\frac{P_T-P_0+D}{P_0}.$$
> Over several periods with income reinvested, period returns chain by multiplication: $1+R_{0,T}=\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1+R_t)=G$.

The HPR carries no time unit: +902.25% describes twelve years, while +10% might describe a week. A **price return** omits income; a **total return** includes it. For 0050 over the case period the price return was +604.13% and the total return +902.25% ([[eg:split]]). Fund comparisons should use total returns, because funds differ in how much they distribute.

### Annualized return

To compare holdings of different lengths, express each as a constant annual rate. The **annualized return** $R_G$ is the return which, compounded once a year for $T$ years, produces the same growth factor:

$$(1+R_G)^{T}=G\quad\Longrightarrow\quad R_G=G^{1/T}-1. \tag{1.9}$$

The root law of [[ex:laws]] does the work. For $T>1$ the exponent $1/T$ shrinks the growth factor. For $T<1$ it exceeds 1 and compounds a short-period return forward, which is extrapolation rather than measurement. [[LM3]] calls $R_G$ the geometric mean return and compares it with the arithmetic mean.

### Continuously compounded return

The **log return** over the holding period is $r_{0,T}=\ln(1+R_{0,T})=\ln G$. Divided by the horizon, it gives the **continuously compounded return per year**, also called the log growth rate:

$$g=\frac{\ln G}{T}=\ln(1+R_G). \tag{1.10}$$

The second equality is the power rule, $\ln G/T=\ln G^{1/T}=\ln(1+R_G)$. Comparing (1.10) with (1.8) shows what $g$ is: the continuously compounded rate equivalent to the effective annual rate $R_G$. The annualized return and the continuously compounded return are one growth rate quoted under two compounding conventions.

> [!exhibit] One growth factor, four expressions {#conv}
> | Quantity | Definition | Back to $G$ | 0050, 2014–2026 |
> |:--|:--|:--|--:|
> | Holding-period return | $R_{0,T}=G-1$ | $G=1+R_{0,T}$ | +902.25% |
> | Annualized return | $R_G=G^{1/T}-1$ | $G=(1+R_G)^{T}$ | 21.30% |
> | Log return over the period | $r_{0,T}=\ln G$ | $G=e^{r_{0,T}}$ | 2.3048 |
> | Continuously compounded return per year | $g=\ln G/T=\ln(1+R_G)$ | $G=e^{gT}$ | 19.31% |
>
> Note: $T=4{,}360/365.25=11.94$ years. Between the two annual measures, $R_G=e^{g}-1$ and $g=\ln(1+R_G)$, exactly as between an EAR and its continuous rate in (1.8).

> [!example] One investment, three numbers, resolved {#three}
> Use [[ex:case]] to verify that +902.25%, 21.30% a year and 19.31% a year describe the same holding. Then decide which figure to quote to a client and which to use in a model.
>
> > [!solution]
> > All three come from $G=10.0225$ and $T=4{,}360/365.25=11.9370$ years. By (1.9), $R_G=10.0225^{1/11.9370}-1=21.298\%$. By (1.10), $g=\ln10.0225/11.9370=2.30483/11.9370=19.308\%$. They are linked by (1.8): $e^{0.19308}-1=21.298\%$ and $\ln1.21298=0.19308$.
> >
> > With the inverse of (1.4), the same growth can be quoted at any frequency: 20.27% compounded semiannually, 19.46% compounded monthly, 19.31% compounded daily or continuously. The gap between 21.30% and 19.31% is a quoting convention, not a disagreement about performance.
> >
> > For a client, quote the annualized return. Like a deposit's EAR it compounds once a year, so it compares directly with deposit rates and with other funds' annualized figures.
> >
> > For modeling, use $g$. It adds over time (Section 5), it scales linearly with the horizon, $\ln G=gT$, and it is the rate in the continuous-time models of [[LM29]] and [[LM42]]. Quote the cumulative +902% only together with its horizon.

> [!pitfall] Dividing a cumulative return by the number of years
> $902.25\%/11.94=75.6\%$ "a year" is the simple-interest rate that produces 902.25%: $1+0.7558\times11.94=10.02$. It ignores compounding and overstates 0050's annualized return by a factor of 3.5. Divide log returns by time, never simple returns.

### Annualizing across horizons and day-count conventions

A return over part of a year annualizes the same way, with the horizon counted in a stated unit. Over $h$ calendar days, or over $N$ trading sessions,

$$R_G=(1+R_h)^{365/h}-1\qquad\text{or}\qquad R_G=(1+R_N)^{D/N}-1, \tag{1.11}$$

where $D$ is the number of trading days per year. For log returns the conversion is linear, $g=r_h\times365/h$ or $g=r_N\times D/N$, because logs add over time. Linear scaling of a simple return, $R_h\times365/h$, produces a stated rate with $h$-day compounding, not an EAR.

Many analysts and software packages set $D=252$, but Taiwan's market is less busy. Counting the days on which 0050 or 00631L traded, the Taiwan Stock Exchange held between 239 and 247 sessions a year in 2015–2025. The yearly counts were 244, 244, 246, 247, 242, 245, 244, 246, 239, 242 and 243, an average of 243.8. Within one sample, the right $D$ is the number of return observations per year in that sample.

> [!example] Annualizing a 90-day return {#ninety}
> Over the 90 calendar days and 61 sessions from the close of 9 July to the close of 7 October 2026, 0050's price rose from NT\$105.80 to NT\$116.05. The fund also paid a NT\$0.60 dividend with ex-date 21 July. Its total return was 10.33%. Annualize it five ways.
>
> > [!solution]
> > Calendar days, by (1.11): $1.1033^{365/90}-1=48.99\%$. Trading days with $D=252$: $1.1033^{252/61}-1=50.10\%$; with Taiwan's average $D=243.8$: $1.1033^{243.8/61}-1=48.13\%$. Continuously compounded: $\ln1.1033\times365/90=0.0983\times4.056=39.87\%$ a year. Simple scaling: $10.33\%\times365/90=41.89\%$.
> >
> > One 90-day return yields "annual" figures from 39.9% to 50.1%. Each is a correct conversion under its own convention, and each assumes that the same 10.33% recurs every 90 days for a year. Report the 10.33% for the period, and state the convention whenever you annualize.

> [!example] Annualizing a daily mean log return: 252 or 243? {#daily}
> The case series has 2,902 daily returns; it is the series of [[LM3]], aligned with 00631L's trading days. Their mean log return was 0.079422%. Annualize it with $D=252$ and with the sample's own frequency, and compare the results with $g$.

## Adding Returns Across Time and Across Assets {lo=5}

### Across time, log returns add

Over consecutive periods growth factors multiply, $G=\prod_t(1+R_t)$. The product rule (1.2) turns the product into a sum:

$$\ln G=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ln(1+R_t)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_t. \tag{1.12}$$

The identity is exact for any path. Simple returns do not add over time, and their sum has no meaning. [[ex:blocks]] splits 0050's twelve years into six two-year blocks.

> [!exhibit] 0050 in two-year blocks: simple returns compound, log returns add {#blocks}
> | Block (close to close) | Days | Simple return $R$ | Log return $r$ |
> |:--|--:|--:|--:|
> | 2014-10-31 → 2016-10-31 | 731 | 16.03% | 0.1487 |
> | 2016-10-31 → 2018-10-31 | 730 | 14.06% | 0.1316 |
> | 2018-10-31 → 2020-10-30 | 730 | 43.56% | 0.3616 |
> | 2020-10-30 → 2022-10-31 | 731 | 2.35% | 0.0232 |
> | 2022-10-31 → 2024-10-30 | 730 | 106.47% | 0.7250 |
> | 2024-10-30 → 2026-10-08 | 708 | 149.61% | 0.9147 |
> | **Whole period** | **4,360** | **902.25%** (compounded) | **2.3048** (summed) |
>
> Note: blocks end at the last close on or before 31 October. The whole-period simple return is $\prod(1+R)-1$; adding the six simple returns gives 332.08%, which describes nothing. The six log returns sum to $\ln G=2.3048$.

The table also shows how simple returns distort comparisons. The last block's 149.61% looks more than ten times the second block's 14.06%; in log points it is seven times, 0.9147 against 0.1316. The final two blocks supplied 71% of the twelve-year log growth, the steep end of the log chart in [[ex:loglin]]. Averaging the six simple returns would also overstate the typical compound return; that gap, volatility drag, belongs to [[LM3]] and [[LM19]].

### Across assets, simple returns add

A portfolio is a sum of money, not a product. Let positions $i=1,\dots,N$ start with values $V_{i,0}$, total value $V_0$ and weights $w_i=V_{i,0}/V_0$. If position $i$ returns $R_i$ over the period, the portfolio ends at $\sum_iV_{i,0}(1+R_i)$, so

$$R_p=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_iR_i \tag{1.13}$$

exactly, with weights measured at the start of the period. Log returns do not aggregate this way. The portfolio's log return is $r_p=\ln\big(\sum_iw_ie^{r_i}\big)$.

The weighted arithmetic–geometric mean inequality states that $\sum_iw_ix_i\ge\prod_ix_i^{w_i}$ for positive $x_i$ (stated without proof; [[LM14]] derives it from Jensen's inequality). Applied with $x_i=e^{r_i}$ and followed by logs, it gives

$$r_p=\ln\Big(\sum_iw_ie^{r_i}\Big)\ \ge\ \sum_iw_ir_i, \tag{1.14}$$

with equality only when all $r_i$ are equal. Averaging log returns across positions therefore understates the portfolio's return, and the error grows with the dispersion of the positions' returns.

> [!example] Averaging log returns across two funds {#avglog}
> On 31 October 2014 an investor puts half her money in 0050 and half in 00631L, the daily 2× fund on the same index. She holds both until 8 October 2026. 0050's growth factor was 10.02 and 00631L's 44.54, log growths of 2.3048 and 3.7965. Compute the portfolio's return correctly and by averaging log returns.
>
> > [!solution]
> > By (1.13) the portfolio's growth factor is $0.5\times10.02+0.5\times44.54=27.28$, a return of +2,628%.
> >
> > Averaging log returns gives $0.5\times2.3048+0.5\times3.7965=3.0507$, which implies $e^{3.0507}=21.13$, or +2,013%. That understates the portfolio by 615 percentage points. The averaged figure is the log of $\sqrt{10.02\times44.54}=21.13$, the geometric mean of the two growth factors, which no buy-and-hold investor earned.
> >
> > The correct portfolio log return is $\ln27.28=3.306$, larger than the average 3.0507, as (1.14) requires.

### Across multiplicative factors: currency and inflation

Some returns combine by multiplication rather than by weighting. A Taiwan investor in a US-dollar asset earns the asset's dollar return and the change in the TWD price of a dollar:

$$1+R^{\text{TWD}}=(1+R^{\text{USD}})(1+R^{\text{FX}})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad r^{\text{TWD}}=r^{\text{USD}}+r^{\text{FX}} \tag{1.15}$$

exactly in logs. In simple returns, $R^{\text{TWD}}=R^{\text{USD}}+R^{\text{FX}}+R^{\text{USD}}R^{\text{FX}}$ carries a cross term. Inflation works the same way: a real return satisfies $1+R^{\text{real}}=(1+R)/(1+\pi)$, so $r^{\text{real}}=r-\ln(1+\pi)$ exactly. The familiar shortcut $R^{\text{real}}\approx R-\pi$ drops a second-order term, which [[LM3]] measures.

> [!example] A US-dollar asset seen from Taiwan {#fx}
> A US equity fund gains 20% in US dollars over a year in which USD/TWD falls from 32.0 to 29.6 (hypothetical figures). Compute the TWD return exactly, by adding log returns, and by adding simple returns.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The dollar lost $29.6/32.0-1=-7.50\%$ against the TWD. Exactly, $1.20\times0.925-1=11.00\%$. In logs, $\ln1.20+\ln0.925=0.18232-0.07796=0.10436$, and $e^{0.10436}-1=11.00\%$. 
> >
> > Adding simple returns gives $20\%-7.5\%=12.5\%$, which overstates by the cross term $0.20\times(-0.075)=-1.50\%$. The currency loss applies to the whole dollar value, gains included.

> [!result] Aggregation rules
> - **Across time:** log returns add, (1.12); simple returns compound.
> - **Across positions** over one period: simple returns add with start-of-period weights, (1.13); log returns do not, and their average understates the portfolio, (1.14).
> - **Across multiplicative factors** such as currency and inflation: log returns add exactly, (1.15); simple returns need a cross term.

> [!pitfall] Averaging log returns across positions
> Index and portfolio returns are value-weighted averages of simple returns. A sector or factor "return" computed as the average log return of its members is the log of a geometric mean. By (1.14) it lies below what an investor holding the members earned. Aggregate simple returns across names first; take logs of the portfolio afterwards if a log-return series is needed.

> [!zh] 時間用對數加，資產用簡單報酬加

## Solving Growth Equations with Logarithms {lo=6}

### One equation, four unknowns

Equation (1.1), $W_T=W_0(1+R)^T$, links four quantities, and any three determine the fourth. The ending value needs only multiplication; the starting value, a present value, is the subject of [[LM6]]. The other two need logarithms or roots:

$$T=\frac{\ln(W_T/W_0)}{\ln(1+R)}=\frac{\ln(W_T/W_0)}{g},\qquad R=\Big(\frac{W_T}{W_0}\Big)^{1/T}-1,\qquad g=\frac{\ln(W_T/W_0)}{T}. \tag{1.16}$$

The time formula takes logs of both sides of (1.1) and applies the power rule, $\ln(W_T/W_0)=T\ln(1+R)$. In log terms every growth problem is a division: the log growth required, divided by the growth rate available or by the time available.

### Doubling time

Setting $W_T/W_0=2$ gives the **doubling time** $T_2=\ln2/\ln(1+R)=\ln2/g$. With a continuously compounded rate the formula is exact and simple, $T_2=0.6931/g$. The familiar rule of 72 approximates $\ln2/\ln(1+R)$ for annual rates; [[LM3]] derives it from a Taylor expansion and explains why it works best near 8%.

### Time to a goal and the required return

> [!example] A lump-sum retirement target {#retire}

>
> > [!solution]
> > (a) By (1.16) with $T=25$: $R=(20/3)^{1/25}-1=6.6667^{0.04}-1=7.88\%$. In log terms, $g=\ln6.6667/25=1.8971/25=7.59\%$.
> >
> > (b) $T=\ln6.6667/\ln1.06=1.8971/0.05827=32.56$ years, at age 67.6. At 60 she would hold $3{,}000{,}000\times1.06^{25}=12{,}875{,}612$, that is NT\$12,875,612.
> >
> > (c) Real growth and inflation add in logs, by (1.15): $g^{\text{nom}}=7.59\%+\ln1.02=7.59\%+1.98\%=9.57\%$, so $R^{\text{nom}}=e^{0.0957}-1=10.04\%$. Directly, $1.0788\times1.02-1=10.04\%$. Two points of inflation raise the required return by more than two points, because inflation compounds too.

### Drawdowns and recovery

A **drawdown** is the percentage decline from the highest value reached so far, $D_t=1-W_t/\max_{s\le t}W_s$. Recovering from a drawdown $D$ requires a gain $R_{\text{rec}}$ with $(1-D)(1+R_{\text{rec}})=1$:

$$R_{\text{rec}}=\frac{1}{1-D}-1=\frac{D}{1-D}. \tag{1.17}$$

The required gain grows faster than the loss: −10% needs +11.1%, −50% needs +100% and −90% needs +900%. In logs the asymmetry disappears. A drawdown is a log return of $\ln(1-D)$, and recovery is the same distance back, $-\ln(1-D)$. At a log growth rate $g$, recovery therefore takes $T_{\text{rec}}=-\ln(1-D)/g$ years, by (1.16).

> [!example] Recovering from August 2024 and April 2025 {#recover}
> On 5 August 2024, 0050 fell 9.13%, after −5.05% the session before. On 7 April 2025, the first session after the Qingming holiday, markets reacted to new US tariffs. 0050 fell 10.00%, Taiwan's daily limit, then 3.25% and 4.60% on the next two sessions. Using [[ex:episodes]], compute for each episode the gain needed, the log distance and the recovery time.
>
> > [!solution]
> > **2024.** From the 11 July 2024 peak the index fell 21.30% by the 5 August close. Recovery required $0.21304/0.78696=+27.07\%$, a log distance of $-\ln0.78696=0.2396$. The index first closed above the old peak on 6 January 2025, 154 calendar days and 104 trading sessions after the trough.
> >
> > **2025.** The next session, 7 January 2025, set a new peak. By 2 April the index was already 12.71% below it. The three sessions from 7 to 9 April took it down a further 16.93%, to a drawdown of 27.49%.
> >
> > Recovery required $0.27490/0.72510=+37.91\%$, a log distance of $0.3214$. On 10 April alone the index rose 9.99%. It regained the peak on 17 July 2025, 99 calendar days and 64 sessions after the trough. 0050 did not trade on five sessions in June 2025, before its 4-for-1 split took effect.
> >
> > **The log view.** The 2025 loss splits additively into $-0.1359$ log points before 7 April and $-0.1855$ in the three crash sessions, $-0.3214$ in all. In simple returns, −12.71% and −16.93% compound to −27.49%. Recovery retraced exactly 0.3214 log points, a distance that reads as "+37.91%" in percent. At the long-run $g=19.31\%$ a year the climb would have taken $0.3214/0.1931=1.66$ years; it took 0.27.

> [!pitfall] Gains and losses do not cancel
> A −20% month followed by a +20% month leaves −4%, because $0.8\times1.2=0.96$. Equal percentage moves in opposite directions always lose money; equal log moves cancel exactly. Translate percentages into log points before judging a recovery, a strategy's worst loss or a "bounce".

> [!zh] 跌 50% 要漲 100%
> 回本所需的報酬是 $D/(1-D)$：跌 10% 要漲 11.1%，跌 50% 要漲 100%，跌 90% 要漲 900%，跌得越深，回本越陡。換成對數就對稱了：下跌 $-\ln(1-D)$ 個對數點，只要再漲回同樣多的對數點就能回本，所需時間＝對數跌幅 ÷ 對數成長率。0050 在 2025 年 4 月 7 日起的關稅崩跌中，從 1 月 7 日高點到 4 月 9 日低點共跌 27.49%，需要上漲 37.91% 才能回本，實際只用了 99 天（7 月 17 日收復）；若以過去每年 19.31% 的連續複利成長率等速計算，需要約 1.66 年。歷史上的反彈速度是紀錄，不是保證。

## Return Calculations in Python {lo=7}

Three habits keep return code honest. Store prices and cash flows exactly as delivered and apply corporate actions explicitly. Compute log returns with `log1p`, which stays accurate for tiny returns. Keep every convention, such as the base date, the day count and $D$, in a named variable instead of a hard-coded number.

The first block rebuilds 0050's total-return series from the raw FinMind files and reproduces the case.

```python
import glob
from datetime import date

import numpy as np
import polars as pl

# 1. raw closes (FinMind TaiwanStockPrice) and cash dividends (TaiwanStockDividendResult)
px = (pl.concat([pl.read_csv(f) for f in sorted(glob.glob("data/raw/0050__*.csv"))])
        .sort("date").select("date", "close"))
div = pl.read_csv("data/raw/0050_dividends.csv").select("date", pl.col("cash_dividend").alias("D"))
SPLIT_DATE, SPLIT_RATIO = "2025-06-18", 4.0                     # 4-for-1, effective date

# 2. total return R_t = (P_t + D_t) / (P_{t-1} / k_t) - 1, with k_t the split ratio on the split date
ret = (px.join(div, on="date", how="left")
         .with_columns(pl.col("D").fill_null(0.0),
                       pl.when(pl.col("date") == pl.lit(SPLIT_DATE)).then(pl.lit(SPLIT_RATIO))
                         .otherwise(pl.lit(1.0)).alias("k"))
         .with_columns(((pl.col("close") + pl.col("D")) / (pl.col("close").shift(1) / pl.col("k")) - 1).alias("R"))
         .drop_nulls("R")
         .with_columns(pl.col("R").log1p().alias("r"), pl.col("date").str.to_date()))

# 3. growth factor, annualized and continuously compounded returns, eqs (1.9), (1.10), (1.12)
BASE = date(2014, 10, 31)
G = float(np.exp(ret["r"].sum()))
T = (ret["date"][-1] - BASE).days / 365.25
print(f"G = {G:.4f}  HPR = {G - 1:.2%}  R_G = {G ** (1 / T) - 1:.2%}  g = {np.log(G) / T:.2%}")
# G = 10.0225  HPR = 902.25%  R_G = 21.30%  g = 19.31%
```

The second block computes drawdowns from the running peak and finds the 2025 recovery date of [[eg:recover]].

```python
# 4. drawdown from the running peak, gain needed (1.17) and the first close back at the peak
dd = (ret.select("date", (1 + pl.col("R")).cum_prod().alias("W"))
         .with_columns((pl.col("W") / pl.col("W").cum_max() - 1).alias("DD")))
trough = dd.filter(pl.col("date").is_between(date(2025, 1, 1), date(2025, 6, 30))).sort("DD").row(0, named=True)
peak_W = dd.filter(pl.col("date") <= trough["date"])["W"].max()
back = dd.filter((pl.col("date") > trough["date"]) & (pl.col("W") >= peak_W))["date"][0]
need = -trough["DD"] / (1 + trough["DD"])
print(trough["date"], f"{trough['DD']:.2%}", f"gain needed {need:.2%}", "recovered", back, (back - trough["date"]).days, "days")
# 2025-04-09 -27.49% gain needed 37.91% recovered 2025-07-17 99 days
```

The raw-file series counts 0050's own 2,906 sessions, four more than the aligned series of the daily example. It keeps the four March 2026 sessions on which 00631L did not trade. Growth factors, drawdowns and recovery dates are identical in both.

## Summary {-}

- Growth multiplies and logarithms add: the product rule (1.2) turns products into sums and powers into multiples. Any logarithm is the natural log rescaled, $\log_bx=\ln x/\ln b$; 0050's log growth of 2.3048 equals 3.325 doublings.
- On a log axis equal distances are equal ratios and constant growth is a straight line. Linear axes make early history look flat and recent moves look extreme.
- A stated rate $r^{(n)}$ compounded $n$ times a year has an EAR of $(1+r^{(n)}/n)^n-1$, rising with $n$ toward $e^r-1$. The number $e=\lim(1+1/n)^n=2.71828\ldots$ is one unit compounded continuously at 100% for a year.
- Compounding frequency matters little at deposit rates (1.4 bp at 1.725%, monthly) and a great deal at high rates (118 bp at 15%, daily). Taiwan caps card revolving interest at 15% and forbids compounding it.
- The holding-period return, the annualized return $R_G=G^{1/T}-1$ and the continuously compounded return $g=\ln G/T$ express one growth factor three ways. $R_G$ and $g$ are one rate quoted annually and continuously, $R_G=e^g-1$; for 0050 they were 21.30% and 19.31%.
- Annualizing short-period returns is extrapolation and depends on conventions: a 90-day return of 10.33% annualizes to anything from 39.9% to 50.1%. Taiwan's market traded about 244 days a year in 2015–2025, not 252.
- Log returns add across time and simple returns add across positions. Averaging log returns across positions understates the portfolio; for a 50/50 mix of 0050 and 00631L the error was 615 percentage points.
- Time to goal and required return come from one equation solved with logs. A drawdown $D$ needs a gain of $D/(1-D)$ but only $-\ln(1-D)$ log points. 0050 regained its 27.49% loss of April 2025, which needed +37.91%, in 99 days.
