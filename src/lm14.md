## Introduction

[[LM13]] defined a random variable as a function on a sample space. This module computes with random variables. Its central result, Jensen's inequality, compares the average of a function with the function of the average. For the logarithm, the gap between the two is the Jensen gap, [[LM3]]'s volatility drag measured in log points.

> [!case] Two averages, one market
> The TAIEX total-return index reinvests cash dividends. Over the 23 calendar years 2003–2025 (2003 from its first close, 2 January 2003), it returned 14.97% a year on average. An investor who held it throughout earned 12.35% a year. The average of 2008's −43.07% and 2009's +83.34% is +20.1% a year, yet together the two years returned only +4.4%.
>
> Day by day, through 8 October 2026, the average return was 0.0625%, while the compound daily rate was 0.0552%. Their gap, the volatility drag of [[LM3]], is 0.7320 bp, within 0.002 bp of half the daily variance, 0.7301 bp. Why is the compound rate never above the average? What exactly is the drag? And when does $\sigma^2/2$ describe it?

## Random Variables and Their Distributions {lo=1}

A random variable assigns a number to every outcome of a sample space $\Omega$ ([[LM13]]). Several outcomes can share a value, and the **distribution** of $X$, also called its **law**, collects their probabilities.

> [!example] Two trades as one random variable {#twotrades}
> [[LM4]]'s bet returns +2% or −1% with equal probability; at its Kelly exposure $f=25$, one trade multiplies wealth by 1.5 or 0.75. Two independent trades give [[LM13]]'s sample space $\Omega=\{++,+-,-+,--\}$, each outcome with probability ¼. [[LM13]] tabulated the distribution of $X=W_2/W_0$. Find its distribution function, its 10% quantile and its median.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The outcomes map to 2.25, 1.125, 1.125 and 0.5625 ([[ex:omega]]), so $p_X(1.125)=\tfrac12$ and $p_X(0.5625)=p_X(2.25)=\tfrac14$.
> >
> > The distribution function jumps from ¼ to ¾ at 1.125, so $F_X(x)=\tfrac12$ has no solution. Yet 1.125 is the only median under the definition below, since $\Pr(X\le1.125)=\Pr(X\ge1.125)=\tfrac34$. The 10% quantile, the smallest $x$ with $F_X(x)\ge0.1$, is 0.5625.

### Distribution functions and quantiles

A discrete random variable has a **probability mass function** (pmf) $p_X(x_j):=\Pr(X=x_j)$ on its finitely or countably many values $x_j$ ([[LM13]]). A continuous one has a **density** $f_X$ ([[LM5]]).

> [!definition] Distribution function, quantile function and median
> The **distribution function** of a random variable $X$ is $F_X(x):=\Pr(X\le x)$, (13.11). For a discrete $X$ it is the sum of $p_X(x_j)$ over all $x_j\le x$; for a continuous one, the area under $f_X$ to the left of $x$. For $0<u<1$, the **quantile function** is
> $$F_X^{-1}(u):=\min\{x:F_X(x)\ge u\}.$$
> A value $x$ is a **median** of $X$ if $\Pr(X\le x)\ge\tfrac12$ and $\Pr(X\ge x)\ge\tfrac12$.

The minimum exists because $F_X$ rises from 0 to 1, never decreases and takes the upper value at a jump. When $F_X$ is continuous and strictly increasing, $F_X^{-1}$ is its inverse and a median solves $F_X(x)=\tfrac12$, as in [[LM5]]. In general the medians form a closed interval, possibly a single point.

### A sample as a random variable

A sample becomes a random variable through its **empirical distribution** $\hat F$, (13.12), with mass $1/T$ on each of TAIEX's $T$ returns, so every frequency is a probability. From this volume on, **population** names a property of a random variable or a model ($\mu$, $\sigma$), and **sample** a statistic of data ($\bar R$, $s$, divisor $T$). [[LM3]]'s "population variance" $s^2$ is the variance of $\hat F$.

Quantiles of $\hat F$ are **order statistics**. Sort the returns, which are all distinct, as $R_{(1)}<R_{(2)}<\dots<R_{(T)}$. Because $\hat F(R_{(j)})=j/T$, the smallest order statistic with $j/T\ge u$ is
$$\hat F^{-1}(u)=R_{(\lceil uT\rceil)}.$$

> [!example] TAIEX's daily return as a random variable {#empirical}
> Treat TAIEX's daily returns as a random variable $R$ under $\hat F$. Find $\hat F(0)$, the median and the 1% and 99% quantiles.
>
> > [!solution]
> > No day returned exactly 0, and 2,644 days fell, so $\hat F(0)=2{,}644/5{,}849=0.4520$, the value of [[LM13]]; the other 3,205 days (54.8%) rose. Because $T$ is odd, the median is the 2,925th return, 0.0879%, above the mean of 0.0625%: 3,020 days (51.6%) beat the mean.
> >
> > The lower tail reaches further ([[ex:quantiles]]). The 1% quantile is $\hat F^{-1}(0.01)=-3.567\%$, against $\hat F^{-1}(0.99)=3.125\%$; at 0.1% it is −6.313%, against 5.823% at 99.9%.

> [!pitfall] A median need not solve $F_X(x)=\tfrac12$
> A fair coin paying 0 or 1 has $F_X(x)=\tfrac12$ on all of $[0,1)$, and every $x$ in $[0,1]$ is a median. [[eg:twotrades]] has one median, although $F_X(x)=\tfrac12$ has no solution. Software reports one point by its own rule (Section 8), so state the definition.

> [!kc]
> A fair die shows 1 to 6, each with probability 1/6. Which values are medians, and what is $F_X^{-1}(\tfrac12)$?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > Every $x$ in $[3,4]$ is a median, since then $\Pr(X\le x)\ge\tfrac36$ and $\Pr(X\ge x)\ge\tfrac36$. The quantile function picks the smallest, $F_X^{-1}(\tfrac12)=3$, while `np.median` of the six faces returns the midpoint 3.5.

## Expectation and LOTUS {lo=2}

An expectation is a probability-weighted average of values.

> [!definition] Expectation
> Let $X$ take the values $x_j$, $j=1,\dots,J$ ($J$ finite or infinite), with pmf $p_X$. If $\sum_j\lvert x_j\rvert\,p_X(x_j)<\infty$, the **expectation** $\E[X]$ is the sum below; otherwise it does not exist. The **indicator** $\mathbf 1_{\mathcal A}$ of an event $\mathcal A$ is 1 on $\mathcal A$ and 0 elsewhere, so
> $$\begin{gathered}\E[X]:=\sum_jx_j\,p_X(x_j),\\ \E[\mathbf 1_{\mathcal A}]=\Pr(\mathcal A).\end{gathered} \tag{14.1}$$

For a density, [[LM5]]'s (5.9) is the continuous form, with the same condition. Random variables that are neither discrete nor continuous need a more general integral, which this book does not use. Under $\hat F$, (14.1) gives $\E_{\hat F}[R]=\bar R$.

An expectation can fail to exist: [[LM5]]'s Cauchy density has no mean, and Problem 3 has a discrete case. Why the average of many independent draws approaches the expectation is the law of large numbers of [[LM17]].

> [!theorem] Law of the unconscious statistician (LOTUS)
> Let $X$ be discrete with values $x_j$, and let $h$ be a function with $\sum_j\lvert h(x_j)\rvert\,p_X(x_j)<\infty$. Then
> $$\E[h(X)]=\sum_jh(x_j)\,p_X(x_j). \tag{14.2}$$

LOTUS weights each value of $X$ by its probability and never needs the pmf of $h(X)$. For densities, (5.9) is LOTUS: when $Y=h(X)$ has a density, (5.9) equals $\int y\,f_Y(y)\,dy$, for a continuous $h$ and an integral that exists. This is stated without proof (Grinstead and Snell 1997, §6.3, Theorem 6.11).

> [!deepdive]- LOTUS by grouping
> Here $y_i$, $i=1,2,\dots$, are the distinct values of $Y=h(X)$. Countable additivity ([[LM13]]) gives $\Pr(Y=y_i)=\sum_{j:\,h(x_j)=y_i}p_X(x_j)$. Grouping the terms of (14.2) by the value of $h$,
> $$\begin{aligned}&\sum_jh(x_j)\,p_X(x_j)\\&\quad=\sum_i\,\sum_{j:\,h(x_j)=y_i}y_i\,p_X(x_j)\\&\quad=\sum_iy_i\Pr(Y=y_i),\end{aligned}$$
> which is $\E[Y]$ by (14.1). Regrouping a series is allowed when it converges absolutely, the condition of (14.2), and the same regrouping of the absolute values shows that $\E[Y]$ exists. $\blacksquare$

> [!proposition] Linearity
> Let $X$ and $Y$ be random variables on one sample space whose expectations exist, and let $\eta$ and $\zeta$ be constants. Then
> $$\begin{gathered}\E[\eta X+\zeta]=\eta\,\E[X]+\zeta,\\ \E[X+Y]=\E[X]+\E[Y].\end{gathered} \tag{14.3}$$

On a countable $\Omega$, grouping the outcomes by the value of $X$ gives $\E[X]=\sum_\omega X(\omega)\Pr(\omega)$, and both identities follow by splitting one absolutely convergent sum into two. For densities, $\E[X+Y]=\E[X]+\E[Y]$ is stated without proof (Grinstead and Snell 1997, §6.3, Theorem 6.10). It follows that $\E[X]\le\E[Y]$ when $X\le Y$ with probability one, since outcomes of probability zero add nothing to the sums.

> [!example] Expectation two ways {#lotus}
> For the $X$ of [[eg:twotrades]], compute $\E[X]$ over the outcomes and over the values. Then compare $\E[\tfrac12\ln X]$, by LOTUS, with $\tfrac12\ln\E[X]$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > Over the outcomes, $\tfrac14(2.25+1.125+1.125+0.5625)=1.265625$, and over the values $\tfrac14\times0.5625+\tfrac12\times1.125+\tfrac14\times2.25=1.265625$. That is $1.125^2$, the product of the two trades' expected factors $1+25\times0.5\%$. The expectation of a product of independent factors is the product of the expectations, a rule that [[LM16]] proves. Here the four outcomes give it directly.
> >
> > LOTUS with $h(x)=\tfrac12\ln x$, the log growth per trade, uses [[ex:omega]]'s last column:
> > $$\begin{aligned}&\tfrac14(40.5465\%)+\tfrac12(5.8892\%)\\&\quad+\tfrac14(-28.7682\%)=5.889\%.\end{aligned}$$
> > This average is $\tfrac12\ln1.125$, [[LM4]]'s $g^*$ again. Against it, $\tfrac12\ln\E[X]=\ln1.125=11.778\%$: here the log of the average is twice the average of the logs.

> [!example] The growth function as an expectation {#growth}
> Show that [[LM4]]'s growth function $g(f)=\E[\ln(1+fR)]$, (4.1), is LOTUS, and evaluate it for TAIEX at $f=1$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > With $h(x)=\ln(1+fx)$ and $R\in\{b,-a\}$, (14.2) gives (4.7), $p\ln(1+fb)+q\ln(1-fa)$. Under $\hat F$ it gives (4.4), $\bar g(f)=\E_{\hat F}[\ln(1+fR)]$.

> [!pitfall] The expectation of a function is not the function of the expectation
> For TAIEX, $g=\bar r$ is not $\ln(1+\bar R)$, which is larger by 0.7316 bp a day (Section 7), and [[eg:lotus]] shows a factor of two. By (14.3) the two agree for every $X$ when $h(x)=\eta x+\zeta$; two-point laws show that no other $h$ does so.

> [!zh] 隨機變數、分位數與期望值
> 隨機變數（random variable）是樣本空間（sample space）上的函數：離散時用機率質量函數（probability mass function），連續時用密度（density），兩者共用同一個累積分布函數（distribution function）$F_X(x)=\Pr(X\le x)$。分位數函數（quantile function）取「使 $F_X(x)\ge u$ 的最小 $x$」，所以一定存在；中位數（median）卻可能是一整段區間，也可能根本解不出 $F_X(x)=\tfrac12$。把加權股價報酬指數 5,849 個日報酬各給 $1/T$ 的機率，就得到經驗分布（empirical distribution），樣本的頻率就是這個模型的機率。樣本平均數（sample mean）就是經驗分布的期望值（expectation）；[[LM3]] 所說的母體變異數（population variance）$s^2$，也就是經驗分布的變異數。LOTUS 讓我們不必先求 $h(X)$ 的分布（distribution），直接用 $X$ 的機率加權 $h(x)$，但函數的期望值一般不等於期望值的函數，$g=\E[\ln(1+R)]$ 並不等於 $\ln(1+\mu)$。

## Variance and Higher Moments {lo=3}

The **variance** of $X$ is $\Var(X):=\E[(X-\mu_X)^2]$, where $\mu_X=\E[X]$, and $\sigma_X:=\sqrt{\Var(X)}$ is its **standard deviation**.

> [!proposition] Second moment and variance
> If $\E[X^2]<\infty$, then $\E[X]$ exists, and for constants $\eta$ and $\zeta$
> $$\begin{gathered}\E[X^2]=\sigma_X^2+\mu_X^2,\\ \Var(\eta X+\zeta)=\eta^2\Var(X).\end{gathered} \tag{14.4}$$

The mean exists because $\lvert x\rvert\le1+x^2$ for every $x$. Expanding the square with (14.3) gives $\E[(X-\mu_X)^2]=\E[X^2]-2\mu_X\E[X]+\mu_X^2=\E[X^2]-\mu_X^2$. For the second identity, $\eta X+\zeta$ has mean $\eta\mu_X+\zeta$, so its deviation from the mean is $\eta(X-\mu_X)$.

Under $\hat F$, (14.4) is [[LM3]]'s identity (3.7), $\frac1T\sum_tR_t^2=s^2+\bar R^2$; in a model it is [[LM4]]'s $\E[R^2]=\sigma^2+\mu^2$. The variance of a sum needs the covariance of its terms ([[LM16]]). By (14.4), $(X-\mu_X)/\sigma_X$ has mean 0 and variance 1, as [[LM5]]'s (5.5) used for the normal. Since $x^2\le1+x^4$ and $\lvert x\rvert^3\le1+x^4$, a finite fourth moment makes every lower moment finite.

> [!definition] Skewness and kurtosis
> Let $\E[X^4]<\infty$ and $\sigma_X>0$. The **skewness** and the **kurtosis** of $X$ are its third and fourth standardized moments,
> $$\begin{gathered}\operatorname{Skew}(X):=\frac{\E[(X-\mu_X)^3]}{\sigma_X^3},\\ \operatorname{Kurt}(X):=\frac{\E[(X-\mu_X)^4]}{\sigma_X^4},\end{gathered} \tag{14.5}$$
> and the **excess kurtosis** is $\operatorname{Kurt}(X)-3$. The skewness needs only $\E\lvert X\rvert^3<\infty$.

Both are unchanged when $X$ becomes $\eta X+\zeta$ with $\eta>0$, because the deviation and $\sigma_X$ scale by the same $\eta$. Applied to a sample, (14.5) gives the sample skewness and kurtosis. The choice of $T$ or $T-1$, and how far such numbers move between samples, are questions for [[LM19]] and [[LM20]].

> [!example] Moments of the +2%/−1% bet {#betmoments}
> Compute the moments of [[LM4]]'s bet, +2% or −1% with equal probability, up to the kurtosis.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The mean is $\mu=0.5\%$, and the deviations are ±1.5%, so $\sigma=1.5\%$ and $\E[R^2]=\tfrac12(0.02^2+0.01^2)=0.00025=\sigma^2+\mu^2$. The raw third moment is $\tfrac12(0.02^3-0.01^3)=3.5\times10^{-6}$, which equals $\mu^3+3\mu\sigma^2$, as in [[LM4]]'s Practice Problem 13.
> >
> > The deviations are symmetric, so the skewness is 0, although the raw third moment is positive. Every squared deviation equals $\sigma^2$, so the kurtosis is 1, an excess kurtosis of −2.

### The normal's moments

For a standard normal $Z$ the odd moments vanish by symmetry, and $\E[Z^2]=1$ ([[LM5]]). Integration by parts, (5.6), moves one factor $z$ onto $\varphi$, since $z\varphi(z)$ is the derivative of $-\varphi(z)$:
$$\begin{aligned}\E[Z^4]&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^3\cdot z\varphi(z)\,dz\\&=\big[-z^3\varphi(z)\big]_{-\infty}^{\infty}+3\,\E[Z^2]=3.\end{aligned}$$
Hence $\Var(Z^2)=\E[Z^4]-(\E[Z^2])^2=2$, the value [[LM3]] used without proof. For a normal $X=\mu+\sigma Z$, expanding the powers of $\mu+\sigma Z$ and dropping the odd moments of $Z$ gives
$$\begin{gathered}\E[Z^3]=0,\quad\E[Z^4]=3,\quad\Var(Z^2)=2,\\ \E[X^3]=\mu^3+3\mu\sigma^2,\\ \E[X^4]=\mu^4+6\mu^2\sigma^2+3\sigma^4.\end{gathered} \tag{14.6}$$
So the normal has skewness 0 and kurtosis 3, which [[LM5]] stated without proof. Excess kurtosis measures tail weight against this benchmark.

> > [!solution]
> > Under $\hat F$, (14.5) gives a skewness of −0.34 and a kurtosis of 8.27, an excess kurtosis of 5.27 ([[ex:moments]]). ## Tail Bounds: Markov, Chebyshev and Cantelli {lo=4}

How often can a random variable stray far from its mean, if only its mean or its variance is known? Three inequalities answer without a model.

> [!theorem] Markov's inequality
> If $X\ge0$ with probability one and $x>0$, then
> $$\Pr(X\ge x)\le\frac{\E[X]}{x}. \tag{14.7}$$

*Proof.* With probability one, $x\mathbf 1\{X\ge x\}\le X$: the left side is $x$ when $X\ge x$ and 0 otherwise. Taking expectations with (14.1) and monotonicity gives $x\Pr(X\ge x)\le\E[X]$. $\blacksquare$

> [!theorem] Chebyshev's and Cantelli's inequalities
> Let $X$ have a finite variance with $\sigma_X>0$. For $k>0$,
> $$\Pr(\lvert X-\mu_X\rvert\ge k\sigma_X)\le\frac1{k^2}, \tag{14.8}$$
> and for $k\ge0$ the lower tail alone satisfies
> $$\Pr(X-\mu_X\le-k\sigma_X)\le\frac1{1+k^2}. \tag{14.9}$$

Chebyshev's (14.8) is Markov's inequality for $(X-\mu_X)^2$ at the level $k^2\sigma_X^2$, since $\E[(X-\mu_X)^2]=\sigma_X^2$. Cantelli's one-sided (14.9) needs a shift (Deep Dive below). Simple laws attain both bounds, so neither can be improved for every law.

For $k\le1$ Chebyshev's bound says nothing. Without a finite variance, as for the Pareto law of Problem 7, neither bound applies. Under $\hat F$, with $\mu=\bar R$ and $\sigma=s$ (divisor $T$), every bound holds exactly, because $\hat F$ is a probability law with these moments.

> [!deepdive]- Cantelli's inequality, and the laws that attain the bounds
> Here $t>0$ is a shift, not a date. If $X-\mu_X\le-k\sigma_X$, then $\mu_X-X+t\ge k\sigma_X+t>0$, so
> $$\begin{gathered}\{X-\mu_X\le-k\sigma_X\}\\ \subseteq\{(\mu_X-X+t)^2\ge(k\sigma_X+t)^2\}.\end{gathered}$$
> The variable $\mu_X-X+t$ has mean $t$ and variance $\sigma_X^2$, so its square has expectation $\sigma_X^2+t^2$ by (14.4). Markov's (14.7) then gives
> $$\Pr(X-\mu_X\le-k\sigma_X)\le\frac{\sigma_X^2+t^2}{(k\sigma_X+t)^2}.$$
> For $k>0$ the derivative in $t$ vanishes where $tk\sigma_X=\sigma_X^2$, so the bound is smallest at $t=\sigma_X/k$, where it equals $1/(1+k^2)$. At $k=0$ the bound 1 is trivial.
>
> The law with $\mu_X-k\sigma_X$ with probability $1/(1+k^2)$ and $\mu_X+\sigma_X/k$ otherwise has mean $\mu_X$ and variance $\sigma_X^2$, and it attains (14.9). For $k\ge1$, the law with $\mu_X\pm k\sigma_X$ with probability $1/(2k^2)$ each and $\mu_X$ otherwise attains (14.8). $\blacksquare$

## Jensen's Inequality {lo=5}

For a two-point return the logarithm's concavity is visible: the chord between the two outcomes on the curve $\ln(1+x)$ lies below the curve. Above the mean $\mu$, the chord gives the average of the logs, $g$, and the curve the log of the average, $\ln(1+\mu)$. The widget starts from 2008 and 2009 with equal probability: $\mu=20.1\%$, but $g=2.141\%$ against $\ln(1+\mu)=18.345\%$.

<div class="widget" data-widget="jensen"><div data-chart="lm14-jensen"></div></div>

> [!theorem] Jensen's inequality
> Let $h$ be concave and differentiable on an open interval $\mathcal I$, and let $X$ be a random variable with
> - values in $\mathcal I$ with probability one,
> - finite expectations $\E\lvert X\rvert$ and $\E\lvert h(X)\rvert$.
>
> Then
> $$\E[h(X)]\le h(\E[X]). \tag{14.10}$$
> If $h$ is strictly concave, equality holds only when $\Pr(X=\mu_X)=1$. For a convex $h$ both inequalities reverse.

*Proof.* The mean $\mu_X$ lies in $\mathcal I$. Indeed, $X$ lies in the open interval $\mathcal I$ with probability one, so its distance to a finite end is positive with probability one. By (14.3), this distance has mean $\mu_X$ minus a lower end, or an upper end minus $\mu_X$, at least 0 by monotonicity. It is not 0: if a variable $Y>0$ with probability one had mean 0, Markov's (14.7) would give $\Pr(Y\ge x)=0$ for every $x>0$. Countable additivity would then give $\Pr(Y>0)=0$.

By [[LM4]]'s Deep Dive on tangent lines, the concave $h$ lies below its tangent at $\mu_X$: for every $x\in\mathcal I$,
$$h(x)\le h(\mu_X)+h'(\mu_X)(x-\mu_X).$$
Put $x=X$, which lies in $\mathcal I$ with probability one, and take expectations with (14.3) and monotonicity: $\E[h(X)]\le h(\mu_X)+h'(\mu_X)(\E[X]-\mu_X)=h(\mu_X)$. $\blacksquare$

The proof needs only the tangent line, which covers every $h$ in this book. For a convex $h$, apply (14.10) to $-h$: with $h(x)=e^x$ it gives $\E[e^X]\ge e^{\E X}$, and [[LM5]]'s (5.12) measures the gap for a normal $X$, $\E[e^X]=e^{\mu_X+\sigma_X^2/2}$.

> [!deepdive]- The strict form
> Here $x_{\text{mid}}$ is a midpoint and $\varepsilon$ a small positive number. Let $h$ be strictly concave and differentiable on $\mathcal I$, and let $x\ne\mu_X$ lie in $\mathcal I$, with $x_{\text{mid}}:=(x+\mu_X)/2$. Strict concavity puts $h(x_{\text{mid}})$ strictly above the chord, and the tangent at $\mu_X$ lies above $h$ at $x_{\text{mid}}$:
> $$\begin{aligned}&\tfrac12h(x)+\tfrac12h(\mu_X)<h(x_{\text{mid}})\\&\quad\le h(\mu_X)+h'(\mu_X)\,\frac{x-\mu_X}2.\end{aligned}$$
> Doubling and subtracting $h(\mu_X)$ gives the strict tangent inequality $h(x)<h(\mu_X)+h'(\mu_X)(x-\mu_X)$ for every $x\ne\mu_X$.
>
> So the tangent gap $h(\mu_X)+h'(\mu_X)(X-\mu_X)-h(X)$ is non-negative, positive when $X\ne\mu_X$, and has expectation $h(\mu_X)-\E[h(X)]$. If $\Pr(X\ne\mu_X)>0$, countable additivity gives some $\varepsilon>0$ for which the gap is at least $\varepsilon$ with positive probability. Markov's (14.7) then makes its expectation at least $\varepsilon$ times that probability, so (14.10) is strict. $\blacksquare$

> [!proposition] Weighted AM–GM inequality
> Let $x_1,\dots,x_J>0$, and let the weights satisfy $w_j\ge0$ and $\sum_jw_j=1$. Then
> $$\sum_jw_jx_j\ge\prod_jx_j^{w_j}, \tag{14.11}$$
> with equality if and only if all $x_j$ with $w_j>0$ are equal.

Let $X$ take the value $x_j$ with probability $w_j$: the weights qualify as probabilities because they are non-negative and sum to 1. The logarithm is strictly concave on $\mathcal I=(0,\infty)$, so (14.10) gives $\sum_jw_j\ln x_j\le\ln\sum_jw_jx_j$; exponentiate. By the strict form, equality needs equal $x_j$ wherever $w_j>0$.

With $w_j=1/J$ this is the strict unweighted inequality that [[LM1]] used for its compounding sequence. With $x_j=e^{r_j}$ and logs, (14.11) is [[LM1]]'s (1.14) for a long-only portfolio.

> [!example] The case's first question {#amgm}
> Apply (14.11) to [[ex:case]]'s 23 calendar-year growth factors with equal weights, and to 2008 and 2009 alone.
>
> > [!solution]
> > With $w_j=1/23$, the left side is one plus the arithmetic mean, 1.1497, and the right side one plus the compound rate, 1.1235. The compound rate cannot exceed the average, and with unequal factors it lies strictly below.
> >
> > For 2008 and 2009 alone, $\tfrac12(0.5693+1.8334)=1.20135$, while $\sqrt{0.5693\times1.8334}=1.02164$. The pair's compound rate is 2.16% a year against an average of 20.1%.

> [!pitfall] Check the direction and the weights
> Jensen's inequality reverses for a convex $h$, so check the sign of $h''$ first ([[LM4]]; Simon and Blume 1994, chapter 21). Its weights must be probabilities. [[LM1]]'s weights $(1.5,-0.5)$, a short position, are not, and (1.14) fails for them; Problem 8 shows where the proof breaks.

> [!zh] Jensen 不等式：切線的期望值
> 凹函數（concave function）的圖形在每一條切線（tangent line）的下方。在平均數（mean）$\mu_X$ 取切線，代入 $X$ 再取期望值（expectation），切線部分的期望值剛好等於 $h(\mu_X)$，於是得到 $\E[h(X)]\le h(\E X)$，這就是 Jensen 不等式（Jensen's inequality）；凸函數（convex function）的方向相反。條件要記清楚：$X$ 必須以機率 1 落在 $h$ 的開區間（open interval）內，而且兩個期望值都存在。把權重看成機率、取 $h=\ln$，就得到加權算幾不等式（weighted AM–GM inequality）。權重必須非負且總和為 1，放空造成的負權重不是機率，不等式就可能不成立。

> [!kc]
> Apply (14.10) to the convex function $h(x)=x^2$. What inequality results, and what is the difference between its two sides?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > For a convex $h$ the inequality reverses: $\E[X^2]\ge(\E X)^2$. By (14.4) the difference is $\E[X^2]-\mu_X^2=\Var(X)$, which is zero only for a constant $X$.

## Volatility Drag and the Jensen Gap {lo=6}

Apply Jensen's inequality to the growth factor $1+R$, with the logarithm as $h$.

> [!result] The compound rate never exceeds the mean
> Let $R>-1$ with probability one, with $\E\lvert R\rvert$ and $\E\lvert\ln(1+R)\rvert$ finite. Then
> $$g\le\ln(1+\mu),\qquad\text{so}\qquad R_G\le\mu, \tag{14.12}$$
> with equality if and only if $\Pr(R=\mu)=1$.

Under $\hat F$, $g=\bar r$ and $R_G$ is [[LM3]]'s geometric mean return, so for any returns the compound rate never exceeds the average. The condition $R>-1$ matters: a normal $R$ puts positive probability below −1, where $\ln(1+R)$ is undefined, so $\E[\ln(1+R)]$ does not exist and SciPy returns `nan`. [[LM4]]'s quadratic approximation (4.2) hides this; a lognormal model, with $\ln(1+R)$ normal, keeps $R>-1$.

### Two names for the gap

The **volatility drag** is [[LM3]]'s $\mu-R_G$ ($\bar R-R_G$ in a sample), in percentage points; the **Jensen gap** is $\ln(1+\mu)-g$, in log points. Since $1+R_G=e^g$,
$$\mu-R_G=(1+\mu)\big(1-e^{-(\ln(1+\mu)-g)}\big),$$
so the drag is about $1+\mu$ times the Jensen gap when both are small.

The Jensen gap has an exact series. Suppose that $R$ takes finitely many values, each with $\lvert R-\mu\rvert<1+\mu$, and write
$$\ln(1+R)=\ln(1+\mu)+\ln\Big(1+\frac{R-\mu}{1+\mu}\Big).$$
[[LM3]]'s series (3.3) converges at every value of the last term, and taking expectations term by term, with $\E[R-\mu]=0$, gives
$$\begin{aligned}\ln(1+\mu)-g&=\frac{\sigma^2}{2(1+\mu)^2}-\frac{\E[(R-\mu)^3]}{3(1+\mu)^3}\\&\quad+\frac{\E[(R-\mu)^4]}{4(1+\mu)^4}-\cdots\end{aligned} \tag{14.13}$$
The third-order term is $-\operatorname{Skew}\cdot\sigma^3/(3(1+\mu)^3)$, so negative skewness widens the gap. To second order in the returns, the factor $1/(1+\mu)^2$ is 1 and the gap is $\sigma^2/2$, the drag of [[LM3]]'s (3.8) and (3.9).

For a lognormal law the gap needs no series. If $\ln(1+R)$ is normal with mean $g$ and standard deviation $\sigma_r$, [[LM5]]'s (5.12) gives $1+\mu=\E[e^{\ln(1+R)}]=e^{g+\sigma_r^2/2}$. The Jensen gap is then exactly $\sigma_r^2/2$, half the variance of the log return rather than of $R$.

> [!zh] 波動拖累的真正原因
> 對 $1+R$ 套用 Jensen 不等式（Jensen's inequality），取 $h=\ln$，對數報酬（log return）的期望值（expectation）$g$ 就不超過 $\ln(1+\mu)$，所以複利報酬率（compound rate）$R_G$ 永遠不超過算術平均（arithmetic mean）$\mu$。兩者的差距有兩種量法：波動拖累（volatility drag）$\mu-R_G$ 以百分點計，Jensen 差距（Jensen gap）$\ln(1+\mu)-g$ 以對數點（log points）計。把對數展開成級數（series），Jensen 差距的二階項是 $\sigma^2/(2(1+\mu)^2)$，三階項由偏態（skewness）決定，四階項由峰態（kurtosis）決定，[[LM3]] 的 $\sigma^2/2$ 只是二階近似。日報酬時二階項幾乎完全精準，年報酬或 2008、2009 年這種大波動時，高階項就不能忽略。若對數報酬服從常態分布（normal distribution），也就是對數常態分布（lognormal distribution）模型，Jensen 差距恰好等於對數報酬變異數（variance）的一半 $\sigma_r^2/2$。

## Random Variables and Expectations in Python {lo=7}

A NumPy array of returns is its empirical distribution, provided each call uses the right convention ([[ex:python]]).

> [!exhibit] NumPy and SciPy calls for distributions and moments {#python}
> | Call | Returns | Convention |
> |:--|:--|:--|
> | `np.mean(R)` | $\bar R=\E_{\hat F}[R]$ | |
> | `np.var(R)`, `np.std(R)` | $s^2$, $s$ | divisor $T$ (`ddof=0`, the default) |
> | `np.quantile(R, u, method="inverted_cdf")` | $\hat F^{-1}(u)=R_{(\lceil uT\rceil)}$ | the quantile function of Section 2 |
> | `np.quantile(R, u)` | interpolates between order statistics | default `"linear"`: not $\hat F^{-1}$ |
> | `np.median(x)` | the middle value, or the midpoint of the middle pair | a fair die: 3.5 |
> | `stats.rv_discrete(values=(xk, pk))` | a discrete law with integer values `xk` | `.median()` and `.ppf(0.5)` of a die: 3 |
> | `stats.skew(R)`, `stats.kurtosis(R)` | skewness; excess kurtosis | divisor $T$ (`bias=True`); add 3 for the kurtosis |
> | `stats.norm(m, sd).moment(4)` | the raw moment $\E[X^4]$ | (14.6) |
> | `stats.norm(m, sd).expect(np.log1p)` | $\E[\ln(1+X)]$, by numerical integration | `nan`: a normal puts mass below −1 |
>
> Note: `stats` is `scipy.stats`; `m` and `sd` are the normal's mean and standard deviation. Checked with NumPy 2.5.3 and SciPy 1.18.1, the versions in `uv.lock`.

Every count against a threshold uses one guard, `R <= c + 1e-12` for $R\le c$ and `R >= c - 1e-12` for $R\ge c$. Index levels have two decimals, so a round return can be an exact tie that floating-point division puts on either side of the threshold. At −3% the guard and an exact count with fractions both give 108.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# R: TAIEX's 5,849 daily total returns as a NumPy array
Rbar, s = R.mean(), R.std()                     # divisor T
q = np.quantile(R, 0.001, method="inverted_cdf")  # R_(6), -6.313%
z = (R - Rbar) / s
lower4 = int((z <= -4 + 1e-12).sum())          # 17 days, with the guard
excess = stats.kurtosis(R)                      # 5.27, excess kurtosis
gap = np.log1p(Rbar) - np.log1p(R).mean()      # Jensen gap, 0.7316 bp
```

> [!pitfall] `np.quantile`'s default interpolates
> By default, `np.quantile` interpolates between order statistics and can return a value that is no day's return. At $u=0.001$ it gives −5.866%, against $\hat F^{-1}(0.001)=-6.313\%$: the interpolation pulls a far-tail quantile towards the centre. Pass `method="inverted_cdf"` for the quantile function, and state the method with every quantile.

## Summary {-}

- A random variable maps outcomes to numbers. One distribution function serves discrete and continuous laws, the quantile function always exists, and the medians form a closed interval.
- Under its empirical distribution $\hat F$, a sample's frequencies are probabilities, $\hat F^{-1}(u)=R_{(\lceil uT\rceil)}$, and its divisor-$T$ statistics are population moments.
- Expectation (14.1) needs absolute convergence; LOTUS (14.2) and linearity (14.3) compute it.
- The identity $\E[X^2]=\sigma_X^2+\mu_X^2$, (14.4), is (3.7) for any law; a normal has $\E[Z^4]=3$, (14.6). TAIEX has skewness −0.34 and excess kurtosis 5.27.
- The bounds (14.7)–(14.9) hold exactly but loosely: 17 TAIEX days at $k=4$ against 344.1.
- Jensen's inequality (14.10) follows from one tangent line and gives (14.11) and $R_G\le\mu$, (14.12).
- The Jensen gap $\ln(1+\mu)-g$ has the exact series (14.13). To second order in the returns, its leading term is [[LM3]]'s $\sigma^2/2$: accurate for daily returns, not for annual ones. For a lognormal law the gap is $\sigma_r^2/2$.

2. The distribution function is 1/16, 5/16, 11/16, 15/16 and 1 at the five values. By (14.1), the binomial theorem gives $\E[G]=((1.02+0.99)/2)^4=1.005^4=1.02015$ ([[LM16]] gives the product rule behind it).    The standardized values are $+\tfrac13$ (probability 0.9) and $-3$, so the skewness is $0.9/27-0.1\times27=-8/3=-2.67$ and the kurtosis $0.9/81+0.1\times81=73/9=8.11$, an excess of 5.11. At $f=8$ the return is $8R$, with $\mu=3.2\%$, $\sigma=14.4\%$, $\E[(8R)^2]=64\times3.4\times10^{-4}=0.02176$, which is $0.144^2+0.032^2$, and $\E[(8R)^3]=512\times(-1.16\times10^{-5})=-0.0059392$. The standardized values do not change, so by (14.5) neither do the skewness and kurtosis.

   The excess kurtosis is close to TAIEX's 5.27, yet this law has no tail: kurtosis measures how much of the variance comes from rare large deviations, here one rare loss. One number cannot tell a fat tail from a rare jump.

5. Moving one factor $z$ onto $\varphi$ as in Section 4, $\E[Z^6]=\big[-z^5\varphi(z)\big]_{-\infty}^{\infty}+5\E[Z^4]=15$, and $\Var(Z^3)=\E[Z^6]-0^2=15$. 8. Let $X$ take the value $x_j$ with probability $w_j$, which needs non-negative weights summing to 1. Positive $x_j$ put $X$ in $\mathcal I=(0,\infty)$, where the logarithm is strictly concave and differentiable; finitely many values make both expectations finite. Then (14.10) gives $\sum_jw_j\ln x_j\le\ln\sum_jw_jx_j$; exponentiate, and use the strict form for equality.

   With weights $(1.5,-0.5)$ one probability would be −0.5, so no such $X$ exists. Indeed, $1.5e^{0.10}-0.5e^{0.20}=1.0471$, whose logarithm, 0.0460, lies below 0.0500.

## Glossary {-}

| Term | 中文 | Meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Random variable | 隨機變數 | A function that assigns a number to each outcome of a sample space. |
| Distribution (law) | 分布 | The probabilities with which a random variable takes its values, given by $p_X$ or $f_X$ and by $F_X$. |
| Probability mass function | 機率質量函數 | $p_X(x_j)=\Pr(X=x_j)$ for a discrete $X$. |
| Distribution function | 累積分布函數 | $F_X(x)=\Pr(X\le x)$, for discrete and continuous $X$ alike. |
| Quantile function | 分位數函數 | $F_X^{-1}(u)=\min\{x:F_X(x)\ge u\}$, $0<u<1$. |
| Median | 中位數 | A value $x$ with $\Pr(X\le x)\ge\tfrac12$ and $\Pr(X\ge x)\ge\tfrac12$; the medians form a closed interval. |
| Empirical distribution | 經驗分布 | $\hat F$: mass $1/T$ on each observation of a sample. |
| Order statistic | 順序統計量 | $R_{(j)}$, the observation of rank $j$ from the smallest; $\hat F^{-1}(u)=R_{(\lceil uT\rceil)}$. |
| Population; sample | 母體；樣本 | A property of a random variable or model; a statistic of data, with its divisor stated. |
| Expectation | 期望值 | $\E[X]=\sum_jx_jp_X(x_j)$, (14.1), or (5.9) for a density, when it converges absolutely. |
| Indicator | 指示函數 | $\mathbf 1_{\mathcal A}$: 1 on the event $\mathcal A$, 0 elsewhere; $\E[\mathbf 1_{\mathcal A}]=\Pr(\mathcal A)$. |
| LOTUS | LOTUS | $\E[h(X)]=\sum_jh(x_j)p_X(x_j)$, (14.2). |
| Variance | 變異數 | $\Var(X)=\E[(X-\mu_X)^2]=\E[X^2]-\mu_X^2$, (14.4). |
| Skewness; kurtosis | 偏態；峰態 | Third and fourth standardized moments, (14.5). |
| Excess kurtosis | 超額峰態 | Kurtosis minus 3, the normal's value. |
| Markov's inequality | 馬可夫不等式 | $\Pr(X\ge x)\le\E[X]/x$ for $X\ge0$ and $x>0$, (14.7). |
| Chebyshev's inequality | 謝比雪夫不等式 | $\Pr(\lvert X-\mu_X\rvert\ge k\sigma_X)\le1/k^2$, (14.8). |
| Cantelli's inequality | Cantelli 不等式 | $\Pr(X-\mu_X\le-k\sigma_X)\le1/(1+k^2)$, (14.9). |
| Jensen's inequality | Jensen 不等式 | $\E[h(X)]\le h(\E X)$ for a concave $h$, (14.10); reversed for a convex $h$. |
| Weighted AM–GM inequality | 加權算幾不等式 | $\sum_jw_jx_j\ge\prod_jx_j^{w_j}$ for $x_j>0$, $w_j\ge0$ and $\sum_jw_j=1$, (14.11). |
| Compound rate | 複利報酬率 | $R_G=e^g-1$, the constant rate with the same growth, [[LM3]]'s geometric mean return (幾何平均報酬); $R_G\le\mu$, (14.12). |
| Volatility drag | 波動拖累 | $\mu-R_G$ ($\bar R-R_G$ in a sample), in percentage points. |
