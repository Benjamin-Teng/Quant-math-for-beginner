## Introduction

Volume III treated returns as random variables; this module describes them as data. Each statistic of a return series is a recipe: which returns, which divisor, which quantile rule, which period. The case shows how far one annual figure moves with the recipe.

> [!case] How volatile is a year of TAIEX?
> Take the TAIEX total-return index, with dividends reinvested and no costs, from 3 January 2003 to 31 December 2025: 5,664 daily log returns. Their standard deviation is 1.18352% a day ([[LM17]]). A year has about 246 sessions, so independent days would give a yearly standard deviation of $\sqrt{246}\times1.18352\%=18.56\%$. Weekly and monthly log returns, scaled to a year the same way, give 18.09% and 18.12% ([[ex:case]]).
>
> Yet the 23 calendar-year log returns have a standard deviation of 22.21% ([[LM15]]). To reach it, the variance of a year's sum would have to be 1.43 times the sum of its daily variances. Do daily variances fail to add up over a year? Or do 23 numbers pin down a standard deviation too loosely to tell?

> [!note] Notation for this module
> TR23 is the TAIEX total-return index: $T=5{,}849$ daily returns (a count), 3 January 2003 to 8 October 2026, over 23.7645 years from the first close, so $A=246.12$. Simple returns are $R_t$, log returns $r_t=\ln(1+R_t)$. Sample moments $\bar R$, $s$, $\bar r$, $s_r$ divide by $T$ and $s_{\mathrm u}$ by $T-1$, (19.1); model moments are $\mu$, $\sigma$, $g$, $\sigma_r$. Quantiles are $\hat F^{-1}(u)=R_{(\lceil uT\rceil)}$ and $\operatorname{med}:=\hat F^{-1}(\tfrac12)$ ([[LM14]]); [[LM5]]'s $\hat\mu$ is $\bar R$.
>
> Local: $j_0$ the returns trimmed at each end; $\operatorname{IQR}$, $\operatorname{MAD}$ the interquartile range and the median absolute deviation; $\widehat{\operatorname{Skew}}$, $\widehat{\operatorname{Kurt}}$ the sample skewness and kurtosis, with subscript adj for the adjusted versions; $\operatorname{Skew}_{\mathrm Q}$, $\operatorname{Kurt}_{\mathrm O}$ the quartile skewness and octile kurtosis; $x_t$, $\bar x$ a series ($r_t$, $\lvert r_t\rvert$ or $r_t^2$) and its mean; $\gamma_k$, $\rho_k$ and $\hat\gamma_k$, $\hat\rho_k$ the autocovariance and autocorrelation at lag $k$, model and sample; $r_t^{(m)}:=r_{t-m+1}+\dots+r_t$ the log return over $m$ days; $\operatorname{VR}(m)$, $\operatorname{VR}_{\mathrm n}(m)$ the overlapping and non-overlapping variance ratios, (19.8), and $o$ the returns skipped before the first block; $\mathrm{SR}$ a Sharpe ratio with $r_f=0$, and $\mathrm{SR}(m)$ that of sums of $m$ days. Counts: $j$ a rank, $k$ a lag, $m$ the days in a sum. The model's ratio in (19.7) gets no symbol, so that $\operatorname{VR}(m)$ keeps one meaning.

> [!note] Research hygiene
> Every TAIEX figure describes TR23, unless a caption names a limit regime or 2003–2025 ([[LM15]]'s and [[LM17]]'s calendar years). The figures are **descriptive**, without standard errors, which are [[LM20]]'s subject. The normal law with the sample's own mean and standard deviation is **in-sample**. The iid band and expectation are **model** properties, and the extra −10% day is **hypothetical**. The limit regimes, and 2003–2025 against TR23, are **stability checks**. Nothing is tested ([[LM21]], [[LM25]]).

## Where Returns Centre {lo=1}

A centre is one number that stands for a whole series. Returns have several, and in a week of extreme days they disagree.

> [!example] The tariff week {#week}
> TR23's returns on 7–11 April 2025, the week after the United States announced new tariffs (The White House 2025), were −9.6997%, −4.0161%, −5.7865%, +9.2512% and +2.7842%. With a standard deviation of 6.72%, this was the most volatile of TR23's 1,224 ISO weeks. Find the week's mean, median, 20% trimmed mean, compound daily rate and mean log return.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The sum is −7.4669%, so the mean is −1.493%. Sorted, the returns are −9.6997%, −5.7865%, −4.0161%, +2.7842% and +9.2512%, so the median is the third, −4.016%. The 20% trimmed mean drops one return at each end and averages the middle three: −2.339%.
> >
> > The growth factor is $\prod_t(1+R_t)=0.91697$, a loss of 8.303%. By (1.9) in days, $R_G=0.91697^{1/5}-1=-1.719\%$, and by (1.12) the mean log return is $\bar r=\tfrac15\ln0.91697=-1.734\%$. Every centre answers a different question.

> [!definition] Mean, median and trimmed mean
> Let $R_{(1)}<\dots<R_{(T)}$ be the sorted returns. The **sample mean** is $\bar R=\frac1T\sum_tR_t$, and the **sample median** is $\operatorname{med}:=\hat F^{-1}(\tfrac12)=R_{(\lceil T/2\rceil)}$ ([[LM14]]). For a count $0\le j_0<T/2$, the **trimmed mean** drops the $j_0$ smallest and the $j_0$ largest returns and averages the rest:
> $$\frac1{T-2j_0}\sum_{j=j_0+1}^{T-j_0}R_{(j)}.$$
> The **5% trimmed mean** takes $j_0=\lfloor0.05T\rfloor$.

A statistic is **robust** when a few observations cannot move it far. Raising the largest return by any amount moves the mean without limit, but leaves the median and a trimmed mean unchanged.

Two more centres concern growth. By (1.12), $\ln G=\sum_tr_t=T\bar r$, so the compound daily rate of (1.9) is $R_G=G^{1/T}-1=e^{\bar r}-1$, slightly above $\bar r$. Under $\hat F$, (14.12) gives $R_G\le\bar R$, and by (3.9) the gap is about $s^2/2$, the volatility drag of [[LM3]].

> [!example] Five centres of TAIEX's day {#centres}
> Compute TR23's mean, median, 5% trimmed mean, compound daily rate and mean log return, and explain their order.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The mean is $\bar R=0.062505\%$, or 0.0625%, and the median is the 2,925th return, 0.0879% ([[LM14]]). The 5% trimmed mean drops $j_0=\lfloor0.05\times5{,}849\rfloor=292$ returns at each end and gives 0.0832%. For growth, $\bar r=0.055170\%$ and $R_G=e^{\bar r}-1=0.055185\%$ ([[LM14]]). [[ex:summary]] lists the first three.
> >
> > The median and the trimmed mean sit above the mean because the largest days are falls (Section 4). The compound rate sits below the mean by the drag: $0.062505\%-0.055185\%=0.7320$ bp, against $s^2/2=0.7301$ bp with $s=1.2084\%$.

> [!pitfall] A robust centre answers another question
> On a skewed law the median and a trimmed mean differ from the mean even without sampling noise, because they describe other points of the distribution. Growth and the Kelly fraction ([[LM4]]) depend on the mean. Use a robust centre to describe the typical day, not to replace $\bar R$ in a formula.

> [!kc]
> In [[eg:week]], lower the first return by 10 points, from −9.6997% to −19.6997%. What happens to the mean, the median and the 20% trimmed mean?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > The mean falls by 10/5 = 2 points, to −3.493%. The changed return is still the lowest, so the median (−4.016%) and the 20% trimmed mean (−2.339%) do not move.

## How Far Returns Spread {lo=2}

Spread is measured around a centre: by squared deviations, by absolute deviations or by quantiles. The book's standard deviation divides by $T$, $s^2=\frac1T\sum_t(R_t-\bar R)^2$, the variance of $\hat F$ ([[LM14]]). Software often divides by $T-1$ instead:

$$s_{\mathrm u}^2:=\frac{T}{T-1}\,s^2. \tag{19.1}$$

[[LM20]] shows that $s_{\mathrm u}^2$ is an unbiased estimator of $\sigma^2$ for iid draws, which is why software defaults to it. On large samples the two barely differ: [[LM10]] found a factor of 1.000114 at 4,382 days.

> [!definition] Absolute deviations and quartiles
> The **mean absolute deviation** is $\frac1T\sum_t\lvert R_t-\bar R\rvert$. The **interquartile range** is $\operatorname{IQR}:=\hat F^{-1}(0.75)-\hat F^{-1}(0.25)$. The **median absolute deviation** $\operatorname{MAD}$ is the median of $\lvert R_t-\operatorname{med}\rvert$, $t=1,\dots,T$, by the same quantile rule.

To compare these scales with $s$, compute them for a normal law. Let $R\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ and $Z=(R-\mu)/\sigma$. Because $z\varphi(z)=-\varphi'(z)$, symmetry gives $\E\lvert Z\rvert=2\int_0^\infty z\varphi(z)\,dz=2\varphi(0)=\sqrt{2/\pi}$. The median $z$ of $\lvert Z\rvert$ solves $\Pr(\lvert Z\rvert\le z)=2\Phi(z)-1=\tfrac12$, so $z=\Phi^{-1}(0.75)=0.6745$, and by (15.6) the quartiles of $R$ are $\mu\pm\sigma\Phi^{-1}(0.75)$.

> [!result] Normal-equivalent scales
> For $R\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$,
> $$\begin{gathered}\E\lvert R-\mu\rvert=\sigma\sqrt{2/\pi},\\ F_{\lvert R-\mu\rvert}^{-1}(\tfrac12)=\sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(0.75),\\ \operatorname{IQR}=2\sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(0.75),\\ \sigma=1.4826\,F_{\lvert R-\mu\rvert}^{-1}(\tfrac12)=\operatorname{IQR}/1.349.\end{gathered} \tag{19.2}$$
> The constants $1/\Phi^{-1}(0.75)$ and $2\Phi^{-1}(0.75)$ are shown rounded; the book's computations use them exactly.

On data, $1.4826\,\operatorname{MAD}$ and $\operatorname{IQR}/1.349$ are **normal-equivalent scales**: on a large normal sample they come out close to $s$.

> [!example] The tariff week's spread {#weekscale}
> For [[eg:week]]'s five returns, compute $s$, $s_{\mathrm u}$, the mean absolute deviation and the MAD.
>
> > [!solution]
> > From the mean, −1.4934% to four decimals, the deviations are −8.2063, −2.5227, −4.2931, +10.7446 and +4.2776 points. Their squares sum to 225.88, so $s=\sqrt{225.88/5}=6.721\%$ and $s_{\mathrm u}=\sqrt{225.88/4}=7.515\%$, 1.118 times larger. The absolute deviations average 6.009%.
> >
> > From the median −4.016%, the absolute deviations are 5.684, 0, 1.770, 13.267 and 6.800 points. Their median, the MAD, is 5.684%. With five days the divisor matters.

> [!example] A typical day against the standard deviation {#robustscale}
> Compute TR23's $s$, $s_{\mathrm u}$, mean absolute deviation, IQR and MAD and the two normal-equivalent scales, and annualize the scales with $A=246.12$.
>
> > [!solution]
> > TR23 has $s=1.2084\%$ and $s_{\mathrm u}=1.2085\%$. The mean absolute deviation is 0.836%, or 0.692 of $s$, against $\sqrt{2/\pi}=0.798$ for a normal law. The quartiles are −0.4904% and 0.6812%, so $\operatorname{IQR}=1.1716\%$. The MAD is 0.5889%: exactly half the days, 2,925, lie within 0.5889% of the median.
> >
> > The normal-equivalent scales are $1.4826\times0.5889\%=0.873\%$ and $1.1716\%/1.349=0.868\%$. Annualized by $\sqrt A$, they give 13.7% and 13.6%, against 18.96% from $s$. The typical day of TAIEX moves like a 13.7% volatility, and the large days add the rest.

[[ex:rollscale]] repeats the comparison over rolling years. The three scales rise and fall together; $s$ stands furthest above the other two when a few extreme days fall into a calm year.

> [!pitfall] A robust scale leaves out the risk
> The normal-equivalent scales treat the largest days almost as if they were absent. For a position, those days are the risk: a variance, a Kelly fraction ([[LM4]]) or a Sharpe ratio needs $s$. Quote a robust scale to describe the typical day.

> [!zh] 一般的一天與平均的一天
> 敘述統計（descriptive statistics）用幾個數字描述一組報酬。平均數（mean）會被極端值拉動；中位數（median）與截尾平均數（trimmed mean）幾乎不受少數幾天影響，屬於穩健統計（robust statistics）。離散程度（dispersion）也一樣：標準差（standard deviation）與平均絕對偏差（mean absolute deviation）讓大漲大跌的日子占很重的分量，四分位距（interquartile range）與中位數絕對離差（median absolute deviation）只看中間那一半。換算成常態分布（normal distribution）下等效的波動率（volatility），加權股價報酬指數「一般的一天」年化約 13.7%，標準差換算卻是 18.96%，差額來自少數大波動日。樣本變異數（sample variance）改除以 $T-1$，是為了得到不偏估計量（unbiased estimator）；只有樣本很小時，除以 $T$ 或 $T-1$ 才有明顯差別。

> [!kc]
> By what factor does $s_{\mathrm u}$ exceed $s$ for the five days of [[eg:week]], and for TR23's 5,849 days?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > By (19.1) the factor is $\sqrt{T/(T-1)}$: $\sqrt{5/4}=1.118$ for five days and $\sqrt{5{,}849/5{,}848}=1.0000855$ for TR23.

## Skewness and Kurtosis as Statistics {lo=3}

[[LM14]] defined skewness and kurtosis for a random variable, (14.5). Applied to $\hat F$ they become statistics, with a choice of divisor and a strong dependence on a few days.

> [!definition] Sample skewness and kurtosis
> With $\bar R$ and $s$ (divisor $T$), the **sample skewness** and **sample kurtosis** are
> $$\begin{gathered}\widehat{\operatorname{Skew}}:=\frac{\frac1T\sum_t(R_t-\bar R)^3}{s^3},\\ \widehat{\operatorname{Kurt}}:=\frac{\frac1T\sum_t(R_t-\bar R)^4}{s^4},\\ \widehat{\operatorname{Skew}}_{\mathrm{adj}}:=\frac{\sqrt{T(T-1)}}{T-2}\,\widehat{\operatorname{Skew}},\\ \widehat{\operatorname{Kurt}}_{\mathrm{adj}}:=3+\frac{T-1}{(T-2)(T-3)}\\ \qquad\times\big[(T+1)(\widehat{\operatorname{Kurt}}-3)+6\big].\end{gathered} \tag{19.3}$$
> The sample excess kurtosis is $\widehat{\operatorname{Kurt}}-3$; the last two are the **adjusted** versions.

Formula (19.3) is (14.5) under $\hat F$. It is what [[LM4]] and [[LM5]] computed for 0050, with $\hat\mu=\bar R$ and $s$, and SciPy's default (`bias=True`). SciPy returns the adjusted versions with `bias=False`; their factors tend to 1 as $T$ grows. How far a sample skewness moves between samples is measured by its standard error ([[LM20]]).

> [!example] When the divisor matters {#divisor}
> Compute the skewness and excess kurtosis, plain and adjusted, of the 23 calendar-year log returns of 2003–2025 and of TR23's daily returns. Then adjust [[LM4]]'s 8.12 for 0050's 2,902 days.
>
> > [!solution]
> > By (19.3) the 23 years have a skewness of −0.99 and an excess kurtosis of 2.31. Adjusted, they become −1.06 and 3.22: the skewness factor is $\sqrt{23\times22}/21=1.071$, and the excess kurtosis is $\tfrac{22}{21\times20}(24\times2.31+6)=3.22$.
> >
> > TR23's daily values move only in the fourth digit, −0.3384 to −0.3385 and 5.268 to 5.274. [[LM4]]'s 8.12 becomes 8.14 at 2,902 days.

Quantiles give shape measures that a few days cannot move far.

> [!definition] Quartile skewness and octile kurtosis
> With quantiles $\hat F^{-1}(u)$ and $\operatorname{IQR}=\hat F^{-1}(\tfrac34)-\hat F^{-1}(\tfrac14)$,
> $$\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Skew}_{\mathrm Q}&:=\frac{\hat F^{-1}(\tfrac34)+\hat F^{-1}(\tfrac14)-2\operatorname{med}}{\operatorname{IQR}},\\ \operatorname{Kurt}_{\mathrm O}&:=\frac1{\operatorname{IQR}}\Big[\hat F^{-1}(\tfrac78)-\hat F^{-1}(\tfrac58)\\&\qquad+\hat F^{-1}(\tfrac38)-\hat F^{-1}(\tfrac18)\Big].\end{aligned} \tag{19.4}$$

The quartile skewness compares the distances from the median to the two quartiles. It lies between −1 and 1 and is 0 for a symmetric law. The octile kurtosis compares the two outer octile ranges with the IQR. By (15.6) it is 1.233 for every normal law; outer octiles further from the quartiles raise it.

> [!example] One more limit-down day {#oneday}
> Append a hypothetical −10% day to TR23's 5,849 returns. How far does each statistic of [[ex:summary]] move?
>
> > [!solution]
> > See [[ex:summary]]'s last column. Relative to the unrounded values, the mean falls 2.8%, the median and the trimmed mean 0.4%, and $s$ rises 0.6%. The two robust scales move by less than 0.1%.
> >
> > The skewness goes from −0.338 to −0.425 (−26%) and the excess kurtosis from 5.27 to 5.88 (+12%). The quartile skewness moves 1.2% and the octile kurtosis 0.05%. One day out of 5,850 moves the moment measures by a quarter and an eighth.

> [!pitfall] Kurtosis measures the far tail, not the peak
> A large excess kurtosis is often read as a peaked distribution. Yet the 10% regime has the larger excess kurtosis, 8.07 against 3.39, and the smaller octile kurtosis. Excess kurtosis averages fourth powers: the ten largest of TR23's 5,849 terms supply 35.0% of their sum ([[LM15]]). Quote it with its period, and beside a quantile measure.

> [!zh] 偏態與峰態是很脆弱的統計量
> 樣本偏態（sample skewness）與樣本峰態（sample kurtosis）把離差（deviation）取三次方、四次方再平均，所以由最極端的幾天決定。只要多加一個假設的 −10% 交易日，樣本偏態就從 −0.338 變成 −0.425，超額峰態（excess kurtosis）從 5.27 升到 5.88。用四分位數（quartile）與八分位數（octile）定義的偏態與峰態只看分布的中段與肩部，同一天只讓它們變動 1.2% 與 0.05%。兩類量數回答不同的問題：2015 年 6 月漲跌幅放寬後，超額峰態從 3.39 升到 8.07，八分位數峰態卻下降。除以 $T$ 還是用調整後的公式，在 5,849 天裡只影響第四位數字，在 23 個年報酬上就差很多。

> [!kc]
> TR23's quartile skewness is +0.013, while its sample skewness is −0.338. Why can the signs differ? And why can the octile kurtosis fall from 1.534 to 1.420 between the regimes while the excess kurtosis rises from 3.39 to 8.07?
>
> > [!answer]-
> > The quartile skewness compares the middle half, where the upper quartile lies slightly further from the median than the lower one. The sample skewness is decided by the largest moves, which are falls. Likewise the octile kurtosis weighs the shoulders and the excess kurtosis the far tail. After June 2015 the outer octiles moved closer to the quartiles, while a few days reached further.

## Autocorrelation {lo=4}

Autocorrelation measures how a series relates to its own past, the order of the days that Sections 2 to 4 ignored.

> [!example] Two ways to correlate a series with its past {#twoways}
> For [[eg:week]]'s five simple returns, compute the lag-1 autocorrelation by (19.5) below, and as the correlation of the four pairs $(R_{t-1},R_t)$. Then compare the two on TR23's log returns.
>
> > [!solution]
> > With the deviations of [[eg:weekscale]], (19.5) divides the sum of the four products of neighbours, 31.37, by the sum of all five squares, 225.88: $\hat\rho_1=0.139$. The four pairs, each side with its own mean and standard deviation, correlate 0.237.
> >
> > On TR23's 5,849 log returns the two agree: 0.0393 against 0.0394. For $\lvert r_t\rvert$ at lag 20 they give 0.1224 against 0.1228, the 0.123 of [[LM17]]. statsmodels' `acf` agrees with (19.5) to within $10^{-15}$.

A series is **weakly stationary** when its mean, its variance and each lag covariance stay the same at every date; [[LM25]] defines this and tests for it. Its **autocovariance** at lag $k$ is $\gamma_k:=\Cov(x_t,x_{t-k})$, and its **autocorrelation** is $\rho_k:=\gamma_k/\gamma_0$, with $\gamma_0=\Var x_t$.

> [!definition] Sample autocorrelation
> For a series $x_1,\dots,x_T$ with mean $\bar x$ and a lag $0\le k<T$,
> $$\begin{gathered}\hat\gamma_k:=\frac1T\sum_{t=k+1}^{T}(x_t-\bar x)(x_{t-k}-\bar x),\\ \hat\rho_k:=\frac{\hat\gamma_k}{\hat\gamma_0}.\end{gathered} \tag{19.5}$$

The book's convention uses the full-sample mean in both factors and divides by $T$ at every lag, as statsmodels' `acf` does. Dividing by $T$ rather than $T-k$ shrinks $\hat\rho_k$ by a factor of about $(T-k)/T$, which matters only at long lags or in short series.

> [!result] The iid band
> Let $x_1,\dots,x_T$ be iid with finite variance, and $T$ large. Then for each lag $k\ge1$, $\hat\rho_k$ is approximately normal with mean near 0 and
> $$\Var\hat\rho_k\approx\frac1T, \tag{19.6}$$
> so about 95% of such autocorrelations lie within the **iid band** $\pm1.96/\sqrt T$. The Deep Dive derives the variance; the approximate normality is stated without proof (a central limit theorem for dependent terms, beyond [[LM17]]'s (17.5)).

> [!deepdive]- Why the iid band is $\pm1.96/\sqrt T$
> Write $\tilde x_t:=x_t-\E x_t$, and replace $\bar x$ by $\E x_t$ in (19.5); the error is of order $1/T$ and is dropped. The $T-k$ products $\tilde x_t\tilde x_{t-k}$, $t=k+1,\dots,T$, have mean 0 by (16.1). Two of them with different $t$ are uncorrelated, because the latest date in their product appears once and has mean 0. Each has variance $\E[\tilde x_t^2\tilde x_{t-k}^2]=\gamma_0^2$, again by (16.1). So by (16.3), $\Var(T\hat\gamma_k)\approx(T-k)\gamma_0^2$. For large $T$, $\hat\gamma_0\approx\gamma_0$, and $\Var\hat\rho_k\approx(T-k)/T^2\approx1/T$.
>
> When the squares correlate, as in volatility clustering, the $\tilde x_t$ are dependent and $\E[\tilde x_t^2\tilde x_{t-k}^2]$ exceeds $\gamma_0^2$. The band is then too narrow ([[LM25]]).

On TR23 the band is $\pm1.96/\sqrt{5{,}849}=\pm0.0256$. In [[ex:acf]], returns barely correlate with their past, but absolute returns correlate with theirs at every lag up to 30: large moves follow large moves, of either sign. This is **volatility clustering**, which [[LM26]] models. Clustering also widens the band, because the products of the Deep Dive then vary more ([[LM25]]).

## How Daily Variances Add Up {lo=5}

Take two days of a weakly stationary series of log returns. By (16.3), $\Var(r_1+r_2)=2\gamma_0+2\gamma_1$, so the two-day variance is $1+\rho_1$ times the sum of the daily variances. Independent days give 1, positively correlated days more.

> [!result] Variance of a sum of $m$ days
> Let $r_t$ be weakly stationary with autocovariances $\gamma_k$, and $r_t^{(m)}:=r_{t-m+1}+\dots+r_t$. Then
> $$\begin{gathered}\Var r_t^{(m)}=m\gamma_0+2\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}(m-k)\gamma_k,\\ \frac{\Var r_t^{(m)}}{m\gamma_0}=1+2\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\Big(1-\frac km\Big)\rho_k.\end{gathered} \tag{19.7}$$

*Proof.* By (16.3) with all weights 1, the variance is the sum of the $m^2$ covariances $\Cov(r_i,r_j)$ within the window. The $m$ diagonal terms give $m\gamma_0$. Lag $k$ occurs in $m-k$ pairs with $i>j$ and in as many with $i<j$, which gives $2(m-k)\gamma_k$. Dividing by $m\gamma_0$ gives the ratio. $\blacksquare$

The ratio in (19.7) is the model's. For uncorrelated days it is 1, and then $\sqrt m$ scales a volatility. As $m$ grows, $\gamma_0$ times the model's ratio tends to the long-run variance that [[LM23]]'s HAC errors estimate.

> [!example] Two days, and LM16's year {#sumvar}
> Evaluate (19.7) at $m=2$ on TR23, against the overlapping estimate of (19.8) below. Then compare [[LM16]]'s model year, 245 days with adjacent correlation 0.04 and no other, with TR23's 246 days.
>
> > [!solution]
> > At $m=2$, TR23 gives $1+\hat\rho_1=1.0393$, and the overlapping estimate is $\operatorname{VR}(2)=1.0392$. [[LM16]]'s model year gives $1+2(0.04)(244/245)=1.0797$, as printed there for U28's TAIEX price index.
> >
> > On TR23, with $\hat\rho_1=0.039337$, lag 1 alone gives $1+2(0.039337)(245/246)=1.0784$. All 245 lags give 1.280 ([[ex:vrtable]]'s identity column), and $\operatorname{VR}(246)=1.208$. The other lags matter more than the first.

On data, every window of $m$ consecutive log returns gives one sum, and the spread of these sums about $m\bar r$ is compared with $m$ daily variances.

> [!definition] Variance ratio
> For log returns $r_1,\dots,r_T$ with mean $\bar r$, $s_r^2$ with divisor $T$, and $1\le m\le T$, the **variance ratio** is
> $$\operatorname{VR}(m):=\frac{\frac1{T-m+1}\sum_{t=m}^{T}\big(r_t^{(m)}-m\bar r\big)^2}{m\,s_r^2}. \tag{19.8}$$
> The **non-overlapping** $\operatorname{VR}_{\mathrm n}(m)$ is the same ratio over the $\lfloor(T-o)/m\rfloor$ consecutive blocks after the first $o$ returns, $0\le o<m$, divided by the number of blocks.

Under iid days, $\operatorname{VR}(m)$ averages about $(1-m/T)/(1-1/T)$, its **iid factor**. The estimator is biased down, by about $m/T$.

> [!deepdive]- The overlapping estimator under iid days
> Let the $r_t$ be iid with variance $\sigma_r^2$. A window sum minus $m\bar r$ is $\sum_{\text{window}}r_t-\frac mT\sum_{\text{all}}r_t$, with mean 0. The two sums share the window's $m$ days, so by (16.3) and (17.1) its variance is
> $$\begin{aligned}&m\sigma_r^2+\frac{m^2}{T^2}\,T\sigma_r^2-2\,\frac mT\,m\sigma_r^2\\&\qquad=m\sigma_r^2\Big(1-\frac mT\Big).\end{aligned}$$
> Likewise $\E s_r^2=\sigma_r^2(1-1/T)$, the bias that (19.1) removes ([[LM20]]). The ratio of these expectations is the iid factor, an approximation to the expectation of the ratio.
>
> Experiment $e=1$ checks it with 10,000 samples of 5,849 iid standard normal log returns from `default_rng([1, 20261011])`. The mean values of $\operatorname{VR}(m)$ are $0.960\pm0.002$ at $m=246$ and $0.791\pm0.005$ at $m=1{,}231$, against factors of 0.958 and 0.790. Each ± is one standard error of a mean (divisor 10,000).

Overlapping windows are far from independent. Under iid days, two consecutive one-year sums share 245 days and correlate $245/246=0.996$. TR23's 5,604 one-year windows hold only about $T/m=23.8$ disjoint years, and its 4,619 five-year windows only 4.75.

## Annualizing, and the Annual Volatility {lo=6,7}

Each annualization rule has a condition. A year's log return is the sum of its daily log returns, (1.12), so by (14.3) the annual mean log return is $A\bar r$, exactly. Variances add only when days are uncorrelated, (19.7): then the annual variance is $As_r^2$ and the volatility $\sqrt A\,s_r$. A Sharpe ratio with $r_f=0$, mean over standard deviation, then scales by $A/\sqrt A=\sqrt A$.

With correlated days, (19.7) changes the denominator. For sums of log returns with a constant mean, under (19.7)'s conditions, the Sharpe ratio of sums of $m$ days is

$$\mathrm{SR}(m)=\frac{m\,\E r_t}{\sqrt{\Var r_t^{(m)}}}=\frac{\sqrt m\,\mathrm{SR}}{\sqrt{\Var r_t^{(m)}/(m\gamma_0)}}, \tag{19.9}$$

estimated with $\operatorname{VR}(m)$ in the denominator. Lo (2002) shows that the $\sqrt{12}$ rule for monthly Sharpe ratios holds only under special conditions. Serial correlation, he finds, can overstate an annual Sharpe ratio by as much as 65%. [[LM33]] gives the Sharpe ratio's standard error when returns are correlated.

Weekly, monthly and yearly log returns are sums of daily log returns over calendar periods, as in [[ex:case]]. [[ex:rules]] applies the four rules to TR23.

## Descriptive Statistics in Python {lo=7}

Each library has its own default divisor and quantile rule, so pass them explicitly.

```python
import numpy as np
import polars as pl
from scipy import stats
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import acf

tr = pl.read_csv("data/taiex_tr.csv")["price"].to_numpy()
R = tr[1:] / tr[:-1] - 1                 # T = 5,849 simple returns
r = np.log1p(R)                          # log returns, for sums
s, s_u = R.std(ddof=0), R.std(ddof=1)    # always pass ddof
med = np.quantile(R, 0.5, method="inverted_cdf")      # R_(2925)
mad = np.quantile(np.abs(R - med), 0.5, method="inverted_cdf")
j0 = int(0.05 * len(R))                  # 292 dropped at each end
trim5 = np.sort(R)[j0:-j0].mean()
print(stats.skew(R), stats.kurtosis(R))  # -0.3384, 5.268
print(stats.kurtosis(R, bias=False))     # 5.274, adjusted


def rho(x, k):                           # (19.5): full mean, divisor T
    xc = x - x.mean()
    return (xc[k:] * xc[:-k]).sum() / (xc * xc).sum()


def vr(x, m):                            # (19.8), overlapping sums
    c = np.concatenate([[0.0], np.cumsum(x)])
    d = c[m:] - c[:-m] - m * x.mean()
    return (d**2).mean() / (m * x.var(ddof=0))


print(rho(r, 1), acf(r, nlags=30, fft=False)[1])   # 0.0393, 0.0393
print(vr(r, 246))                                  # 1.208
d = pl.read_csv("data/taiex_tr.csv", try_parse_dates=True)
d = d.with_columns(r=pl.col("price").log().diff()).drop_nulls()
yr = (d.filter(pl.col("date").dt.year() <= 2025)
       .group_by(pl.col("date").dt.year().alias("y"))
       .agg(pl.col("r").sum()).sort("y"))
print(yr["r"].std(ddof=0))               # 0.22207, divisor 23
```

NumPy's `std` divides by $T$ unless given `ddof=1`, and Polars' by $T-1$ unless given `ddof=0`. SciPy's `skew` and `kurtosis` use divisor-$T$ moments unless given `bias=False`. `np.median` averages the two middle values of an even sample, so the book's rule needs `method="inverted_cdf"`. statsmodels' `acf` already uses the book's convention.

## Summary {-}

- Robust centres describe the typical day; growth needs the mean.
- The standard deviation divides by $T$ or, as $s_{\mathrm u}$, by $T-1$, (19.1); (19.2) makes robust scales normal-equivalent.
- Sample skewness and kurtosis, (19.3), hang on a few days; the quantile measures of (19.4) do not.
- TAIEX's returns barely autocorrelate, (19.5), its absolute returns strongly; the iid band (19.6) assumes what clustering breaks.
- The variance of a sum of $m$ days depends on every lag up to $m-1$, (19.7); $\operatorname{VR}(m)$, (19.8), measures it, biased down by about $m/T$.
- Log means annualize exactly; variances, volatilities and Sharpe ratios only for uncorrelated days, (19.9).
- TAIEX's annual volatility depends on how it is measured, and 23 calendar years pin it down loosely.

## Solutions {-}

1. **C** is correct. Sorted, the returns are −5.6081%, −4.0582%, −2.8622%, −2.3407% and +6.3683%. The middle three average −3.087%. A is the mean, with nothing trimmed. B is the median, which trims two returns at each end.

2. **A** is correct: $22.207\%\times\sqrt{23/22}=22.71\%$. B multiplies by $23/22$, the factor of the variance, not of the standard deviation. C divides by the square root instead of multiplying.

3. **B** is correct: $0.5889\%\times1.4826\times\sqrt{246.12}=13.70\%$. A annualizes the MAD itself, which is not a standard deviation. C divides by 1.4826 instead of multiplying.

4. **A** is correct: $(0.681-0.490-2\times0.088)/(0.681+0.490)=0.015/1.171=0.013$. B reverses the sign, taking twice the median minus the quartiles. C leaves out the median, $(0.681-0.490)/1.171=0.163$.

5. **C** is correct. At $m=2$ the only lag is 1, with weight $1-\tfrac12$, so the ratio is $1+2\cdot\tfrac12\cdot0.0393=1.0393$. A forgets the weight $1-k/m$. B is its square root, the ratio of volatilities.

6. **B** is correct: the iid factor is $(1-1{,}231/5{,}849)/(1-1/5{,}849)=0.79$. A assumes that the estimator is unbiased. C is $m/T$, the size of the bias, not the ratio.

7. The model's ratio is $1+2(0.04)(245/246)=1.0797$. Without it, $\sqrt{246}\times1.2107\%=18.99\%$; with it, $18.99\%\times\sqrt{1.0797}=19.73\%$. TR23's lag 1 alone gives almost the same, 1.0784, but $\operatorname{VR}(246)=1.208$: the other 244 lags add more than the first.

8. (a) As in the second Deep Dive, by (16.3) and (17.1) a window sum minus $m\bar r$ has variance $m\sigma_r^2(1-m/T)$, and $\E s_r^2=\sigma_r^2(1-1/T)$. Their ratio is $(1-m/T)/(1-1/T)$, a ratio of expectations in place of the expected ratio.
   (b) At $m=246$, $T=5{,}664$ it is 0.957; at $m=1{,}231$, $T=5{,}849$ it is 0.790.
   (c) The ratio is $1.0536/0.957=1.101$, and $\sqrt{1.101}\times18.563\%=19.48\%$.

9. (a) By $\sqrt{246}$, $0.0400\times\sqrt{246}=0.627$; by (19.9), $0.627/\sqrt{1.0536}=0.611$.
   (b) The ratio is $11.646/22.207=0.524$.
   (c) The means agree because both spread the same total log growth over the years. The calendar years' standard deviation, 22.207%, exceeds even the overlapping sums' $\sqrt{1.0536}\times18.563\%=19.05\%$, so their ratio is lower.

10. (a) By (15.11), $R-\mu=\sigma\sqrt{(\nu-2)/\nu}\,Y$ with $Y\sim t_\nu$. The $t_\nu$ law is symmetric, so $\Pr(\lvert Y\rvert\le y)=2F_{t_\nu}(y)-1$, which equals $\tfrac12$ at $y=F_{t_\nu}^{-1}(0.75)$. The median of $\lvert R-\mu\rvert$ is therefore $\sigma\sqrt{(\nu-2)/\nu}\,F_{t_\nu}^{-1}(0.75)$; divide by $\Phi^{-1}(0.75)$ and $\sigma$.
    (b) At $\nu=5$, $F_{t_5}^{-1}(0.75)=0.7267$, and the ratio is $0.7267\times\sqrt{0.6}/0.6745=0.835$. At $\nu=3.065$, the quantile is 0.7628 and the ratio 0.667. TR23's 0.723 lies between them.

11. Of the 30 lags, 9 lie outside the band for $r_t$, and all 30 for $\lvert r_t\rvert$ and $r_t^2$ ([[ex:acf]]). Lag 29 of $r_t$ is −0.02561, inside the band of ±0.02563 by 0.00002. NumPy and statsmodels agree to within $10^{-15}$.

12. The four annual standard deviations are 18.56%, 18.09%, 18.12% and 22.21% ([[ex:case]]). The grids run from 11.89% ($o=171$) to 25.04% ($o=212$), with 20.18% at $o=0$, and their median is 19.13%, the 123rd of 246 sorted values. `np.median` would average the 123rd and 124th, 19.15%.

## Glossary {-}

| Term | 中文 | Meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Descriptive statistics | 敘述統計 | Numbers that summarize a sample, with no claim about sampling error. |
| Median | 中位數 | $\operatorname{med}=\hat F^{-1}(\tfrac12)$, the middle return. |
| Trimmed mean | 截尾平均數 | The mean after dropping the $j_0$ smallest and $j_0$ largest values. |
| Robust statistics | 穩健統計 | Statistics that a few observations cannot move far. |
| Mean absolute deviation | 平均絕對偏差 | The mean of $\lvert R_t-\bar R\rvert$. |
| Interquartile range | 四分位距 | $\hat F^{-1}(0.75)-\hat F^{-1}(0.25)$. |
| Median absolute deviation | 中位數絕對離差 | MAD, the median of $\lvert R_t-\operatorname{med}\rvert$. |
| Normal-equivalent scale | 常態等效尺度 | $1.4826\,\operatorname{MAD}$ or $\operatorname{IQR}/1.349$, which a normal law makes equal to $\sigma$, (19.2). |
| Sample skewness, kurtosis | 樣本偏態、樣本峰態 | (14.5) under $\hat F$, (19.3); the adjusted versions are SciPy's `bias=False`. |
| Quartile skewness, octile kurtosis | 四分位數偏態、八分位數峰態 | Shape measures from quantiles, (19.4). |
| Weakly stationary | 平穩 | Mean, variance and each lag covariance the same at every date ([[LM25]]). |
| Autocorrelation | 自我相關 | $\rho_k=\gamma_k/\gamma_0$, the correlation of a series with itself $k$ dates earlier; sample version (19.5). |
| Autocorrelation function | 自我相關函數 | The autocorrelations as a function of the lag $k$. |
| iid band | 獨立同分布區間 | $\pm1.96/\sqrt T$, which holds about 95% of iid sample autocorrelations, (19.6). |
| Volatility clustering | 波動群聚現象 | Large moves followed by large moves, of either sign. |
| Variance ratio | 變異數比 | $\operatorname{VR}(m)$, the variance of overlapping sums of $m$ days over $m$ daily variances, (19.8). |
| iid factor | 獨立同分布因子 | $(1-m/T)/(1-1/T)$, the approximate mean of $\operatorname{VR}(m)$ under iid days. |
