## Introduction

An integral adds up a quantity that changes continuously: interest accruing under a moving rate, cash arriving as a stream, probability spread along the return axis. Each is a rate multiplied by a small width and summed, and each is the area under a curve. This module builds the integral from such sums, evaluates it with the fundamental theorem of calculus, extends it to infinite intervals and computes it numerically when no formula exists. One risk question runs through all of it.

> [!case] How often should a −5% day happen?
> Take 0050's 2,902 daily total returns from 3 November 2014 to 8 October 2026. Their mean is 0.0871% and their standard deviation 1.2385%. A risk model that treats daily returns as normal with these two numbers draws a bell curve. The probability of a day at or below −5% is the area under that curve to the left of −5%.
>
> That area is $2.0\times10^{-5}$. Over 2,902 days the model expects **0.058** such days, or one every **206 years**.
>
> The data contain **nine** ([[ex:case]]). Five of them fell between April 2024 and July 2026. On 7 April 2025 0050 closed down 10.000%, the exchange's daily limit; under the fitted model a day that bad comes once in $2\times10^{13}$ years.
>
> Was the area computed wrongly, is the model wrong, or was the market unlucky? The area has no formula, because the bell curve has no elementary antiderivative. By Section 8 you will compute it four ways, compare it with the data threshold by threshold, and see where the model's tail runs out.

## Accumulation and Area {lo=1}

### Rate times time, added up

A deposit earning a constant continuously compounded rate $r$ for a time $\Delta t$ accumulates $r\,\Delta t$ of log growth ([[LM1]]). If the rate moves during the year, no single product works. Split the year into short pieces on which the rate is nearly constant, multiply each piece's rate by its length, and add. The finer the split, the closer the sum comes to one number: the accumulated rate.

Draw the rate as a curve over the time axis. Each product is a thin rectangle, the sum is the area of a staircase, and the limit is the area under the curve. Accumulation and area are one idea.

> [!definition] Riemann sum and definite integral
> For $f$ defined on $[a,b]$, choose a partition $a=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_N=b$ with widths $\Delta t_i=t_i-t_{i-1}$ and a sample point $\xi_i\in[t_{i-1},t_i]$ in each piece. The **Riemann sum** is $\sum_{i=1}^N f(\xi_i)\,\Delta t_i$. The function $f$ is **integrable** on $[a,b]$ if these sums approach one number as the widest piece shrinks to zero, whatever the sample points. That number is the **definite integral**
> $$\int_a^b f(t)\,dt:=\lim_{\max_i\Delta t_i\to0}\ \sum_{i=1}^N f(\xi_i)\,\Delta t_i. \tag{5.1}$$

Every continuous function on a closed interval is integrable. So is a bounded function with finitely many jumps, such as a policy rate that changes on announcement dates (stated without proof; Stewart, Clegg and Watson 2021). The letter $t$ is a dummy: $\int_a^b f(t)\,dt=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$.

Where $f$ is negative the products are negative, so the integral is a **signed** area: area above the axis minus area below. A P&L rate that is positive in the morning and negative in the afternoon integrates to the day's net P&L, not its gross. Three properties follow at once from the same properties of finite sums:

- **Linearity:** $\int_a^b(\eta f+\zeta h)=\eta\int_a^b f+\zeta\int_a^b h$ for constants $\eta$, $\zeta$.
- **Additivity:** $\int_a^b f=\int_a^{x_0} f+\int_{x_0}^b f$ for $x_0\in[a,b]$; we set $\int_b^a f:=-\int_a^b f$.
- **Monotonicity:** if $f\le h$ on $[a,b]$ then $\int_a^b f\le\int_a^b h$; in particular $f_{\min}(b-a)\le\int_a^b f\le f_{\max}(b-a)$ when $f_{\min}\le f\le f_{\max}$.

### Left, right and midpoint sums

With $N$ equal panels of width $\Delta t=(b-a)/N$ and grid points $t_i=a+i\,\Delta t$, three choices of sample point have names:

$$L_N=\Delta t\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}f(t_i),\qquad R_N=\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(t_i),\qquad M_N=\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{N}f\big(t_i-\tfrac{\Delta t}2\big).$$

For an increasing $f$ the left value of each panel is its minimum and the right value its maximum, so $L_N\le\int_a^b f\le R_N$. The gap telescopes:

$$R_N-L_N=\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big[f(t_i)-f(t_{i-1})\big]=\Delta t\,\big[f(b)-f(a)\big].$$

So for a monotone function each one-sided sum lies within $\Delta t\,\lvert f(b)-f(a)\rvert$ of the integral. The error is $O(\Delta t)$: halving the panel width halves the error bound. The midpoint sum does much better, with an error of order $\Delta t^2$; Section 8 shows why.

> [!pitfall] Rates per year, time in days
> An integral of a rate is only meaningful when $t$ is measured in the unit of the rate's denominator. A 2% annual rate held for 30 calendar days accrues $0.02\times30/365=0.164\%$ of log growth, not $0.02\times30$. > [!zh] 積分就是「累積」
> 速率乘以時間就是累積量：利率乘以期間得到利息，機率密度乘以區間寬度得到機率。速率會變動時，就把區間切細，每一小段用「當時的速率 × 寬度」近似，再全部加總，這就是黎曼和；切到無限細的極限就是定積分，也就是曲線下的有號面積。左端點與右端點近似的誤差與格寬 $\Delta t$ 成正比，中點近似的誤差與 $\Delta t^2$ 成正比，所以中點法收斂快得多。銀行的「每日計息」本身就是一個 $\Delta t=1/365$ 的黎曼和。

## The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus {lo=2}

Riemann sums define the integral but are a slow way to compute it. The fundamental theorem of calculus connects integrals with the derivatives of [[LM2]]: accumulating a rate and differentiating an accumulated amount undo each other.

Define the **accumulation function** $I(x):=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt$. Increase $x$ by a small $\Delta x$: $I$ gains a thin strip of width $\Delta x$ whose height is close to $f(x)$, so $I(x+\Delta x)-I(x)\approx f(x)\,\Delta x$. The rate at which accumulated area grows is the height of the curve.

> [!theorem] Fundamental theorem of calculus
> Let $f$ be continuous on $[a,b]$.
> 1. The accumulation function $I(x):=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ is differentiable on $(a,b)$, with $I'(x)=f(x)$.
> 2. If $F$ is any function with $F'=f$ on $[a,b]$, an **antiderivative** of $f$, then
> $$\int_a^b f(t)\,dt=F(b)-F(a)=:\big[F(t)\big]_a^b. \tag{5.2}$$

*Proof.* (1) For $\Delta x>0$ let $f_{\min}$ and $f_{\max}$ be the minimum and maximum of $f$ on $[x,x+\Delta x]$. Additivity and monotonicity give $f_{\min}\,\Delta x\le I(x+\Delta x)-I(x)\le f_{\max}\,\Delta x$; divide by $\Delta x$. As $\Delta x\to0$, continuity squeezes $f_{\min}$ and $f_{\max}$ to $f(x)$, so the difference quotient tends to $f(x)$; the case $\Delta x<0$ is symmetric.

(2) By part 1, $I-F$ has zero derivative on $(a,b)$, so it is constant there by the mean value theorem ([[LM2]]), and by continuity on $[a,b]$. Since $I(a)=0$, $I(x)=F(x)-F(a)$, and $x=b$ gives (5.2). $\blacksquare$

Part 2 is the computing tool: find any antiderivative and subtract. Two antiderivatives differ by a constant, which cancels. The family of all antiderivatives is the **indefinite integral**, written $\int f(t)\,dt=F(t)+C$. Every derivative rule of [[LM2]] read backwards is an antiderivative; [[ex:antider]] lists the ones this book uses.

> [!exhibit] Antiderivatives used in this book {#antider}

### Accumulation with a moving rate

The theorem finishes [[eg:riemann]] exactly. An antiderivative of $2.0\%-0.5\%\,e^{-2t}$ is $2.0\%\,t+0.25\%\,e^{-2t}$, so $\int_0^1 r(t)\,dt=2.0\%+0.25\%\,(e^{-2}-1)=1.783834\%$.

More generally, money compounding at a moving rate satisfies $W'(t)=r(t)\,W(t)$: it grows at the rate $r(t)$ per unit of wealth. By the chain rule $(\ln W)'=W'/W=r(t)$, so $\ln W$ is an antiderivative of $r$, and (5.2) gives

$$W(T)=W(0)\exp\Big(\int_0^T r(t)\,dt\Big),\qquad P(T)=\exp\Big(-\int_0^T r(t)\,dt\Big). \tag{5.3}$$

$P(T)$ is the discount factor for time $T$. With a constant rate, (5.3) is the $e^{rT}$ of [[LM1]]; with a moving rate, the integral replaces $rT$. The average rate $y(T):=\frac1T\int_0^T r(t)\,dt$ is the continuously compounded zero yield, and $r(T)$ is the forward rate of [[LM2]], as Section 4 confirms.

### Limits that move

Part 1 combined with the chain rule differentiates integrals whose limits depend on a variable. If $u$ and $h$ are differentiable and $f$ is continuous on an interval containing their values,

$$\frac{d}{dx}\int_{h(x)}^{u(x)}f(t)\,dt=f\big(u(x)\big)\,u'(x)-f\big(h(x)\big)\,h'(x).$$

The case that matters most here is the normal distribution function $\Phi(z):=\int_{-\infty}^{z}\varphi(t)\,dt$, the area to the left of $z$ under the standard normal density. Section 5 shows that this area is finite. Writing $\Phi(z)=\Phi(0)+\int_0^z\varphi(t)\,dt$ and applying part 1 gives $\Phi'(z)=\varphi(z)$.

> [!example] How sensitive is a tail probability to volatility? {#sens}
> Under a normal model with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, the probability of a day at or below $\kappa$ is $\Phi(z)$ with $z=(\kappa-\mu)/\sigma$; Section 4 derives this. For the case, $\kappa=-5\%$, $\mu=\hat\mu=0.0871\%$ and $\sigma=s=1.2385\%$; at full precision ([[eg:standardize]]) they give $z=-4.1074386$, where $\varphi(z)=8.6578\times10^{-5}$ and $\Phi(z)=2.0004\times10^{-5}$ (Section 7 computes $\varphi$, Section 8 $\Phi$). Find the elasticity $(\sigma/\Phi)\,d\Phi(z)/d\sigma$, and compare its linear prediction for a 10% higher $\sigma$ with the exact change.
>
> > [!solution]
> > The chain rule and $\Phi'=\varphi$ give $d\Phi(z)/d\sigma=\varphi(z)\,dz/d\sigma$ with $dz/d\sigma=-(\kappa-\mu)/\sigma^2=-z/\sigma$. The elasticity is therefore $-z\,\varphi(z)/\Phi(z)=4.1074386\times4.328=17.78$. A 1% error in $\sigma$ moves the tail probability by about 18%.
> >
> > For $\sigma$ 10% higher, 1.3624%, the linear prediction is $+178\%$. Exactly, $z$ becomes $-4.1074386/1.1=-3.73404$ and $\Phi(z)=9.422\times10^{-5}$, 4.71 times the original: $+371\%$. The linear rule ignores compounding: an elasticity converts small percentage changes, and over a 10% move they compound.
> >
> > Even in logs, $17.78\times\ln1.1=1.6946$, a factor of 5.44, overshoots, because the elasticity falls to 14.83 at the new $z$. Under a normal model a 10% error in $\sigma$ is almost a factor-of-five error in the probability of a −5% day.

> [!pitfall] The theorem needs continuity on the whole interval
> Applied blindly, (5.2) gives $\int_{-1}^{1}x^{-2}\,dx=\big[-1/x\big]_{-1}^{1}=-2$: a negative area for a positive function. The integrand is unbounded at 0, so the integral is improper (Section 5); each half diverges, and the integral does not exist. Check that $f$ is continuous on the closed interval before subtracting antiderivative values.

> [!zh] 微積分基本定理：累積與速率互為逆運算
> 把速率從 $a$ 累積到 $x$ 得到累積函數 $I(x)$；再對 $I$ 微分，就回到原本的速率 $f(x)$。所以算定積分不必真的加總無限多個長方形，只要找到任何一個導數等於 $f$ 的函數 $F$，計算 $F(b)-F(a)$ 即可。利率會變動時，財富是 $W(T)=W(0)\exp\big(\int_0^T r\big)$，積分取代了 $rT$。同一個定理也說常態分配函數的導數就是密度，$\Phi'=\varphi$；由此可知尾端機率對波動度極為敏感：在 −5% 的門檻，$\sigma$ 錯 10%，機率就差將近 5 倍。

> [!kc]
> [[LM2]] defined the instantaneous forward rate as $-\frac{d}{dT}\ln P(T)$. Show that this definition agrees with (5.3).
>
> > [!answer]-
> > From (5.3), $\ln P(T)=-\int_0^T r(t)\,dt$. By part 1 of the theorem, $-\frac{d}{dT}\ln P(T)=r(T)$: the forward rate of LM2 is exactly the integrand of (5.3). A rate curve and its discount curve carry the same information; differentiation and integration convert one into the other.

## Techniques: Substitution and Integration by Parts {lo=3}

Most antiderivatives in finance come from two rules, each a derivative rule of [[LM2]] read backwards. The chain rule becomes substitution; the product rule becomes integration by parts.

### Substitution

If $F'=f$, the chain rule gives $\frac{d}{dx}F\big(u(x)\big)=f\big(u(x)\big)\,u'(x)$. Integrating both sides with (5.2) gives the rule.

> [!result] Substitution rule
> If $u$ has a continuous derivative on $[a,b]$ and $f$ is continuous on an interval containing $u([a,b])$, then
> $$\int_a^b f\big(u(x)\big)\,u'(x)\,dx=\int_{u(a)}^{u(b)}f(u)\,du. \tag{5.4}$$

In practice: name the inner function $u$, replace $u'(x)\,dx$ by $du$, and move the limits with the variable. The bookkeeping “$du=u'(x)\,dx$” is the chain rule.

**Standardizing.** The substitution that matters most here is linear. The normal density with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$ is $f(x)=\frac1\sigma\varphi\big(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\big)$. With $u=(x-\mu)/\sigma$ we have $du=dx/\sigma$, and (5.4) gives

$$\int_a^b\frac1\sigma\,\varphi\Big(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\Big)dx=\int_{(a-\mu)/\sigma}^{(b-\mu)/\sigma}\varphi(u)\,du=\Phi\Big(\frac{b-\mu}{\sigma}\Big)-\Phi\Big(\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\Big). \tag{5.5}$$

Every normal probability is a difference of two values of one function, $\Phi$. The factor $1/\sigma$ is not decoration: stretching the horizontal axis by $\sigma$ must shrink the height by $\sigma$, or the area would change. Letting $a\to-\infty$, which Section 5 justifies, gives $\Pr(R\le\kappa)=\Phi\big((\kappa-\mu)/\sigma\big)$.

**A non-linear substitution.** With $u=-x^2/2$ and $du=-x\,dx$,

$$\int x\,e^{-x^2/2}\,dx=-\int e^{u}\,du=-e^{-x^2/2}+C,\qquad\text{so}\qquad\int z\,\varphi(z)\,dz=-\varphi(z)+C.$$

This identity gives the normal model's tail means in Section 7 and the polar-coordinates proof in Section 5.

### Integration by parts

The product rule $(uh)'=u'h+uh'$, integrated with (5.2), trades one integral for another.

> [!result] Integration by parts
> If $u$ and $h$ have continuous derivatives on $[a,b]$, then
> $$\int_a^b u(x)\,h'(x)\,dx=\big[u(x)\,h(x)\big]_a^b-\int_a^b u'(x)\,h(x)\,dx. \tag{5.6}$$

The trade pays when $u$ becomes simpler on differentiation, such as a power of $t$, while $h'$ is easy to integrate, such as an exponential.

**Time-weighted discounting.** Take $u=t$ and $h'=e^{-rt}$, so $h=-e^{-rt}/r$:

$$\int_0^T t\,e^{-rt}\,dt=\Big[-\frac{t}{r}\,e^{-rt}\Big]_0^T+\frac1r\int_0^T e^{-rt}\,dt=\frac{1-(1+rT)\,e^{-rT}}{r^2}.$$

The integrand weights each instant's discount factor by its date; Section 6 turns the integral into a duration. The Deep Dive of [[LM3]] used the same rule to derive the integral form of Taylor's remainder.

## Improper Integrals and the Gaussian Integral {lo=4}

Tail probabilities, perpetuities and moments integrate over unbounded intervals. Definition (5.1) needs a bounded interval and a bounded integrand, so we extend it with a limit.

> [!definition] Improper integral
> If $f$ is integrable on $[a,b]$ for every $b>a$, then $\int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx:=\lim_{b\to\infty}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$. The integral **converges** if the limit exists and is finite, and **diverges** otherwise; $\int_{-\infty}^b f$ is defined in the same way. If $f$ is unbounded near $a$, then $\int_a^b f:=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{a+\varepsilon}^b f$. A two-sided integral $\int_{-\infty}^\infty f$ converges if $\int_{-\infty}^0 f$ and $\int_0^\infty f$ both converge, and its value is their sum.

Power functions are the benchmark.

> [!proposition] Power test
> $\int_1^\infty x^{-\beta}\,dx$ converges if and only if $\beta>1$, and then equals $1/(\beta-1)$. $\int_0^1 x^{-\beta}\,dx$ converges if and only if $\beta<1$.

*Proof.* For $\beta\ne1$, $\int_1^b x^{-\beta}dx=(b^{1-\beta}-1)/(1-\beta)$, which tends to $1/(\beta-1)$ if $\beta>1$ and to $\infty$ if $\beta<1$. For $\beta=1$ the integral is $\ln b\to\infty$. The second statement follows in the same way from $\int_\varepsilon^1 x^{-\beta}dx$ as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. $\blacksquare$

So $1/x$ is the dividing line: $\int_1^\infty x^{-2}dx=1$, but $\int_1^\infty x^{-1}dx=\infty$, although both integrands go to zero. Exponentials decay faster than any power. For $r>0$, $\int_0^\infty e^{-rt}dt=\lim_{b\to\infty}(1-e^{-rb})/r=1/r$, the value of a perpetuity paying 1 a year (Section 6).

> [!proposition] Comparison test
> If $0\le f(x)\le h(x)$ for $x\ge a$ and $\int_a^\infty h$ converges, then $\int_a^\infty f$ converges. If $\int_a^\infty f$ diverges, so does $\int_a^\infty h$.

The reason: $I(b)=\int_a^b f$ never decreases as $b$ grows and never exceeds $\int_a^\infty h$. A non-decreasing bounded function has a finite limit, a property of the real numbers that we state without proof.

### The Gaussian integral

The bell curve $e^{-x^2/2}$ has no elementary antiderivative, yet its total area is known exactly. It is finite: $(\lvert x\rvert-1)^2\ge0$ gives $x^2/2\ge\lvert x\rvert-1/2$, so $e^{-x^2/2}\le e^{1/2}e^{-\lvert x\rvert}$, whose integral over each half-line converges.

> [!theorem] Gaussian integral
> $$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2/2}\,dx=\sqrt{2\pi}. \tag{5.7}$$

> [!deepdive]- Proof of the Gaussian integral with polar coordinates
> Here $x,y$ are Cartesian coordinates and $\rho,\theta$ polar ones, a point's distance from the origin and its angle. Let $J_b:=\int_{-b}^{b}e^{-x^2/2}dx$; we need $\lim_{b\to\infty}J_b$.
>
> **Step 1 (from a square to a double integral).** $J_b^2=\Big(\int_{-b}^{b}e^{-x^2/2}dx\Big)\Big(\int_{-b}^{b}e^{-y^2/2}dy\Big)=\iint_{[-b,b]^2}e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2}\,dx\,dy$. A double integral is the limit of Riemann sums over small rectangles. For a continuous function on a rectangle it equals either iterated integral (Fubini's theorem, stated without proof; Stewart, Clegg and Watson 2021). Here the integrand factorizes as $e^{-x^2/2}e^{-y^2/2}$, so the iterated integral is the product of the two one-dimensional integrals.
>
> **Step 2 (polar coordinates on a disc).** The region between radii $\rho$ and $\rho+d\rho$ and angles $\theta$ and $\theta+d\theta$ is nearly a rectangle with sides $d\rho$ and $\rho\,d\theta$. Its area is therefore $\rho\,d\rho\,d\theta$, with an error of order $d\rho^2\,d\theta$ that vanishes in the limit. The integrand depends on $\rho$ only, through $e^{-\rho^2/2}$. On the disc of radius $b$, using the substitution $u=-\rho^2/2$ of Section 4,
> $$\iint_{x^2+y^2\le b^2}e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2}\,dx\,dy=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^b e^{-\rho^2/2}\,\rho\,d\rho\,d\theta=2\pi\big[-e^{-\rho^2/2}\big]_0^b=2\pi\big(1-e^{-b^2/2}\big).$$
>
> **Step 3 (squeeze).** The disc of radius $b$ lies inside the square $[-b,b]^2$, which lies inside the disc of radius $b\sqrt2$. The integrand is positive, so
> $$2\pi\big(1-e^{-b^2/2}\big)\le J_b^2\le2\pi\big(1-e^{-b^2}\big).$$
> Both bounds tend to $2\pi$, so $J_b\to\sqrt{2\pi}$. The two half-line integrals converge by the comparison above, so the two-sided integral equals $\lim_{b\to\infty}J_b=\sqrt{2\pi}$. $\blacksquare$
>
> The factor $\rho$ in the polar area element is what makes the problem solvable: $\rho\,e^{-\rho^2/2}$ has an elementary antiderivative, while $e^{-x^2/2}$ does not.

Two consequences carry the rest of the module. First, $\varphi(z)=e^{-z^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ has total area 1, so $\Phi(\infty)=1$; by symmetry $\Phi(0)=1/2$ and $\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)$. Second, by (5.5) every normal density $\varphi\big((x-\mu)/\sigma\big)/\sigma$ has total area 1.

**Moments of the normal.** For every $k$ the function $\lvert z\rvert^k e^{-z^2/4}$ is bounded, because exponentials beat powers. So $\lvert z\rvert^k\varphi(z)$ is at most a constant times $e^{-z^2/4}$, and every moment $\int z^k\varphi(z)\,dz$ converges by comparison. Odd moments vanish by symmetry. For the second moment, (5.6) with $u=z$ and $h'=z\,\varphi(z)$, so that $h=-\varphi(z)$ by Section 4, gives

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^2\varphi(z)\,dz=\big[-z\,\varphi(z)\big]_{-\infty}^{\infty}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(z)\,dz=0+1=1.$$

So $\varphi$ has mean 0 and variance 1. By (5.5), the density $\varphi\big((x-\mu)/\sigma\big)/\sigma$ has mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, which is why fitting a normal means matching the sample mean and standard deviation.

### Tails that make moments infinite

The normal tail falls like $e^{-x^2/2}$, faster than any power, so every moment is finite. A density whose tail falls only like a power behaves differently. The **Pareto** density with **tail index** $\alpha>0$,

$$f(x)=\alpha\,x^{-\alpha-1}\quad\text{for }x\ge1\quad(\text{and }0\text{ below }1),$$

integrates to 1 by the power test. Its $k$-th moment is $\int_1^\infty x^k\,\alpha x^{-\alpha-1}dx=\alpha\int_1^\infty x^{k-\alpha-1}dx$, which by the power test converges if and only if $k<\alpha$, with value $\alpha/(\alpha-k)$. By comparison, any density whose tail behaves like a constant times $x^{-\alpha-1}$ obeys the same rule. The Student-t density with $\nu$ degrees of freedom, proportional to $(1+x^2/\nu)^{-(\nu+1)/2}$, has tails like $\lvert x\rvert^{-(\nu+1)}$, so its moments exist only below order $\nu$.

> [!exhibit] Tails and moments: which integrals converge {#tails}
> | Density, up to a constant | Tail falls like | Mean | Variance | Kurtosis |
> |:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
> | Normal, $e^{-x^2/2}$ | $e^{-x^2/2}$ | finite | finite | finite (3) |
> | Laplace, $e^{-\lvert x\rvert}$ | $e^{-\lvert x\rvert}$ | finite | finite | finite (6) |
> | Student-t, $\nu=5$ | $\lvert x\rvert^{-6}$ | finite | finite | finite (9) |
> | Student-t, $\nu=3$ | $\lvert x\rvert^{-4}$ | finite | finite | infinite |
> | Pareto, $\alpha=3$ | $x^{-4}$ | finite | finite | infinite |
> | Pareto, $\alpha=1.5$ | $x^{-2.5}$ | finite | infinite | does not exist (infinite variance) |
> | Cauchy (Student-t, $\nu=1$) | $\lvert x\rvert^{-2}$ | does not exist | does not exist | does not exist |
>
> Note: kurtosis is $\E[(X-\mu)^4]/\sigma^4$; the finite values are stated without proof (NIST/SEMATECH; for the Student-t, $3+6/(\nu-4)$ when $\nu>4$). A moment of order $k$ exists exactly when $\int\lvert x\rvert^k f(x)\,dx$ converges.

[[ex:tails]] lists which moments converge, and empirical work places market returns in its middle rows. Mandelbrot (1963) modelled cotton price changes with stable distributions, whose variance is infinite; Cont (2001) instead puts the tail index of most return series between 2 and 5. Gopikrishnan et al. (1999) estimate $\alpha\approx3$ for index returns, and Gabaix et al. (2003) propose a mechanism. With $\alpha\approx3$ the variance is finite, the fourth moment is infinite and the third sits on the borderline.

Without the single day of 7 April 2025 it is 7.05; without the four largest moves, two down and two up, it is 3.85. If the tail index is near 3, the population fourth moment is a divergent integral and no sample is long enough for its estimate to settle. Quote a kurtosis together with its sample period, and prefer tail measures that need fewer moments ([[LM15]], [[LM37]]).

## Continuous Discounting and Continuous Cash Flows {lo=5}

Section 3's accumulation formula has a mirror image: discounting a stream of cash flows. Discrete payments form geometric series, which [[LM6]] sums. Here payments arrive continuously, or so often that a continuous stream is the simpler model.

A stream pays at the rate $\mathrm{CF}(t)$ NT\$ per year. Over a short interval $[t,t+\Delta t]$ it pays about $\mathrm{CF}(t)\,\Delta t$, worth $\mathrm{CF}(t)\,\Delta t\,e^{-rt}$ today at a continuously compounded rate $r$. Summing over a partition of $[0,T]$ gives a Riemann sum, and in the limit

$$\mathrm{PV}=\int_0^T \mathrm{CF}(t)\,e^{-rt}\,dt,\qquad \mathrm{FV}=\int_0^T \mathrm{CF}(t)\,e^{r(T-t)}\,dt=e^{rT}\,\mathrm{PV}. \tag{5.8}$$

With a moving rate, replace $e^{-rt}$ by the discount factor $P(t)$ of (5.3).

> [!exhibit] Present values of continuous streams discounted at the continuously compounded rate r {#pvtable}
> | Stream $\mathrm{CF}(t)$ on $[0,T]$ | Present value | Condition |
> |:--|:--|:--|
> | lump sum of 1 paid at $T$ | $e^{-rT}$ | — |
> | level, $\mathrm{CF}_0$ per year | $\mathrm{CF}_0\,(1-e^{-rT})/r$ | $r\ne0$ |
> | level forever | $\mathrm{CF}_0/r$ | $r>0$ |
> | growing, $\mathrm{CF}_0\,e^{gt}$ | $\mathrm{CF}_0\,\big(1-e^{-(r-g)T}\big)/(r-g)$ | $r\ne g$; $\mathrm{CF}_0T$ if $r=g$ |
> | growing forever | $\mathrm{CF}_0/(r-g)$ | $r>g$; diverges if $g\ge r$ |
> | any $\mathrm{CF}(t)$, moving rate $r(t)$ | $\int_0^T \mathrm{CF}(t)\,P(t)\,dt$, $P$ from (5.3) | — |
>
> Note: each row follows from (5.8) and the antiderivative of $e^{-(r-g)t}$ in [[ex:antider]]; the perpetuities are improper integrals (Section 5). The growing perpetuity's condition $r>g$ is the continuous counterpart of a condition in the Gordon growth model (M. J. Gordon) of [[LM6]]: dividends must grow more slowly than the required return.

> [!example] Monthly rent versus a continuous stream {#rent}
> A property pays NT\$30,000 a month for 20 years. Discount at 2% a year, continuously compounded (an illustrative rate). Value the rent (a) paid at the end of each month, (b) paid at the start of each month, (c) paid in mid-month, and (d) modelled as a continuous stream of NT\$360,000 a year.
>
> > [!solution]
> > (d) By [[ex:pvtable]], $\mathrm{PV}=360{,}000\times(1-e^{-0.4})/0.02=360{,}000\times16.483998=$ NT\$5,934,239.
> >
> > (a)–(c) are sums of 240 terms $30{,}000\,e^{-0.02\,t_j}$ over the payment dates $t_j$, geometric series that [[LM6]] sums in closed form. Payment in arrears is worth NT\$5,929,295, in advance NT\$5,939,186 and in mid-month NT\$5,934,238.
> >
> > Each discrete stream is a Riemann sum of the integral (5.8) with $\Delta t=1/12$. Payment in arrears is the right-endpoint rule, in advance the left-endpoint rule and mid-month the midpoint rule. The one-sided sums miss the integral by −NT\$4,944 and +NT\$4,947 (sum minus integral), about $\pm0.083\%\approx\pm r\,\Delta t/2$, the leading $O(\Delta t)$ error. The mid-month stream falls short by less than NT\$1 (NT\$0.69), an $O(\Delta t^2)$ error.
> >
> > A continuous stream approximates frequent payments well only if you know where the payments sit. Rent paid in advance is worth 0.167% more than rent paid in arrears.

### Duration of a stream

The present-value-weighted average date of a stream is its Macaulay **duration**,

$$D_{\mathrm{Mac}}:=\frac{\int_0^T t\,\mathrm{CF}(t)\,e^{-rt}\,dt}{\int_0^T \mathrm{CF}(t)\,e^{-rt}\,dt}.$$

For a level stream the numerator is $\mathrm{CF}_0$ times Section 4's time-weighted integral, so

$$D_{\mathrm{Mac}}=\frac{1-(1+rT)\,e^{-rT}}{r\,(1-e^{-rT})}=\frac1r-\frac{T}{e^{rT}-1}.$$

As $T\to\infty$, $D_{\mathrm{Mac}}\to1/r$: a perpetuity discounted at 2.5% has a duration of 40 years. Differentiating $\mathrm{PV}(r)$ under the integral sign gives $\mathrm{PV}'(r)=-\int_0^T t\,\mathrm{CF}(t)\,e^{-rt}dt$, so $-\mathrm{PV}'(r)/\mathrm{PV}(r)=D_{\mathrm{Mac}}$. This step is permitted because the integrand and its derivative in $r$ are continuous on $[0,T]$ (stated without proof). Under continuous compounding, modified and Macaulay duration coincide; with annual compounding they differ by the factor $1+y$ of [[LM2]].

> [!pitfall] Mixing compounding conventions
> The formulas of [[ex:pvtable]] need a continuously compounded rate. Convert an effective annual rate first ([[LM1]]): an effective 2% is a continuously compounded $r=\ln1.02=1.9803\%$. Plugging 2% straight in values [[eg:rent]]'s stream at NT\$5,934,239 instead of NT\$5,945,187. ## Densities and Expectations {lo=6}

A risk model assigns probabilities to ranges of returns. When a return can take any value in a range, each single value has probability zero. Probability is then spread along the axis with a density, as interest was spread along the time axis in Section 2: probabilities become areas, and averages become integrals. This section defines only what the case needs; [[LM13]] and [[LM14]] build probability theory properly.

> [!definition] Density, distribution function, expectation
> A **probability density** is a function $f\ge0$ with $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx=1$. A quantity $X$ has density $f$ if $\Pr(a<X\le b)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ for all $a<b$. Its **distribution function** is $F(x):=\Pr(X\le x)=\int_{-\infty}^x f(t)\,dt$, and a **median** of $X$ is a value $x$ with $F(x)=1/2$. For a function $h$ with $\int\lvert h(x)\rvert\,f(x)\,dx<\infty$, the **expectation** of $h(X)$ is
> $$\E[h(X)]:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(x)\,f(x)\,dx. \tag{5.9}$$
> In particular the mean is $\mu=\E[X]$ and the variance $\sigma^2=\E[(X-\mu)^2]$.

By the fundamental theorem, $F'=f$ wherever $f$ is continuous: the density is the rate at which probability accumulates. Definition (5.9) is the limit of the probability-weighted averages $\sum_i h(x_i)\Pr(x_{i-1}<X\le x_i)$, which are Riemann sums. Linearity of the integral makes expectation linear, $\E[\eta X+\zeta]=\eta\,\E[X]+\zeta$. When the integral in (5.9) diverges, the expectation does not exist, as for the heavy tails of Section 5.

The case's normal model is $R\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, with density $\varphi\big((x-\mu)/\sigma\big)/\sigma$, mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$ (Section 5). Fitting it to 0050 sets $\mu=\hat\mu=0.0871\%$ and $\sigma=s=1.2385\%$, the sample mean and population standard deviation; [[LM20]] shows that this is the maximum-likelihood choice.

### Bracketing a normal tail by parts

$\Pr(R\le-5\%)=\Phi(-4.1074386)$ has no formula, but integration by parts brackets it. For $\omega>0$ let $Q(\omega):=1-\Phi(\omega)=\int_\omega^\infty\varphi(t)\,dt$, which equals $\Phi(-\omega)$ by symmetry. Write $\varphi(t)=\frac1t\cdot t\,\varphi(t)$ and apply (5.6) with $u=1/t$ and $h=-\varphi(t)$, using $(-\varphi)'=t\,\varphi$:

$$Q(\omega)=\Big[-\frac{\varphi(t)}{t}\Big]_\omega^\infty-\int_\omega^\infty\frac{\varphi(t)}{t^2}\,dt=\frac{\varphi(\omega)}{\omega}-\int_\omega^\infty\frac{\varphi(t)}{t^2}\,dt.$$

The remaining integral is positive, so $Q(\omega)<\varphi(\omega)/\omega$. The same step applied to that integral, with $u=1/t^3$, gives $\int_\omega^\infty\varphi(t)\,t^{-2}dt=\varphi(\omega)/\omega^3-3\int_\omega^\infty\varphi(t)\,t^{-4}dt<\varphi(\omega)/\omega^3$. Together,

$$\frac{\varphi(\omega)}{\omega}\Big(1-\frac1{\omega^2}\Big)<1-\Phi(\omega)<\frac{\varphi(\omega)}{\omega}\qquad(\omega>0). \tag{5.10}$$

The two bounds differ by the relative amount $1/\omega^2$, so for large $\omega$ the tail is $\approx\varphi(\omega)/\omega$, with a relative error below $1/\omega^2$. Gordon (1941) sharpened the lower bound to $\varphi(\omega)/(\omega+1/\omega)$. The ratio $Q(\omega)/\varphi(\omega)$ is called **Mills' ratio**. The normal tail shrinks like $e^{-\omega^2/2}/\omega$, so each extra standard deviation divides it by a factor that itself keeps growing.

> [!pitfall] “Sigma” is a statement about a model
> “A 4-sigma day” means a probability of $\Phi(-4)=3.2\times10^{-5}$ only under a normal model: about once in 130 years of 243.1 trading days. In August 2007 Goldman Sachs's finance chief said: “We were seeing things that were 25-standard deviation moves, several days in a row” (*Financial Times*, 13 August 2007; quoted by Dowd et al. 2008). Under normality a 25-sigma day has a probability of about $3\times10^{-138}$. When an “impossible” day recurs, the lesson is that the model's tail is wrong, not that the market was unlucky.

Part of that shape comes from volatility that changes over time ([[LM26]]): two of the nine days fell in early 2020, and three in under four months of 2024.

## Numerical Integration {lo=7}

Liouville's theorem rules out a formula for $\Phi$, and integrands defined by data or by other models rarely have one either. A numerical rule replaces the integral by a weighted sum of function values. Its quality lies in its **error order**: how fast the error falls as the grid is refined.

### Three rules

On $N$ panels of width $\Delta x=(b-a)/N$, with $f_i=f(a+i\,\Delta x)$:

- **Midpoint:** $M_N=\Delta x\sum_{i=1}^N f(m_i)$, the height at each panel's midpoint $m_i=a+(i-\tfrac12)\Delta x$.
- **Trapezoid:** $T_N=\Delta x\big(\tfrac12f_0+f_1+\cdots+f_{N-1}+\tfrac12f_N\big)=\tfrac12(L_N+R_N)$, the area under each panel's chord.
- **Simpson** ($N$ even): $S_N=\tfrac{\Delta x}3\big(f_0+4f_1+2f_2+4f_3+\cdots+2f_{N-2}+4f_{N-1}+f_N\big)$, a parabola through each pair of panels.

### Error orders from Taylor expansions

Take one panel with midpoint $m$, from $m-\Delta x/2$ to $m+\Delta x/2$, and expand $f$ about $m$ ([[LM3]]): $f(x)=f(m)+f'(m)(x-m)+\tfrac12f''(m)(x-m)^2+\tfrac16f'''(m)(x-m)^3+O\big((x-m)^4\big)$. Integrating over the panel, the odd powers of $x-m$ cancel:

$$\int_{m-\Delta x/2}^{m+\Delta x/2}f=\Delta x\,f(m)+\frac{\Delta x^3}{24}f''(m)+O(\Delta x^5).$$

The midpoint rule takes $\Delta x\,f(m)$, so its panel error, exact minus rule, is $+\frac{\Delta x^3}{24}f''(m)+O(\Delta x^5)$. The trapezoid rule takes $\tfrac{\Delta x}2\big[f(m-\tfrac{\Delta x}2)+f(m+\tfrac{\Delta x}2)\big]=\Delta x\,f(m)+\frac{\Delta x^3}{8}f''(m)+O(\Delta x^5)$, so its panel error is $-\frac{\Delta x^3}{12}f''(m)+O(\Delta x^5)$. Summed over the $N$ panels, $\sum_i \Delta x\,f''(m_i)$ is a midpoint sum for $\int_a^b f''=f'(b)-f'(a)$, so for $f$ with continuous derivatives up to order four on $[a,b]$

$$\int_a^b f-T_N\approx-\frac{\Delta x^2}{12}\big[f'(b)-f'(a)\big],\qquad \int_a^b f-M_N\approx+\frac{\Delta x^2}{24}\big[f'(b)-f'(a)\big], \tag{5.13}$$

with neglected terms of order $\Delta x^4$. Both rules are $O(\Delta x^2)$: halving $\Delta x$ divides the error by 4. For $f$ with a continuous second derivative on $[a,b]$, the exact statement $\int_a^b f-T_N=-(b-a)\,\Delta x^2f''(\xi)/12$ for some $\xi\in(a,b)$ is stated without proof (Burden, Faires and Burden 2016, chapter 4).

The two errors in (5.13) have opposite signs and a 2 : 1 ratio, so $(2M_N+T_N)/3$ cancels the $\Delta x^2$ term. That combination is Simpson's rule on the $2N$ half-panels: since $T_{2N}=(T_N+M_N)/2$, $S_{2N}=(2M_N+T_N)/3=(4T_{2N}-T_N)/3$. Removing a known leading error term in this way is called **Richardson extrapolation**. What remains is fourth order: for $f$ with a continuous fourth derivative on $[a,b]$,

$$\int_a^b f-S_N=-\frac{(b-a)\,\Delta x^4}{180}\,f^{(4)}(\xi)\qquad\text{for some }\xi\in(a,b), \tag{5.14}$$

stated without proof (Burden, Faires and Burden 2016); the Deep Dive derives its leading term. Simpson's rule is therefore $O(\Delta x^4)$: halving $\Delta x$ divides the error by 16, and the rule is exact for cubic polynomials. Orders bound the worst case. Over the whole real line the trapezoid rule for $e^{-x^2/2}$ overshoots by only $1.3\times10^{-8}$ at $\Delta x=1$, because all its endpoint derivatives vanish at $\pm\infty$.

> [!deepdive]- The leading error term of Simpson's rule
> Take a double panel $[m-\Delta x,m+\Delta x]$ and expand $f$ about its midpoint $m$ to fourth order. Integrating over the double panel kills the odd powers of $x-m$:
> $$\int_{m-\Delta x}^{m+\Delta x}f=2\Delta x\,f(m)+\frac{\Delta x^3}{3}f''(m)+\frac{\Delta x^5}{60}f^{(4)}(m)+O(\Delta x^7).$$
> Simpson's rule on this double panel is $\tfrac{\Delta x}3\big[f(m-\Delta x)+4f(m)+f(m+\Delta x)\big]$. Since $f(m-\Delta x)+f(m+\Delta x)=2f(m)+\Delta x^2f''(m)+\tfrac{\Delta x^4}{12}f^{(4)}(m)+O(\Delta x^6)$, it equals
> $$2\Delta x\,f(m)+\frac{\Delta x^3}{3}f''(m)+\frac{\Delta x^5}{36}f^{(4)}(m)+O(\Delta x^7).$$
> The $\Delta x$ and $\Delta x^3$ terms agree, which is why the rule is exact for cubics. The difference, exact minus rule, is $\Delta x^5f^{(4)}(m)\,(1/60-1/36)=-\Delta x^5f^{(4)}(m)/90$. Summing over the $(b-a)/(2\Delta x)$ double panels, with midpoints $m_j$, gives $-\frac{\Delta x^4}{180}\sum_j 2\Delta x\,f^{(4)}(m_j)\approx-\frac{(b-a)\,\Delta x^4}{180}f^{(4)}(\xi)$, the leading term of (5.14). $\blacksquare$

### Adaptive quadrature

Production code adapts the grid to the integrand. `scipy.integrate.quad` uses the QUADPACK algorithms (Piessens et al. 1983). On a finite interval it applies a 21-point Gauss–Kronrod rule within subintervals and subdivides adaptively; an infinite range is first mapped onto a finite interval (SciPy documentation). With the case's unrounded $z$, `quad(phi, -inf, z)` returns $2.000355\times10^{-5}$ with an error estimate of $2.2\times10^{-10}$, within a relative $7\times10^{-11}$ of the value obtained from the complementary error function.

> [!pitfall] Error orders are asymptotic
> At $N=16$ the observed ratios in [[ex:numinttable]] are 3.46 and 7.88, not 4 and 16. The orders describe the limit $\Delta x\to0$, and they appear only once the grid resolves the integrand. Always check by halving $\Delta x$: the ratio of successive errors estimates the order and the size of the remaining error, and without an exact value successive differences do the same. A tail integral on a coarse grid can be wrong by a third without any warning.

> [!zh] 數值積分：誤差階就是收斂速度
> 常態密度函數沒有初等的反導函數，所以 $\Phi$ 沒有公式，尾端機率一定要用數值方法計算。梯形法用弦取代曲線，中點法取每格中點的高度，兩者誤差都是 $O(\Delta x^2)$，而且符號相反、大小比 2:1，所以 $(2M_N+T_N)/3$ 會把 $\Delta x^2$ 項消掉，得到 $O(\Delta x^4)$ 的辛普森法，這就是理查森外插。檢查方法很簡單：格寬每減半一次，梯形法誤差應縮小 4 倍、辛普森法 16 倍；比例不對，代表格子還太粗、尚未進入漸近區。實務上可以直接用 `scipy.integrate.quad` 的自適應積分。

> [!kc]
> Simpson's rule with $N=8$ has an error of $3.2\times10^{-6}$ on a smooth integrand, and the grid is already in the asymptotic regime. Estimate the error with $N=32$, and the number of panels needed for an error of $10^{-10}$.
>
> > [!answer]-
> > Two halvings divide the error by $16^2=256$: about $1.25\times10^{-8}$ at $N=32$. Reaching $10^{-10}$ needs a further factor of 125, so $(N/32)^4=125$ and $N=32\times125^{1/4}=107.0$; take $N=108$, since Simpson's rule needs an even number of panels.

## Integration in Python {lo=7}

Three habits make numerical integrals trustworthy. Compute a known case alongside the unknown one, halve the step and watch the ratio, and keep data and model in separate, testable functions. `quad` also integrates (5.8) directly when $\mathrm{CF}(t)$ or $r(t)$ has no closed form; the lab values [[eg:rent]]'s stream that way.

```python
import math
import numpy as np
from scipy import integrate, special

def trapezoid(f, a, b, N):    # N panels; error O(dx^2), eq. (5.13)
    x = np.linspace(a, b, N + 1); y = f(x); dx = (b - a) / N
    return dx * (y.sum() - 0.5 * (y[0] + y[-1]))

def simpson(f, a, b, N):      # N even; error O(dx^4), eq. (5.14)
    x = np.linspace(a, b, N + 1); y = f(x); dx = (b - a) / N
    return dx / 3 * (y[0] + y[-1] + 4 * y[1:-1:2].sum() + 2 * y[2:-1:2].sum())

phi = lambda t: np.exp(-0.5 * t * t) / math.sqrt(2 * math.pi)
z = -4.1074386                                  # (kappa - mu_hat) / s at -5%
exact = 0.5 * special.erfc(-z / math.sqrt(2))   # Phi(z) = 2.000355e-05
for N in (64, 128, 256):
    print(N, f"{trapezoid(phi, -12, z, N) - exact:.2e}",
          f"{simpson(phi, -12, z, N) - exact:.2e}")
# 64 4.49e-07 6.23e-09 / 128 1.13e-07 3.94e-10 / 256 2.82e-08 2.47e-11
print("%.6e %.1e" % integrate.quad(phi, -np.inf, z))  # 2.000355e-05 2.2e-10
```

```python
import polars as pl
from scipy import stats
px = pl.read_csv("data/0050_daily_raw.csv")
dv = pl.read_csv("data/0050_dividends.csv").select("date", D="cash_dividend")
days = pl.read_csv("data/00631L_daily_raw.csv").select("date")
c, d = pl.col("close"), pl.col("D").fill_null(0.0)
split = pl.when(pl.col("date") == pl.lit("2025-06-18")).then(4.0).otherwise(1.0)
ret = (px.join(days, on="date", how="semi").join(dv, on="date", how="left")
         .sort("date").with_columns(R=(c + d) / (c.shift(1) / split) - 1)
         .drop_nulls("R"))
mu, s, n = ret["R"].mean(), ret["R"].std(ddof=0), ret.height  # n = 2,902
for kappa in (-0.03, -0.04, -0.05):
    model = n * stats.norm.cdf((kappa - mu) / s)
    # Two-decimal prices make limit moves and round returns exact ties.
    hits = ret.filter(pl.col("R") <= kappa + 1e-12).height  # float guard
    print(f"{kappa:+.0%} normal {model:.3f} actual {hits}")
# -3% normal 18.400 actual 37 / -4% normal 1.403 actual 18
# -5% normal 0.058 actual 9
```

## Summary {-}

- A definite integral (5.1) is the limit of Riemann sums: accumulated rate times width, or signed area. For monotone integrands the left and right sums err by at most $\Delta t\lvert f(b)-f(a)\rvert$; the midpoint sum's error is $O(\Delta t^2)$.
- The fundamental theorem of calculus (5.2) evaluates integrals with antiderivatives and gives $\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f=f(x)$. Money at a moving rate grows by $\exp\big(\int_0^T r\big)$. Because $\Phi'=\varphi$, 0050's −5% tail probability moves about 18% per 1% change in $\sigma$.
- Substitution (5.4) reduces every normal probability to $\Phi$ through $z=(\kappa-\mu)/\sigma$; integration by parts (5.6) handles $t\,e^{-rt}$, recovers a yield curve from its forward curve, and brackets the normal tail (5.10).
- Improper integrals are limits. $\int_1^\infty x^{-\beta}dx$ converges only for $\beta>1$; $\int e^{-x^2/2}dx=\sqrt{2\pi}$ (5.7). A power tail of index $\alpha$ keeps only the moments below order $\alpha$. With $\alpha\approx3$ kurtosis is infinite, and its sample estimate never settles.
- A continuous stream is worth $\int_0^T \mathrm{CF}(t)e^{-rt}dt$ (5.8). Payments in arrears, in advance and mid-period are right, left and midpoint Riemann sums of that integral. Under continuous compounding, duration equals $-\mathrm{PV}'/\mathrm{PV}$.
- Probabilities and expectations are integrals of a density (5.9). For 0050 the fitted normal gives $\Pr(R\le-5\%)=2.0\times10^{-5}$, 0.058 expected days against 9 actual, and a tail mean of −5.27% against −6.54%. The lognormal mean is exactly $e^{\mu_X+\sigma_X^2/2}$ (5.12).
- The trapezoid and midpoint rules are $O(\Delta x^2)$, Simpson's rule $O(\Delta x^4)$ (5.13–5.14). Halving the step and checking the error ratio is the practical test; adaptive quadrature in `scipy.integrate.quad` handles infinite ranges.

## Solutions {-}

1. **B** is correct. $(1-e^{-0.3})/0.03=8.6394$. A, 8.5104, pays 1 at each year-end and discounts at the same continuous rate. It is the right-endpoint Riemann sum of the stream, low by about $r\,\Delta t/2$ with $\Delta t=1$. C ignores discounting.

2. With $\Delta x=0.25$: $L_4=1.512437$ (error, sum minus exact, $-0.205845$), $R_4=1.942007$ (error $+0.223725$), exact $e-1=1.718282$. Both errors are below $\Delta x\,(e-1)=0.429570$ in size. The average $T_4=1.727222$ errs by $+0.008940$, close to the overshoot that (5.13) predicts: $\int_0^1e^x\,dx-T_4\approx-\frac{\Delta x^2}{12}\big[f'(1)-f'(0)\big]=-\frac{0.0625}{12}\times1.718282=-0.008949$. Averaging the one-sided sums turns an $O(\Delta x)$ error into an $O(\Delta x^2)$ one.

3. **A** is correct. By part 1 of the theorem and the chain rule, the derivative is $e^{-(x^2)^2}\cdot2x=2x\,e^{-x^4}$. B forgets the chain rule; C substitutes the wrong argument.

4. With $u=-x^2/2$: $\int_0^1 x\,e^{-x^2/2}dx=\big[-e^{-x^2/2}\big]_0^1=1-e^{-1/2}=0.3935$. As the upper limit grows, $e^{-b^2/2}\to0$, so $\int_0^\infty x\,e^{-x^2/2}dx=1$.

5. Letting $T\to\infty$ in Section 4's result gives $\int_0^\infty t\,e^{-rt}dt=1/r^2$, and the perpetuity's value is $\int_0^\infty e^{-rt}dt=1/r$. Hence $D_{\mathrm{Mac}}=(1/r^2)/(1/r)=1/r$: 40 years at 2.5%.

6. **C** is correct. By the power test, $\int_1^\infty x^{-3/2}dx=1/(3/2-1)=2$. A has $\beta=1/2\le1$ at infinity; B has $\beta=3/2\ge1$ near zero. Both diverge.

7. With $\alpha=2.5$: $\E[X]=2.5/1.5=5/3=1.6667$, $\E[X^2]=2.5/0.5=5$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X)=5-25/9=2.2222$. Moments of order $k\ge2.5$ are infinite, so the third moment, skewness (its standardized form, [[LM19]]) and kurtosis do not exist.

8. $z=(-0.06-0.0005)/0.015=-4.03333$ and $\omega=4.03333$. Then $\varphi(\omega)=1.1706\times10^{-4}$, the upper bound is $\varphi(\omega)/\omega=2.9023\times10^{-5}$ and the lower bound $2.7239\times10^{-5}$. The exact value, $2.7496\times10^{-5}$, lies inside: the upper bound is 5.6% high and the lower bound 0.9% low. Per year the model expects $243.1\times2.7496\times10^{-5}=0.00668$ days, one every 150 years.

9. **A** is correct: $\Phi(-4)=3.17\times10^{-5}=0.0032\%$. B is the two-sided probability of a move of four standard deviations in either direction. C is the one-sided Chebyshev (Cantelli) bound $1/(1+4^2)$ of [[LM14]], which holds for every distribution with a finite variance and is far larger.

10. $\mathrm{PV}=(1-e^{-0.02\times30})/0.02=22.5594$; forever, $1/(0.05-0.03)=50.0$. The first 30 years deliver $1-e^{-0.6}=45.1\%$ of the perpetuity's value; [[LM6]] finds almost the same share, 45%, for a discrete perpetuity at a 2% discount rate.

11. The integrand values are $1$, $0.9692332$, $0.8824969$, $0.7548396$ and $0.6065307$ at $x=0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1$. $T_2=0.8428811$ (error, rule minus exact, $-0.0127433$), $T_4=0.8524588$ (error $-0.0031656$, a ratio of 4.03) and $S_4=0.8556513$ (error $+0.0000269$). Simpson's weights $\tfrac{\Delta x}3(1,4,2,4,1)$ with $\Delta x=0.25$ equal $\tfrac43$ times the $T_4$ weights $\Delta x(\tfrac12,1,1,1,\tfrac12)$ minus $\tfrac13$ times the $T_2$ weights $0.5\,(\tfrac12,0,1,0,\tfrac12)$, so $S_4=(4T_4-T_2)/3$ identically.

12. **C** is correct. By (5.14) the error is proportional to $\Delta x^4$, and $2^4=16$. A is the trapezoid and midpoint factor.

13. Mean $e^{0.08+0.02}=e^{0.10}=1.1052$; median $e^{0.08}=1.0833$; $\Pr(G<1)=\Pr(\ln G<0)=\Phi(-0.08/0.20)=\Phi(-0.4)=0.3446$.

14. With $\mu=0$, $\sigma=1$ and $z=-2$, (5.11) gives $\E[Z\mid Z\le-2]=-\varphi(2)/\Phi(-2)=-0.05399/0.02275=-2.3732$. The large-$\omega$ approximation $\varphi(\omega)/Q(\omega)\approx\omega+1/\omega=2.5$ overstates the depth by 5%: at $\omega=2$ the bracket (5.10) is still wide, since $1/\omega^2=0.25$.

15. Six days: 2020-03-20 (+7.95%), 2022-11-11 (+5.31%), 2024-08-06 (+5.51%), 2025-04-10 (+9.99%), 2026-04-08 (+5.18%) and 2026-07-31 (+10.00%). The normal model expects $2{,}902\times\Phi(-3.9668)=0.106$ days, so the data hold 57 times as many. The normal model fails in the upper tail too.

16. The model needs $\Phi\big((-0.05-\hat\mu)/\sigma\big)=9/2{,}902$, so $(-0.05-\hat\mu)/\sigma=-2.7369$ and $\sigma=0.050871/2.7369=1.8587\%$. It then expects 379 days at or below −2% against 104 actual, and 140 at or below −3% against 37.

## Glossary {-}

| Term | 中文 | Meaning |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Riemann sum | 黎曼和 | $\sum_i f(\xi_i)\,\Delta t_i$ over a partition; its limit is the integral, (5.1). |
| Definite integral | 定積分 | Limit of Riemann sums; accumulated change or signed area. |
| Antiderivative | 反導函數 | A function $F$ with $F'=f$. |
| Fundamental theorem of calculus | 微積分基本定理 | $\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f=f(x)$ and $\int_a^b f=F(b)-F(a)$, (5.2). |
| Substitution | 變數變換 | The chain rule read backwards, (5.4). |
| Integration by parts | 分部積分 | The product rule read backwards, (5.6). |
| Improper integral | 瑕積分（廣義積分） | A limit of integrals, for an unbounded interval or integrand. |
| Gaussian integral | 高斯積分 | $\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-x^2/2}dx=\sqrt{2\pi}$, (5.7). |
| Tail index | 尾部指數 | $\alpha$ in a power tail $x^{-\alpha-1}$; moments exist below order $\alpha$. |
| Kurtosis | 峰態 | $\E[(X-\mu)^4]/\sigma^4$: 3 for the normal, infinite if $2<\alpha\le4$ (undefined if $\alpha\le2$); excess kurtosis (超額峰態) subtracts 3. |
| Continuous annuity | 連續年金 | Level stream; present value $\mathrm{CF}_0(1-e^{-rT})/r$. |
| Duration | 存續期間 | Present-value-weighted average date; $-\mathrm{PV}'/\mathrm{PV}$ under continuous compounding. |
| Probability density | 機率密度函數 | $f\ge0$ with total area 1; probabilities are areas under it. |
| Distribution function | 累積分配函數 | $F(x)=\Pr(X\le x)=\int_{-\infty}^x f$; $F'=f$. |
| Expectation | 期望值 | $\E[h(X)]=\int h(x)\,f(x)\,dx$, (5.9). |
| Mills' ratio | 米爾斯比率 | $(1-\Phi(\omega))/\varphi(\omega)$; bounds in (5.10). |
| Conditional tail mean | 條件尾端平均 | $\E[R\mid R\le\kappa]$, (5.11); with $\kappa$ at the value-at-risk level, minus the expected shortfall (預期短缺) of [[LM37]]. |
| Lognormal | 對數常態 | $G=e^X$, $X$ normal; mean $e^{\mu_X+\sigma_X^2/2}$, median $e^{\mu_X}$, (5.12). |
| Trapezoidal rule | 梯形法 | Chords on each panel; error $O(\Delta x^2)$, (5.13). |
| Simpson's rule | 辛普森法 | Parabolas through panel pairs; error $O(\Delta x^4)$, (5.14). |
| Richardson extrapolation | 理查森外插 | Combining two step sizes to cancel a known leading error term. |
| Adaptive quadrature | 自適應數值積分 | Refining the grid where the estimated error is largest. |
