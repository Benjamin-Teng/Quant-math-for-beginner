## Findings in full

### 1 (major, independent reviewer)

- **Where:** Section 6 'The Inverse', l.314: 'To solve one system, `np.linalg.solve` runs elimination on b alone … Its M^{-1}b is also usually less accurate (Higham 2002a, ch. 14).' The KC answer at l.333 repeats it: 'does more work and usually loses accuracy … The rounding errors of the computed inverse also pass into M^{-1}b'.
- **Problem:** The cited chapter does not support 'usually less accurate'. Higham's own analysis finds the forward error of x = inv(A)·b comparable to that of LU with partial pivoting. What the inverse route gives up is cost and backward stability: its residual can be larger. The editor's verified wording in the anchors is 'costs more and has weaker error bounds'. 'Runs elimination on b alone' also misstates the cost, because solve eliminates M as well (about ⅔n³ operations); inv simply applies that elimination to n right-hand sides.
- **Correction:** l.314: "To solve one system, `np.linalg.solve` factors M once and applies the elimination to b alone. `np.linalg.inv` applies it to all n columns of I_n, so it costs more. Its M^{-1}b also has weaker error bounds: it is not backward stable, so its residual Mx−b can be larger (Higham 2002a, ch. 14)." KC l.333: "Computing inv(M) @ b does more work and gives up backward stability. `inv` applies elimination to the n columns of I_n and then multiplies, while `solve` applies it to b alone. The result can have a larger residual, although x itself is usually about as accurate (Higham 2002a, ch. 14)."
- **Evidence:** Druinsky and Toledo (2012), 'How accurate is inv(A)*b?', arXiv:1201.6035, §1, paraphrased: Higham 2002 (p. 260 and the exercise solution on p. 559) shows inv(A)*b about as accurate as A\b, yet usually not backward stable. plans/vol-2-anchors.md, LM9 facts: 'costs more and has weaker error bounds'. P10 on NumPy 2.5.3: max|solve − inv@b| = 4.4e-16.

### A1 (minor, number auditor)

- **Where:** Overview bullet 3 (line 21, "24 against 18 at n=3"); Cramer's Rule, line 213 ("At n=3 that is 24 terms against about 18 operations"); Exhibit 6 (cost) note, line 218
- **Problem:** The comparison puts Cramer's (n+1)! terms next to elimination's leading term (2/3)n^3 "operations", which are different units. At n=3 the module's own exact count from Section 5, sum_{k=1}^{n-1} 2(n-k)^2 = 2(4)+2(1) = 10, is well below the printed "about 18" (80% under it). The 18 is only the leading term, and at n=3 the n^2 correction is half of it. Counted in the same units, Cramer at n=3 needs 24 terms x 2 multiplications = 48, plus 4x5 = 20 additions and 3 divisions: 71 operations. A full elimination solve needs 10 + 3 multiplier divisions + 6 right-hand-side operations + 9 back-substitution operations = 28. So the printed pair "24 against 18" makes Cramer look 1.3 times as costly when it is about 2.5 times. The KC for n=4 does convert products to multiplications (120 -> 360), so the module counts in two different ways. The n=20 claim stands either way: 21! = 5.1e19 terms (about 9.7e20 multiplications) against 5,333 (exact count 4,940).
- **Correction:** Compare like with like at n=3, e.g. "At n=3 that is 24 terms (48 multiplications) against 10 operations by Section 5's count (28 with the right-hand side and back substitution), and at n=20 about 5.1x10^19 terms against about 5,333". Alternatively drop the n=3 pair from the overview and the text. Computation: sum 2(n-k)^2 = (2n^3-3n^2+n)/3 gives 10 at n=3 and 4,940 at n=20; checked for n=1..39 in exact arithmetic.
- **Evidence:** Own exact script (scratchpad stage3/lm09-auditor/exact.py): the exact elimination count is 10 at n=3, 28 at n=4 and 4,940 at n=20; (n+1)! = 24 and 51,090,942,171,709,440,000; (2/3)n^3 = 18 and 5,333.33.
